## Simon's view on SEO and blogging as a SaaS acquisition channel in 2024

- Simon posted that "SEO content blogging is very unlikely to be a needle moving channel in 2024". He says most commenters on LinkedIn, Threads and YouTube disagreed, though people mostly agreed with his other six points.
- He later clarified that this is a "bold statement" and a "bit of a stretch". He does not think blogging hurts, and he still does it for all his products.
- He employs an SEO specialist who works on blog content all day.
- His core claim: if you launch a new SaaS in 2024 and make Google ranking your main acquisition channel, then "unless you're in the outlier group you're probably not going to do anything needle moving".
- Blogging works as a supporting channel. It is not what he would build a business on.

## Evidence from FeedHive: rankings that bring little traffic

- FeedHive has several articles on page one of Google. For example, the "TikTok bios" article ranks in the top five.
- When Simon counts clicks to that article and tracks conversions, the numbers are "very very little". Some come through, but not much.
- He says: "if we were only doing this we wouldn't be in business".
- He has published hundreds of blog posts across FeedHive and LinkDrip, and is now doing the same for Aidbase. He calls this "standard blogging".
- He admits they have not tried building larger topic structures such as hubs, and says that could be worth testing.

## Rankings are not permanent

- A common pro-SEO argument is "once you rank, you're set". Simon's experience is different: "there isn't really a once you rank".
- On his Saas products, once an article reaches page one it is very hard to stay there. Someone else comes in and outranks you.
- Ranking is something you keep working on.
- Simon notes that Google keeps rolling out core updates. A March 2024 update was meant to remove "unhelpful content", with a stated goal of cutting low-quality, unoriginal results by 40%.
- Seen from that angle, SEO is a year-long investment that Google can undermine overnight with an algorithm change.

## Why AI content could weaken search itself

Simon describes a hypothetical, not a prediction:

- With a model like GPT-5, one person could generate 10,000 articles pushing a false claim (his example: that vaccines cause cancer), complete with fake citations and AI-generated video, and publish them to rank on Google.
- If that happens at scale, the open web becomes a "wasteland" where nothing can be trusted, and people stop searching it.
- Trusted information could end up in a few protected databases owned by big players, and a new blog could not simply publish its way in.
- When Kyle answered that the practical response is to build authority now, Simon called it a good comeback. He agreed that becoming a trusted source early matters if that future arrives. He stressed it is conjecture.

## Pricing experiment on Aidbase

- Aidbase's cheapest paid plan was $99/month, with a top plan at $399/month.
- Simon added a $29/month plan and set its limits somewhere between the free plan and the $99 plan.
- Result: "substantially more signups", but almost all on the $29 plan. Barely anyone takes the $99 plan, and almost nobody the $399 plan.
- The few $399 customers are mostly ones Simon talked to personally. He closed them with a sales conversation and added something extra to make the plan worthwhile.
- He explains the pressure as a symptom of the SaaS market: "so many offers… so many competitors" that prices keep getting driven down.
- Next steps he is considering:
  - lower the limits on the $29 plan so it is less attractive, or
  - widen the gap in limits between the $29 and $99 plans so the jump in price looks worth it.
- Pricing psychology he uses:
  - An **anchor**: a high top price makes the middle plan look cheaper.
  - A **reverse anchor**: a very cheap plan with very low limits pushes people toward the middle plan, because "I might as well jump to the middle one".
- He checks whether the extra subscriptions raise total revenue, and he sets limits so the new plan does not cost more to deliver.

## Fixing churn from feature requests

- Simon warns that building the feature churned users ask for is "a little bit like playing Russian roulette".
- On FeedHive, users kept saying one missing feature was why they cancelled. When the team shipped it, nothing changed, because users found a new reason to churn.
- Smaller things users had phrased as "it would also be nice" were different. When FeedHive added those, churn dropped significantly.
- Interviewing users one-to-one about why they leave is hard to do, but it is the way to collect these data points.

## Simon's rule on using your unfair advantage

- He agrees with Kyle that you should lean on your existing expertise when choosing a channel.
- His own: "video ads for me and we all have these things that will just get us ahead of the game faster".
- When launching something new, he asks where his unfair advantages are and capitalizes on them.
