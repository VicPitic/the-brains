## Starting without an audience: Simon's failed first SaaS, Sigmetic

- Simon's first SaaS (about three years before this 2022 interview) was Sigmetic. It plugged into GitHub and crunched numbers on code pushed and pull request reviews to show managers who the high performers on an engineering team were. It also had a Leaderboard-style dashboard for a big TV screen.
- He got it fully built, with signup and paid plans, and ended with "exactly zero paying users."
- Root cause: he was thinking like a salesman (the 80/20 rule, where 20% of salespeople produce 80% of the sales) and applied sales culture to developers. Developers did not want to be on a leaderboard, and managers did not think it measured code quality or kept developers motivated.
- He had access to real users (the managers and engineers at the company he consulted for) and ignored their rejection. His mindset was "they just don't want to change... I know better," and he leaned on the Henry Ford "faster horses" story.
- His lesson: "Absolutely no one is going to be interested in this." Listen to the few people who comment on your idea, and don't wave the feedback away.
- He tried launching on Reddit, Product Hunt and Indie Hackers with no audience or online authority. It was "like yelling out on the board literally no one cared." This pushed him to spend a year building an audience before his next product.

## Audience building: give value with no ask, then wait for the audience before launching

- The trigger was a chapter on audience building in the book Rework by Basecamp.
- His first six months of posting on Twitter and LinkedIn went nowhere. Every post said "follow me here, sign up for my newsletter, sign up for my SaaS." It was "extremely centered around what I wanted to get out of it," and that comes through when you are a small channel.
- He gave himself one last month: share value for free and ask for nothing. If he didn't reach 1,000 Twitter followers in that month, he would quit audience building.
- Result: he crossed 10,000 followers in roughly a month, over a summer.
- He then committed not to launch a new product until he had at least 30,000 Twitter followers. He spent another four or five months sharing content with no ebooks and no links to click.
- Topic choice: freelancing and how to get clients, because that was what he had accomplishments in. His rule is to have a bare minimum of authority before you teach a topic. That can be having built a SaaS, having worked at a startup, or having interviewed many startup founders.
- Short videos recorded in his living room in one take, with little editing, did well on Twitter. Followers kept saying he'd do well on YouTube. He put the videos on a YouTube channel, then tried one properly edited long-form video and "was completely sold." He edits his own videos in Adobe Premiere and learned from tutorials as a complete amateur.

## Building the FeedHive MVP with early users instead of choosing features himself

- The idea came from his own need to schedule content. HypeFury (Twitter only) was doing well. Buffer and Hootsuite felt old and clunky ("imagine being an agency having to sit a full working day in that tool"). Typefully followed HypeFury. Simon saw demand for modern lightweight tools and decided to "take a bite of the big cake" and replace Hootsuite, Buffer and Agorapulse.
- His MVP method: don't pick the features yourself, "find users immediately to help you get there."
- He announced in a Twitter chat group of content creators that he'd build a multi-platform scheduler and needed help. He offered lifetime access for free to anyone who joined a small group to help build it. About 25 people joined.
- The first MVP was a website with a text box to write a tweet or thread and schedule it, nothing else.
- Their top request was a proper calendar view, since many tools only had a queue of tweets, while Hootsuite was one of the few with a calendar. So the MVP became a Twitter scheduler with automatic queueing and a real calendar view.
- Six weeks of building went on quietly with that group. Then he announced it on Twitter and shared screenshots and updates (building in public). A month later he started letting people in.
- Because the 20 to 25 helpers were big creators with large followings, they shared it widely at the public beta launch.
- His summary: "Get the people in there first and then just listen closely to what they would want to see first."

## FeedHive growth numbers and how users are acquired

- The day of the public beta launch, FeedHive hit $300 MRR within the first 24 hours or so, and about $1,000 MRR by the end of that month.
- After that, he says it is "a very boring story." FeedHive added roughly $1,000 MRR every month, with no explosive growth and no down months.
- At the time of the interview it was about $30-32K MRR, and he was approaching $400K ARR.
- Acquisition mix: organic awareness campaigns (many run by Simon himself) plus paid ads. A lot of users come from paid ads, mainly Facebook and Google.
- Spend: more than $50,000 on ads in that year alone, and a lot of that was money wasted on experimenting.

## Paid ads: let the platform target, put the effort into creatives

- Paid ads are not a silver bullet. Targeting is relatively trivial because the platforms' AI models handle it, so don't build ultra-narrow audiences or do heavy audience research. "You just let the platforms do the thing."
- What matters is the creative: "You need to make killer ads, ads that almost look like great content in itself." He says people who think ads are a magic tool usually have poor creatives and a weak offer presentation.
- It costs a lot to learn, but once you have a winning ad, "you just let it run and users just drop in." He calls it "user acquisition while I sleep." It is inherently scalable, unlike cold outreach, sales calls or constant influencer outreach.
- He has a Figma board full of ad creative assets and a team that helps produce ad creatives and videos.
- When to start: not as your first move. First be confident you have an onboarding flow and a way to convert paying users. Early on, while still finding product-market fit, ads can be used to validate ideas, but as an acquisition channel they are not the first thing he'd start with.

## Landing pages: sell the difference or the outcome, depending on how familiar the category is

- If people already know the concept (e.g. a social media scheduler), focus on what makes you stand out: a calendar view or features competitors lack. The outcome ("you will grow on social") matters but people already get it.
- Picture the visitor with your site and five competitor tabs open, and help them pick yours instead of convincing them they need the category.
- If few people are familiar with the concept or the way you solve the problem, be much more outcome oriented and explain the problem first.

## Systems, templates and a reusable asset library as the way he produces so much

- He says he works about 60 to 65 hours a week, more than a typical full-time job, and he pushes back on the idea that he works 120 hours. He says he does nothing magic to manage time. He works on whatever has priority or gives him energy, and he is good at context switching.
- The real driver is process focus. For anything repeated he asks: can I automate it, delegate it, or at least document it step by step?
- Every time he does something, he asks whether one or two parts can become a general-purpose building block for later projects.
- Examples of his asset library: pre-edited YouTube thumbnails, a stock b-roll library of himself filming with a computer and showing various emotions, code packages, landing page templates and boilerplate, and a large Figma board of ad creative assets.
- He keeps a huge internal wiki ("an internal Wikipedia page") with well-documented processes, so he can delegate or hire quickly without much onboarding.
- Example: after 100-plus YouTube videos, each one produced something reusable that makes editing the next one faster.
- Tools he names: FeedHive (to schedule his content, he was the first user) and ClickUp, which he prefers to Trello (a long-time favorite) and Notion, since it replaced several tools for him and his team as a calendar, notes and project management tool.

## Hiring, delegating and running contractors

- He has no employees, only contractors.
- After launching FeedHive he brought on a few early adopters as part-time contractors and a friend from Denmark as a would-be co-founder, reaching a team of five fast. Growth did not materialize on its own, they were burning too much money too fast, and the co-founder arrangement (a Danish company with his Swiss company) had too much logistical friction. Within two to three months he removed the whole team and was alone for about four months. He then rebuilt the team slowly as revenue came in.
- His advice: before hiring for a role, try it yourself. For design, use Canva. For ads, build a full campaign and burn some money on your own bad creatives. Then you'll know how to specify and manage the work, since "they can't read your minds."
- Hire when you can write a clear specification and checklist (fonts, colors, border styles). Telling a designer "make it look good" leads to frustration on both sides.
- He goes further: the best time to hire is to buy yourself time, delegating work you could already do perfectly well yourself and can specify down to the smallest detail.
- Management without experience quickly leads to loose ends and stress.
- His remaining worry: if he pulled himself out completely, his company would not run well, because he is still working in the business more than on it. His fix is better-documented processes, "the whole manual to my company." His plan is to step back over five to ten years and eventually sell, since "it's a SaaS company, it's not going to be living forever."

## Going full-time, runway, salary and the LinkDrip pre-sale approach

- Path: sales (starting around age 18-19, working on commission only, selling search ads for the Danish version of Google) to freelance web development (WordPress, Joomla), built up on the side until he could go full-time freelance. He rated freedom above the higher income he could have made in sales. He then did consulting at startups and bigger companies before his first SaaS.
- He moved from Denmark to Switzerland over two years before the interview. Swiss residency required a work contract, so he has paid himself a salary from day one of FeedHive. It is as low as he can get away with, and most profit is reinvested into the business, LinkDrip and the YouTube effort.
- He left consulting for FeedHive when it was around $500 MRR, with savings from consulting giving him roughly a year of runway. He said the plan was to "burn this money" and make it profitable within a year, and he would not necessarily recommend this because it is risky.
- On sales skills carrying over: the direct craft barely transfers because FeedHive is low-touch self-serve. The mentality does: he is not afraid of selling or marketing, which many developers are.
- LinkDrip (his new SaaS) differs from FeedHive by pre-selling first rather than launching to a free closed community. He sold many lifetime deals in the previous month, funding the build, and had over 650 early adopters on a waiting list. He kept the community-driven approach: a public Trello roadmap where people suggest and vote on features, reviewed every day to decide what to prepare for the private beta launching in a few months.
