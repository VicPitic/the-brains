## Simon's three-tactic path to $1,000 MRR in 30 days

- Simon cites public Indie Hackers data: bootstrapped SaaS takes on average nine months to reach $1,000 MRR, and five months already counts as an outlier. He notes the dataset was built by one individual creator and was small, so it should be taken "with a grain of salt", but he doesn't think the results are way off.
- His claim: almost all successful bootstrapped SaaS founders do three things consistently: build a social media presence, find early adopters to build with, and build in public.
- His own result with FeedHive: $300 MRR in the first 24 hours after launch, the first $1,000 MRR after about a month, then another $1,000 MRR added every month for the next year.
- He says this happened with no big Product Hunt launch, no Hacker News front page and no TechCrunch coverage. The launch was a single tweet, backed by the audience, the early adopters and the content he had already built.
- He adds that you still need a SaaS idea that actually works, otherwise the tactics are wasted.

## Tactic 1: Build a social media presence before building the product

- Simon's examples of bootstrapped founders who are very active on social media and share valuable content: Arvid (FeedbackPanda, bootstrapped to $50,000 MRR before selling), Mike (Upvoty, over $30,000 MRR and growing fast), and Tony (BlackMagic, $8,000 MRR within months of starting earlier that year).
- He argues it is less difficult than people think and takes patience, consistency and simply getting started.
- His own start was low-budget: videos shot on a phone, normal indoor lamps, and a laptop placed slightly behind the camera as a teleprompter. They were posted on Twitter and people still liked them. He barely got any likes at first and kept going, focusing on creating value.
- His steps:
  - Pick a platform; he suggests Twitter or LinkedIn.
  - Post daily, one to two posts.
  - Pick a topic you want to be known for. It doesn't have to relate to your startup, but you should be able to see yourself posting about it long term.
  - Make a free Canva account and use image templates to spice up content.
  - Schedule content ahead of time (he uses FeedHive, his own startup) so you don't spend all your time on social media.
- Proof point: he grew a following of more than 200,000 people across four platforms in a little under two years, and built the first version of FeedHive while building his own presence.
- Don't monetize the audience for a long time. No affiliate links, no ebook, no course. "Just keep sharing valuable content without asking for anything in return, no strings attached."
- Use the time to build authority, credibility, and connections with other creators and entrepreneurs. He says social media is "just as much about networking and building relationships as it is about creating content."

## Tactic 2: Find early adopters and build the product with them

- Order of operations: while building an audience, connect with other creators who have their own personal brands and audiences. Then, before putting an MVP together, find early adopters.
- The key distinction: "You need users to build your product with, not for."
- How to do it:
  - Find a group of people interested in what you plan to build.
  - Invite them into a closed chat group or Discord server.
  - Include them in all decisions, big and small. You still build it, but they help shape the product from day one.
- Two benefits:
  - You are much more likely to build something users actually want.
  - You get advocates from day one. Users who feel part of the project are loyal, and if they are creators and entrepreneurs with their own audiences, they multiply your reach on launch day.
- What he did with FeedHive: the first users came in when the product was "a text field on a grey website and a few buttons", buggy and ugly. He built the MVP for almost two months with a closed group, discussing features, pricing and market positioning daily.
- He says that building alone on gut feeling would have "completely missed the mark."
- Common founder mistake: building far ahead without knowing who it is for, and treating validation as a few people saying "sure, that sounds great" after hearing the idea once. His rule: "You need to keep validating your product."
- The group later grew into a Facebook group of more than 1,000 members. The purpose is unchanged: building the product in close collaboration with the community, which still takes part in decisions on FeedHive's direction.

## Tactic 3: Building in public as a social media marketing strategy

- Simon's definition: "creating content from your journey of building a startup." He acknowledges others feel strongly about the open startup movement and says he is only sharing his own perspective.
- He frames it as a social media marketing strategy: create content that is interesting and valuable to your audience, which drives awareness, leads and subscribers to your SaaS.
- No obligations: you don't have to share revenue numbers, employee pay, or any secrets or insights you think are business critical.
- What to share:
  - The ups and downs of running a startup and the process of creating.
  - Tips, advice, learnings and findings as they come in.
  - The unique, "juicy" parts, not boring generic startup advice.
- Be honest and open. He says people connect better with "realistic and small rather than extraordinary and mind-blowing", so don't try to impress anyone. People want something realistic that motivates them to feel they could do it too.
- His timing: he started sharing about FeedHive a month before it went live, and the early adopter group started doing the same. That combination produced the 24-hour launch result of $300 MRR.
