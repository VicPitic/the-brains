## Finding a problem with founder fit before testing the market

- Simon's starting scenario: no audience, no followers, no subscribers, empty bank account, no network. His approach to building a SaaS from scratch is four steps.
- Step one is finding a problem that fits him as the founder ("founder fit"), before asking whether the market needs the product. He runs three bootstrapped SaaS products (two built from scratch, one acquired).
- Three questions test founder fit:
  - **Can you dogfood it?** Are you the ideal customer? If so, you speak the users' language, so talking to users is easier, and you stay motivated because you need the product yourself.
  - **Are you passionate about the problem?** Check whether you get excited by "I'm here to make it easier for people to X". There will be "a lot of struggles and emotional roller coasters", and as a founder you have no boss, "only commitment you have is your commitment to solving this problem".
  - **What are your unfair advantages?** Everyone has some: expertise, experience in a field, rare insight, or knowing someone who knows someone. He compares it to "playing Monopoly but you're allowed to cheat". Dogfooding plus passion is already a big advantage.
- Exercise: list problems you have yourself, list problem domains you are passionate about, list your unfair advantages, then cross-reference them to find the ideal problem.
- After that, test the problem and solution in the market (covered in a separate video).

## How FeedHive passed the founder-fit test

- FeedHive (social media management tool) started on Twitter in 2021. About six months before building it, Simon built a social media following.
- He wanted a robust social media tool for aspiring entrepreneurs, and existing tools were lacking, so he was the first user (dogfooding).
- He was committed to the problem because he had seen the power of having an audience and wanted to scale to other platforms.
- Unfair advantage: he had grown a Twitter audience of more than 30,000 followers in a few months and was good at marketing himself on social media.
- A handful of other founders spotted the same opportunity at the same time. According to Simon, none of those tools exist today, because those founders "weren't really that much into social media" and lacked the same unfair advantage.
- If starting again, he would make sure he could tick all these boxes again.

## Pick one organic distribution channel and double down

- Step two is creating one organic distribution channel for reaching users. He would focus on a single channel and know it well enough to navigate it when things get unpredictable.
- Options he lists: blogging and SEO, social media content, a YouTube channel, an email newsletter, a community (Discord or Facebook group), or word of mouth.
- His own experience with social media and YouTube has been "absolutely outstanding". The goal is a win-win: create value, help and inspire upcoming entrepreneurs, while building awareness of your product and mission.
- Tactic:
  - Find small online groups and communities centered on the problem you are solving.
  - Note the questions and pains people repeatedly raise.
  - Answer them through content: short videos for TikTok and Instagram Reels, long-form videos for YouTube, blog posts, a personal newsletter, or a Facebook group or subreddit devoted to the problem.
  - Once one channel works well, drop the others and double down on it. That becomes the organic distribution channel.

## Build in public with your community

- Step three is building the product. By now you know the problem well, have it yourself, and have talked to peers. The content you made has produced many ideas, so "what should I build" should no longer be a question.
- Build in public, alongside the community you have been engaging with. You are already seen as an authority on the problem.
- On fear of copycats: "just let them". Without your authority, commitment and unfair advantages in the area, others "don't have a chance to compete with you".
- Collect as much feedback and ideas as possible. Many community members like being part of the project, and they act as a board of advisors for a fresh perspective when you have doubts.
- Eager users may pay before the product is finished.

## Pre-selling LinkTrip before writing code

- LinkTrip is a no-code link engagement builder. It was sold as a lifetime pre-sale offer "before we wrote a single line of code".
- Result: almost $100,000 in sales in around two months.
- Simon attributes this to a strong community and people being so eager about the solution that they were happy to join early.
- He says he "cannot recommend this approach enough" and would do it again from scratch.

## Ship the first version fast with no-code and AI

- Use no-code and AI to get a first version into users' hands quickly:
  - Webflow for the landing page.
  - ChatGPT to create copy.
  - Bubble to build the first MVP of the app.
  - If confident coding, use as many open-source libraries as possible.
- Anything that gets a first version ready faster is the right choice. Then release it.
- From that point you should start to see early users, paying subscribers and revenue "just around the corner".

## Expect failure and build a SaaS factory of reusable assets

- Step four: expect it all to fail. "Statistically speaking you will fail", so make sure the effort isn't wasted by creating reusable building blocks.
- Turn the process into an ideation framework for generating new ideas. Understand your unfair advantages, then improve them to be "even more unfair".
- Build distribution so it can be reused to target the same users with a new product.
- Make reusable templates of creatives so new videos, blog posts and content are easy to produce.
- Generalize code and publish it as private npm packages. With no-code, save recipes and templates.
- His own chain of reuse: his first SaaS, Sigmetic, never made it, but he reused a lot of it to create FeedHive a year later, then reused a lot of that to create LinkTrip a year after.
- He used the same assets to create at least three other SaaS products in the same period. They never passed initial validation, and because they were fast to build he had "zero second thoughts" about dumping them when they wouldn't work.
- Metaphor: building SaaS products is like playing darts. You often miss, sometimes hit the bullseye. Keep creating new arrows so you get another throw. "Instead of creating a SaaS you should create a small SaaS factory."
