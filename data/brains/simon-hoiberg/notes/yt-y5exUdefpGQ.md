## Building an audience before building the SaaS

- Simon's first SaaS failed. He built it in secret, nobody had the problem, and the launch on Reddit, Twitter and Indie Hackers "drowned" among other people's posts. He describes the worst part as "yelling out into the void".
- After reading *Rework* (Basecamp) and its emphasis on audience trust and attention, he decided the next SaaS might still fail, but "it's certainly not going to be from not being heard".
- His first audience attempt also failed. He was impatient and every post carried "follow me", "follow me on this other platform", "subscribe to my newsletter", and even kept promoting the failed SaaS. His agenda "shined through too clearly".
- He then ran an experiment: two months of sharing the best value he had with no asks (no follow requests, no sign-ups), ready to drop the whole approach if it failed. The goal was 1,000 Twitter followers in two months.
- Result: more than 10,000 followers after six weeks. He kept the no-ask approach for about six months and reached roughly 30,000 followers before ever mentioning FeedHive.
- Why it works, in his view: people are used to valuable content being "a secret sales funnel in disguise" and expect the conversion attempt around the corner. Six months of giving without asking meant that when he first asked for money, "I was taken so much more seriously".

## Growing on Twitter: content, engagement and time spent

- Mix of two activities: writing original content and engaging with other people's posts, roughly a 50/50 split of effort at the time.
- Engagement tactic: add real value to posts by much bigger accounts, without stealing their thunder or traffic and with no shameless plugs. Big accounts notice and may retweet you. Valuable replies also get liked, which lifts them to the top of the comment section, and he calls this a far better way to direct traffic than asking for follows or stacking hashtags.
- Time: about 8-10 hours a week writing content. He batch-scheduled ahead, using a Sunday to write content, which left weekday time to engage (about 30-60 minutes a day).
- He engages less now that he has bigger audiences on multiple platforms.
- Current audience: about 100K on Twitter, 25K on Instagram, 30K on LinkedIn, about 12K on YouTube.
- Short, concise writing on Twitter helped him sharpen ideas, which carries over to writing YouTube titles. He notes good short titles are harder than they look.

## Validating FeedHive with 25 users and a lifetime offer

- The idea came from his own pain. He tried Hootsuite and Buffer ("I absolutely hated it"), found them monolithic, clunky and slow, and found smaller startup tools too Twitter-specific. He wanted one fast tool with first-class LinkedIn and Instagram support plus newer features like Twitter threads and conditional posting.
- Despite a crowded market, he paid attention to discussions on Twitter and LinkedIn and found others with the same problem.
- He collected 25 people from Twitter (also active on LinkedIn and Instagram) and built an MVP just for them, inviting them in after two weeks of building.
- Offer: lifetime free unlimited usage in exchange for being the early group. Feedback ran in a Twitter group chat ("could have been a WhatsApp group or a Slack server").
- The core product was shaped in daily discussions with this group over about two months, then he launched a public beta.
- The beta got good traction on Twitter and LinkedIn, and within a few weeks FeedHive had its first ~$1K MRR, which told him "there's actually something here".
- He posted screenshots and progress updates ("building in public") to his 30K followers to check for outside interest, and some people wrote saying they would pay to get in early.
- He admits the lifetime-free offer is a leap of faith: free users saying "yes I would have paid" is biased validation.
- On pre-selling: he likes charging before the product exists as "the ultimate validation", but warns people over-promise with landing pages full of features they will never build. If you charge upfront, make sure what you promise is realistic. The fact that 25 people stayed engaged for two months was his secondary validation that he could deliver.

## FeedHive's growth and ad funnel

- FeedHive was founded in late 2020. At the time of the interview (July 2022) it was around $200K ARR, with a team of five contractors and no employees. Hiring permanent staff in Switzerland is expensive, so he uses contractors.
- Most FeedHive users come from Facebook ads, run as a multi-step funnel with retargeting:
  1. Promote relevant content to a cold audience (blog posts such as "best alternatives to Later", or "is it worth using Canva as a social media scheduler" after Canva announced a scheduler).
  2. Retarget people who visited the blog post with ads about social media and its benefits, moving them from unaware to problem/solution aware.
  3. A further sequence for people who engage with the retargeted audience: discounted offers, trials, sign-up prompts, feature highlights.
- Principle: in a super competitive space like social media schedulers, "you can't just promote an ad saying sign up for my product". Warm people up so they recognize your brand patterns in text and graphics.
- Consistent branding matters: an in-house graphic designer produces recognizable creatives, and an agency produces content written to work both for SEO and for ads.
- Calculating acquisition cost per user gets more complicated with multi-step funnels, which he accepts.

## Selling ebooks with Instagram and Facebook ads

- His ebooks are software development titles, priced at $19 (on sale at $29 at the time he mentions "290 on a sale" in the transcript, which is ambiguous, so treat the price as roughly $19). About 98% of ebook revenue comes from Facebook ads, mostly shown on Instagram.
- Instagram audience as social proof: when someone sees an ad, they click the profile to see who is selling it. Many followers, engagement and helpful comments make the ads convert better.
- Engagement also earns a small reward from Facebook: slightly cheaper ads, and an edge in the ad auction if the content is engaging.
- Ebook funnel is simple: one ad, a tiny funnel and some retargeting. Cheap price allows impulse purchases from cold audiences, with retargeting for those who only visit the site.
- He never "boosts" organic content. He keeps ads separate so he can track return on ad spend (ROAS).
- Selling directly from posts does not work well for him on Twitter, though he plugs products on viral tweets.

## Paying Instagram pages for shoutouts

- Early on he did not run ads, because ads with no social proof are ineffective and expensive. Instead he paid big profiles open to promotion to share his content and mention him.
- Includes curated accounts that mostly repost the best content in a niche (for example, a JavaScript account), which credit the creator properly.
- Example price: about $150 for a post plus a 24-hour story plus his name in the page's bio for 24 hours, on an account with about 800,000 followers.
- He calculated roughly 10 cents per follower from some of these promotions, which can beat most ad platforms if followers are the objective.
- Caveat: he found this works better on Instagram than anywhere else. When he asked bigger YouTubers about paid channel shoutouts, nobody was interested.

## Building a YouTube channel alongside the SaaS

- He initially did not want YouTube (too time consuming, too much to learn). He had built a brand on Twitter with 2-minute explainer videos (JavaScript, getting freelance clients) before Shorts and Reels took off. People kept asking for a channel, so he posted those short videos on YouTube and had about 1,000 subscribers almost overnight, then went into it more seriously.
- Channel topic: online business (SaaS, selling ebooks and info products, dropshipping experiments, ways programmers can start businesses). About 60 videos, around 12.6K subscribers at the interview.
- Revenue: about $4K per month, split roughly 70% sponsors, 15% ad revenue (about $500/month), 15% attributed ebook sales. He cautions that 13K subscribers earning $4K a month is not typical.
- Topic matters: advertisers bid more for online business, e-commerce and personal investing than for programming or gaming, so ad revenue per view is higher.
- Sponsorship pitch: he charges more than a typical YouTuber his size, with the promise that he is determined to reach 100K subscribers that year. Sponsoring now is a good investment because videos are evergreen and the price will rise once he hits that mark. His other social audiences act as extra proof.
- Schedule: one big video every other week, plus two Shorts in the off week. He does scripting, filming and editing himself. He plans to hire editors and is working on onboarding systems.
- Strategy: he aims at the YouTube home page (browse features), where most of his traffic comes from, rather than search. Thumbnails and titles are key.
- Sourcing ideas: look at similar channels to see what themes work, and listen to repeated questions from his own audience. He adds storytelling, which raised retention "tremendously". A topic must pass a checklist, including whether he can tell a good story about it.

## Thumbnail-first video process and scripting

- Order of work: idea, then title and thumbnail, then script, then film and edit. If he cannot produce an exciting title and a thumbnail that gives the right association, he discards the idea before putting in the effort.
- Test: paste the title and thumbnail into a screenshot of the YouTube home grid, glance at it, then look away. You have about one second to create the right association. He sends the screenshot to a few brutally honest people and asks "would you click?" and drops the idea if they say no.
- Scripting: he writes full scripts and reads them from a teleprompter, about 95% word by word, because as a non-native English speaker he rambles when freestyling. He adds annotations (pause here, emphasize this word) so the delivery does not sound read.
- Hours: a few hours a day on planning, scripting and writing for YouTube.

## Avoiding burnout and delegating

- Working on two things (FeedHive and YouTube) keeps him from burning out. Switching to the other project is his rest, because doing one thing constantly is "flexing the same muscle". YouTube is the creative outlet; the SaaS is management, systems and programming.
- Main upcoming challenge: both FeedHive and YouTube are taking off and he must recruit and onboard help without onboarding structures yet. With contractors, turnover is high, so he needs a repeatable process for people coming and going.
- On delegation: delegating is about buying time, not about hiring people more specialized than himself. Mentally it is hard because he knows he could do many tasks better himself in the moment.
- Recommendations he gives: *Build to Sell Radio* podcast (John Warrillow), the book *Storyworthy* (Matthew Dicks, on storytelling for videos and ads), and Arvid Kahl as a bootstrapper he follows.
