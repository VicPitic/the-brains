## Simon's case for paid ads as a bootstrapped SaaS channel

- Simon says paid ads are a "very very effective marketing channel" for bootstrapped and indie products, and answers "100% yes" to whether indie founders should run them.
- Set aside organic social media, paid ads is the channel he personally masters best, and he has been heavy on them since starting his SaaS products.
- Big-brand ads (he names monday.com and Zapier) often look like TV commercials, with huge production budgets. He argues they perform poorly on YouTube, and some big companies run ads at a loss just to stay top of mind.
- He says these companies are not hard to compete with. He cites an Intercom ad with 200,000 views and six likes: "everyone just scrolls by".
- A founder who already makes good social or YouTube content will likely do ads well, because most big companies do not use creators from this generation.
- Targeting and setup are now easy on Facebook, Instagram, YouTube and Google. The platforms' machine learning finds the right people, so the founder's job is "creating great creatives".

## Facebook and Instagram ads: creative is the whole game

- Simon separates direct-response search ads from creative-led platforms (Facebook, Instagram, YouTube).
- On creative-led platforms, setup is streamlined. Success comes down to creative.
- He warns against ads with no emotion. His examples are competitors' (Hootsuite, Buffer) "try it for 30 days, sign up today" ads with a logo. "No one's going to stop scrolling for that."
- Creative that stops the scroll, spikes a reaction and gets engagement beats generic creative.
- He says to wait on video ads unless you already know how to make video. If not, start with image ads on Facebook.

## Google search ads: expensive and landing-page-dependent

- Google search ads are a direct-response format shown to people actively searching, so they are intent-based.
- There is little creative lever (a headline and a description). You mostly pay Google to appear in search.
- Simon does not recommend them to beginners. For someone inexperienced they are likely where you waste the most money.
- They are quite expensive and rely heavily on the landing page doing the work.
- He does run search ads and says they convert very well.
- The other speaker (not Simon) mentioned a Google new-account coupon. That point is left out.

## The rage-bait Aidbase ad and why engagement makes ads cheaper

- Simon's best-performing Aidbase ad on Facebook says "Let's face it, it's time to replace your chat support team with AI". It shows a stressed support agent with a 19-minute average response time next to a happy robot with a 3-second average response time. It has about 800 reactions, roughly 150 comments and 18 shares.
- Almost all the comments are hostile, for example "I would rather talk to literally nothing than a chatbot". Simon says only about two are positive.
- None of the comments criticise Aidbase as a product. They bash AI and the idea that it takes human jobs. He calls it rage bait, deliberately designed to tap emotion and provoke this reaction.
- Simon says the tracking shows that for every one negative commenter, 5 to 10 people sign up.
- Why it still converts:
  - The likely buyer may be pro-AI and wants AI to take over support. The negative comments do not put them off.
  - Visible likes, comments and shares signal that something is happening around the tool.
- How the Facebook ad auction works, per Simon:
  - Each ad opportunity triggers a small auction among advertisers targeting that person.
  - Normally you let Facebook set your bid from your ad budget and the cost of a conversion.
  - Ads are not equal in the auction. Facebook values ads that perform well as content (people stop, read comments, reply, debate).
  - Facebook rewards good content with a small discount, so you show to more people at lower cost and use the budget more effectively.
- He is not saying to design ads to anger everyone. His point is that engagement is rewarded mechanically, and an emotional ad stops the scroll.

## How the winning ad was found: testing, then building on one sentence

- An earlier Aidbase ad pitched the freedom a founder gets from AI support. In a testing round, one caption line stood out: "it's time to replace your live chat support team with AI".
- That sentence spiked angry emojis and "replace" comments, even though the image did not match it.
- Simon took that sentence and built an image to emphasise the emotion:
  - Generated a tired man with headphones looking down in Midjourney.
  - Generated a happy robot with headphones in Midjourney.
  - Combined them in Figma in a split view with text.
  - Ran two to three more rounds of tests and tweaks.
- The tired-man image is Midjourney-generated, not a stock photo.
- Takeaway: let the data show which line resonates, then design the creative around that emotion.

## A testing process for founders on a small budget

- Simon says you can start with $100 to $200 a month. Do not expect many signups at first. Use the ads to test creative.
- Platforms let you combine different images and different text and show the combinations. Collect data on which image, caption and description resonate best.
- Process:
  1. Put new creatives in a dedicated test campaign (he does this for every new creative).
  2. Run a test at about $10 a day for four to five days.
  3. Look at which combination of caption, description and image resonates.
  4. Build the next test round on the winners.
  5. Repeat for roughly two to three months until you have a few winning combinations and a good intuitive feel.
  6. Then slowly scale the budget and see whether the ads give results.
- His goal in the first months is to learn, treated as "a game that you're paying a little bit of money for".
- Simon does not claim any one ad is the winner up front. He lets the data decide.

## Scaling too early and the cost of learning

- Simon says he spent more than $20,000 on ads for FeedHive before they started converting.
- He was aggressive and inexperienced. After about five conversions he decided he had a winning creative and scaled, which "is a great way to waste a lot of money really fast".
- Advertising has no free credits, so some money will be wasted while learning.
- He says you can learn this with a small budget, slowly, testing and iterating a lot.

## How to get started this week, plus YouTube video ads

- Simon's steps for a beginner:
  1. Create a Facebook ad account (about 5 minutes).
  2. Make a Canva or Figma account. Canva is a great choice for images and short videos.
  3. Use ChatGPT for ad copy. Do not use its output directly. Describe your product and target users, ask for three angles of ad creative and a suggested image, then tweak it.
  4. Put a few hundred dollars into Facebook and see what happens.
- He expects no results at first. His bet is that you will get hooked once you see comments, clicks and visits.
- If it does not click, ads may not be your channel, but he urges trying.
- YouTube video ads are the best-performing medium across his products, and he is heavy on them.
- Good YouTube ads look like a YouTube video. Make it in your normal YouTube style, spend one to two minutes promoting the product, and run it as an ad.
- He says that with some tuning and practice (the hook, the format) this would likely beat big TV-style commercials on return on ad spend.
- He suggests a YouTuber retarget their existing subscribers with a one-minute product ad.

## Side notes from the episode: Aidbase embeds and building with contractors

- Aidbase is Simon's AI chatbot and ticket-form product. Embeddable widgets previously used iframes, which he calls heavy and slow, with a bad user experience.
- He moved to Web components with Lit elements, which he now calls a "horrible decision" for developer experience. The team rebuilt it and now offers web components and an npm React version.
- On the free plan the widget carries small Aidbase branding. Because it is not in an iframe, Google crawls those links, so they act as backlinks.
- On managing developers, Simon says he sometimes jumps into code when he shouldn't. A task he expects to be quick can take five hours, so delegating is usually better. He sees being forced to manage as helping build clear communication and processes.
