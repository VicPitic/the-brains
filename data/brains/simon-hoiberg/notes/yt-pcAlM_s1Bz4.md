## Why micro SaaS is getting squeezed (the drop shipping parallel)

- Simon compares micro SaaS today to drop shipping around 2017: cheap to start, easy to copy, good margins at first.
- The drop shipping playbook: find a cheap product on AliExpress, list it on a Shopify store, run Facebook ads, and the supplier ships directly to the customer. In his words, "95% of the e-commerce operation belonged to someone else."
- The squeeze: more sellers of the same products, pricier Facebook ads, suppliers and Shopify taking their share, and customers learning to spot generic stores. Margins went paper thin because drop shippers stood "at the end of a long line of companies that all got paid first."
- E-commerce did not die. Winners started owning more of the operation: real brands, their own products (often via manufacturers or controlled inventory), and their own distribution through social channels and email lists.
- He argues SaaS is following the same path right now.

## The rented-stack micro SaaS model and why it is dying

- Micro SaaS boomed because one founder could build almost everything by wiring together rented services.
- Typical stack he describes: Vercel for hosting, Supabase for data, OpenAI for every AI request, Stripe for payments, and customers from Google, Meta, X, Product Hunt or "whoever controls the traffic this month." Again about 95% of the operation belongs to someone else.
- This worked while software was hard to build and customers had few options.
- AI now makes software "ridiculously cheap and fast to produce." Every small SaaS faces new competitors, free alternatives, open source projects, and bigger platforms adding the same feature into existing products.
- Meanwhile every rented layer (hosting, database, AI provider, payment processor, ad platform) gets paid before the founder, who keeps whatever is left.
- His conclusion: SaaS stays enormous, but the micro SaaS playbook of wrapping a few APIs, adding a monthly subscription and renting the whole operation is dying.

## What survivors need to do: reclaim infrastructure

- Self-host the product.
- Control the data and configuration.
- Understand the infrastructure.
- Make every important dependency replaceable.
- He admits this is harder than "connecting five services and launching another wrapper over the weekend," but argues easy businesses attract everyone, and then suppliers, platforms and advertisers slowly take the margin until nothing is left.

## What survivors need to do: own distribution

- Build an audience on social channels people actually follow, plus an email list you control.
- Ads should amplify that distribution instead of being the only way to reach a customer.
- Same lesson as the e-commerce brands that stopped paying Facebook for every customer.

## FounderStack: one-time purchase instead of subscriptions

- Most of Simon's SaaS products were sold as subscriptions. A little over a year before this video he introduced FounderStack, a bundle of all of them as a one-time purchase.
- It was meant to be a short campaign lasting a few months, but people kept buying consistently, so he kept it available alongside the subscriptions.
- He says the unit economics "actually work really well" because he has spent a lot of time making infrastructure very efficient, so products can be maintained without every customer paying monthly.
- He thinks "the old idea of buying software and actually owning it deserves a comeback."
- He acknowledges lifetime deals have a bad reputation, associated with cheap marketplace deals (he names AppSumo and Dealify) where you buy a random tool and hope the company exists next year. His goal is serious software that keeps improving while buyers own it instead of "leasing it forever."
- The bundle currently has four tools, with numbers five and six about to launch. He wants to keep expanding the portfolio with useful products sold through FounderStack.

## Simon's plan: self-hostable, open source, and owned hardware

- Stated as plans, not done: "We're not there yet."
- Make the entire SaaS portfolio self-hostable, and possibly open source.
- Move hosting from Hetzner to a Swiss company called Private Layer.
- Eventually rent real colocation space in a data center in Lucerne, running products on hardware he physically owns.
- That hardware would also run open-weight models such as Kimi, GLM and MiniMax, instead of depending on a rented AI provider.
- His reasoning: AI makes good software easier to build and maintain, and his infrastructure keeps getting more efficient.
- Stated direction: customers actually own the software they buy, with at least the option to fully self-host every product.
