## Why Simon builds bootstrapped SaaS and why $1M ARR is the target

- He frames $1M ARR as a huge milestone for "normal people" building bootstrapped businesses, even if VC-backed startups would shrug at it.
- His mantra: "forget all the big billion dollar headlines and just build a million dollar SaaS." He thinks many people can do it today.
- He has run three SaaS products, was about to launch a fourth, and runs a YouTube channel about SaaS and online business. He was a software developer and freelance consultant before going full-time into entrepreneurship about three years before this episode.
- He has tried other models and compares them to SaaS: dropshipping (a business reaching almost $1M a year, since gone), affiliate marketing, courses.
- Why SaaS wins for him: recurring income, the enjoyment of building, and the scalability of "sit and type some stuff on my keyboard and put it on the internet and then random people will just come by and give me their money."
- He notes most users of his tools have never spoken to him, and many don't know who he is. He likens that to the SaaS tools most people use daily without knowing the founders.

## The scale of his portfolio and the state of the market

- FeedHive: social media management tool. LinkDrip: link shortener. Both are his main revenue drivers.
- TinyQ: a small editing tool he acquired.
- 8base (eightbase): an AI support ecosystem covering chatbots, ticket forms and email, with AI as the primary helper and automation layer.
- He acknowledges SaaS growth rates were declining at the time (a ProfitWell chart of annual growth since 2021 was discussed), but he stays in the game anyway.
- He flags that SaaS is arguably the riskiest business model, because you can lose a lot of money.
- He says the downside is real, and that his co-host's six-figure-in-the-red experience shows how it can go.

## 8base launch trajectory and the post-launch plateau

- He launched 8base about two months before recording and made a YouTube video that drove the launch.
- MRR went up to about $7,000 relatively fast, then "the initial round of churn kicked in" and settled at roughly $4,000-5,000 MRR.
- The pattern: a launch spike from hype ("Simon's launching a new SaaS, let's try it out"), then growth flattened.
- His current job is turning spiky, launch-driven growth into a consistent, continuous growth engine.
- On reaching $1M ARR within a year: "I don't know man, it's a stretch." He says he is not in a big hurry.

## Free plan vs. opt-out trial: a deliberate departure from his other products

- FeedHive and LinkDrip use a 7-day opt-out trial. The credit card is required up front, access is blocked until trial signup, and the user is auto-upgraded after 7 days unless they cancel.
- Early on, FeedHive tried a freemium model. It "just didn't work." Putting everything behind a paywall made growth increase "much much much faster."
- With 8base he is deliberately trying the opposite, partly because of his open-source background:
  - A premium plan plus a fairly generous, fully free plan.
  - No trial, and no aggressive upgrade prompting.
  - Signup drops people straight into the tool. There is only a small modal in the corner, and upgrade prompts appear when limits are hit.
  - Features aren't locked out. Users can start using the tool immediately.

## The free-plan growth strategy: chat widgets as backlinks and branding

- The core of 8base is a chatbot. On the free plan the widget shows "Powered by 8base" branding.
- His main goal for now is getting the widget onto as many websites as possible rather than driving revenue.
- Reasons it works:
  - Backlinks: he does not use iframes. Google reportedly doesn't count widget links as backlinks if its crawler can identify them as widgets, and without an iframe it can't tell. So each embed is effectively a backlink.
  - Traffic and exposure: other visitors see the branding when they open the chatbot.
- Other levers: influencer marketing (the first round of influencers started posting the day of recording) and advertising a bit later. The influencer goal is again to get people onto the free plan and the widget onto their sites.
- AI cost is real. Free users get included AI, though they can bring their own OpenAI key to bypass limits. The result is that 8base is running at a loss.
- His stance: he expects to run in the red for most of the year and does not aim for profitability this year. He is happy to invest in widget distribution.
- He hopes to reach profitability before $1M ARR, and admits burning toward a million with no profit would be uncomfortable for a bootstrapper.

## Distribution experiments and "sneaky" growth ideas

- His general belief: bootstrappers competing with VC-backed tools in SEO and ads "need to be sneaky" and creative, because they lack budgets and staff.
- Idea he floated, which he says he doesn't plan to do: manual chatbot outreach. Crawl websites that have chat widgets, open the chat, strike up a conversation about their site, then pitch 8base. A small team of five could be hired on Upwork.
  - Best targets would be small ecommerce and dropshipping stores and small SaaS, where the founder is staffing the chat personally and is stressed.
  - Poor targets would be larger companies with live support agents, who may not want to promote a tool that replaces them.
  - He notes he has never heard of anyone doing this.
- On cold email, he points out that buying email lists and spamming is now clearly illegal (especially in the EU) without consent. He had not tried automated outbound at scale and called it a future experiment.
- Suggestion he received and says he'll check out: use a tech-detection tool like BuiltWith to build a list of sites using competitors' live chat, then run a multi-channel outreach tool (lemlist) combining LinkedIn interaction and email sequences.
- His sources of SEO and ads advice for others are limited here: he says SEO is not his strong suit, and that he leans on influencer marketing and advertising.

## Experimenting as an operating principle

- He wants the podcast itself to run "the lean way," like his SaaS: drop a bunch of experiments, try different formats, and use audience feedback to decide what works.
- He and his co-host agreed to hold each other accountable for outlandish growth experiments and report results in later episodes (a viral-style post test on one side, chatbot outreach or lemlist outreach on his).
