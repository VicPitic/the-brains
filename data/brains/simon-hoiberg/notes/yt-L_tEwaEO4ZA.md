## The "Fast Fashion" problem in SaaS

- Simon calls it the "Temu effect": tools like Lovable, Cursor and Claude Code mean there will be SaaS products for almost everything.
- He expects AI to write about 95% of the code for many products, design the interfaces and UX, and debug and QA its own output.
- His view: these products probably won't be amazing, but many buyers will settle, "just like cheap products on Temu."
- To avoid the race to the bottom, he says it is more important than ever to position a product uniquely in a crowded market.
- He frames eight common SaaS positions, each with pros, cons and examples. The test: which position is your product in, and should you change it?

## Position 1: Low-cost alternative

- Be openly a cheaper alternative to a well-known tool in an established market that solves a known problem.
- Example: Papermill-style "low-cost Zapier alternative" (Simon names "Papley" as a low-cost Zapier alternative that markets itself this way openly).
- Pros:
  - No need to educate the market or convince anyone the problem exists.
  - Dropping the incumbent's name plus "cheaper" instantly aligns the right customers with the value prop.
  - Makes paid advertising an ideal channel.
- Con: the "cheap knockoff" or "AI slop" vibe can attach to you.
- Advice for indie hackers building with AI tools: nothing wrong with it, but it pays off to "call it what it is" in your messaging and offer.

## Position 2: Light version

- Solve the problem in a simpler way, with a more minimalistic and often more limited interface.
- Examples:
  - Canva started as a light version of Photoshop.
  - Simon's own tool TinyKiwi is now a light version of Canva.
  - Mailer Lite-style "light" tools for email, a light version of Mailchimp and SendGrid, with "light" in the name.
- Pro: captures less tech-savvy users who find the alternatives too complex and overwhelming.
- Con: it is hard to stay light as you evolve. Canva grew into complex software that many users now struggle with.

## Position 3: Enterprise

- Solve a huge, frequent, painful problem, with extra focus on customer service, tailored solutions, manual setup and account management.
- Simon sees this as where SaaS meets tech-enabled service businesses.
- He thinks these hybrid software-plus-service businesses are the most likely to survive the Temu effect "because they still heavily depend on humans."
- Examples: HubSpot and Salesforce.
- Pro: this is where real money flows. HubSpot charges more than $3,000 per month for its enterprise solution, which he says is on the cheaper end of enterprise software.
- Con: the business and infrastructure behind these services are often extremely complex.

## Position 4: Unique player

- Offer a very unique approach, often thanks to access to certain resources or specific expertise few others have.
- Examples:
  - Grammarly was one of a kind in helping users improve writing, before the AI era.
  - OpenAI was essentially alone for its first three or four years until competition caught up.
- Con: you must constantly fight for uniqueness. Few business models or tech products are immune to being copied, so appearing unique gets harder over time.

## Position 5: First mover

- Be first to discover a problem and claim a new market.
- Example: Slack, which pioneered chat-based team collaboration. Discord and Microsoft Teams have far more users, yet Slack is still seen as the industry standard.
- Con 1: you probably have to educate the market about a problem people may not know they have, which is "painfully difficult."
- Con 2: as the first mover you make all the mistakes, and competitors learn from them without taking the same hit.

## Position 6: Niche

- Solve a problem for a very specific, small market segment and tailor the tool to that user.
- Example: Oberlo, at its core an inventory management system tailored to dropshipping businesses using Shopify. It served that narrow segment until Shopify acquired it and folded it in.
- Simon calls this one of the best, most tried-and-tested positions, and "probably the best position" for a small indie SaaS.
- His advice: find a small group of users and build for them. Don't worry that big players solve the same problem, because you customize the whole platform for that group.
- Con: it can cap growth. You may later have to expand to a bigger market, which can alienate your original core users.

## Positions 7 and 8: Privacy first and integrations

**Privacy first**
- Solve a problem while prioritizing data privacy and security above all else.
- Examples: Bam Analytics, a simple privacy-first alternative to Google Analytics that is very popular. n8n markets self-hosting as a way to protect data by deploying on-prem.
- Cons: you can lock yourself into a market segment that is too small. As privacy concerns grow, alternatives may adopt the same practices and outcompete you.

**Integrations**
- Position as the tool with the most third-party integrations. For some users it is the final drop that wins them over.
- Example: Zapier, which boasts 6,000 integrations, more than competitors like Make and IFTTT.
- Pro: clearly beneficial where integrations are central to the service.
- Con: competitors can catch up by offering the same integrations, so it is hard to protect.

## Positioning takeaways

- These eight are popular positions, not the only ones. New ones appear as markets evolve.
- A current example he gives, unique to the big AI players: "who can burn the most cash by offering the most AI requests for a fixed monthly fee."
- Reflection questions he poses: where does your product fit in, and how do you stand out?
- He says positioning is hard, and certain product types make it much harder. He says there are AI SaaS products he would avoid building entirely, and points to a separate video for that.
