## Why Simon avoids AI copywriter SaaS

- Simon calls the AI copywriter market flooded and saturated. GPT-3 launched in 2022, and the low barrier to entry played out. Early movers like Copy AI and Peppertype made it big, but now it is "very easy to spin up a new AI copywriter."
- If a tool only wraps OpenAI's API, it does basically the same as every other tool. He expects a race to the bottom on pricing and one of the highest churn rates in any market.
- He notes several big LinkedIn influencers shipping the same AI copywriters. He says it "smells a little bit like a cash grab," while allowing he may not see what is going on behind the scenes.
- He sees little that these tools offer beyond what standard LLMs like ChatGPT or Gemini already provide.
- Where it works: an AI copywriter as one feature inside a bigger product. FeedHive, his social media management tool, has an excellent one, but it is one of many features of an AI-powered marketing tool. As a standalone product he would stay far away.

## Why bootstrappers should not build AI coding tools

- Simon would avoid tools like Cursor and Windsurf as a bootstrap founder, even though they have product-market fit.
- Part of Cursor's appeal is how much it offers at a cheap price. Compared with Cline (open-source VS Code extension, bring your own API key), he concludes the average Cursor user is "very likely losing money for Cursor."
- Core problem with building on AI: LLM API pricing scales with consumption. More user interaction means higher expenses, so a successful active user base becomes a financial liability.
- A product that aggressively consumes AI has a business model that bleeds cash as it grows.
- Cursor is backed by top-tier investment funds that can absorb the losses to chase market dominance, a classic VC hypergrowth play.
- Competing there as a bootstrapper means competing on "who can afford bleeding the most cash," not on who builds the best product.

## Cost-per-usage economics of AI SaaS

- Simon states the general rule: LLM API cost is directly proportional to consumption, so heavy-use products erode margin as usage grows.
- Bootstrapped companies normally celebrate an active user base. With heavy AI consumption it becomes a liability.
- Implication he draws: avoid product categories where each user action triggers expensive inference unless you can price to cover it or are funded to absorb losses.

## Why he would never build an AI therapist

- The value prop is obvious (on-demand therapist in your pocket), and many people already use ChatGPT for emotional support. He still calls the idea "a complete ethical shitshow."
- Examples he cites:
  - March 2023: a man in Belgium ended his life after weeks of talking to an AI chatbot called Eliza on the Chai app. His wife said it encouraged him by convincing him he could save the planet by sacrificing himself.
  - 2023: the US National Eating Disorders Association took down its chatbot Tessa after it gave harmful suggestions such as restricting calories and losing weight, the opposite of its purpose.
- He does not claim the chatbots caused these events, since people may already have had trauma, depression or psychosis, and acting on advice is the person's choice.
- His point: if your product ends up in the middle of a case like that, it is "way, way too much to handle" for a small bootstrapper or indie hacker.
- He says Peter Levels started an AI therapist and quickly wrapped it up after pushback from his audience, which he believes was the right call.
- Rule: stick to non-critical products. Avoid anything touching people's physical or mental health.

## Why AI detector and humanizer tools are a bad bet

- The pitch: run AI-written content through a detector, fix the flagged parts so Google will not know you used AI. Advanced tools rewrite it for you.
- He sees three flaws:
  - **Wrong problem.** Google is clear that the problem is low-quality, spammy content made to manipulate rankings, not AI use. Content passing a detector can still get deranked if quality is low.
  - **Cat-and-mouse with Google.** You have no access to Google's algorithms, and you should not build a business guessing how their internal systems work.
  - **Worse content.** Patching an already-written piece is a terrible idea.
- Better direction: build a tool or service that produces great, high-quality content with AI from the start. Google does not mind AI as long as the content is good and helpful.
- He says a few tools already do this, and he has a video on building a custom workflow for it in n8n.

## Why AI summarizers are a feature, not a product

- Summarizing meetings or email threads is a real problem, but "it has been solved many times already." He puts it in the same bucket as AI copywriters, and says it is even worse.
- It is no longer a product but an "aggressively integrated and completely free feature" built into major productivity ecosystems.
- Example: Google Workspace summarizes emails in one click, natively and free, and every Google Meet call generates a recap with key action items.
- Trust barrier: you would need access to every email in a customer's inbox, including sensitive ones, plus every meeting and every employee's calendar. Companies do not hand that to a small bootstrapped company.
- He acknowledges users already trust Google or Microsoft with this data, and says he personally would not want to compete there.

## How Simon screens new SaaS ideas

- Context: he runs a portfolio of AI SaaS products covering social media, link tracking, customer support and graphic design. His team brainstorms, prototypes and tests new ideas every year.
- Screening rule: when a new idea falls into a category too close to these five (copywriters, coding tools, therapists, detectors, summarizers), they reject it immediately.
- Warning sign: ideas that feel seductive after playing with the latest models or a first automation workflow, where the build and go-to-market seem crystal clear. He says some of these are deceptively obvious and will "with overwhelming certainty" set you up for failure.
- Common thread across the five:
  - Saturated markets with a low barrier to entry.
  - A feature that platforms or LLMs give away for free.
  - Usage-based AI costs against VC-funded competitors.
  - Ethical or liability exposure that a small founder cannot absorb.
  - Solving the wrong problem or playing a game against a platform.
- He points to a simple approach for unlocking thousands of worthwhile micro SaaS ideas, covered in a separate video.
