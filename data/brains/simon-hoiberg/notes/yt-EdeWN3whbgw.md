## Checkpoint 1: Human-centric vs process-centric company DNA

- Simon cut his team from 11 people to 3 over the last year by replacing their work with AI. Revenue went up, output increased and margins improved. He runs a portfolio of 5 SaaS products.
- He cites an MIT study saying 95% of AI initiatives fail to deliver measurable value, so only 5% of companies actually succeed in replacing human work with AI. He says AI is not the problem; the failure is treating AI like a magic employee instead of a machine that follows strict instructions.
- He argues that replacing a team with AI only works for a specific type of company run by a specific type of owner. For others it "might be a terrible idea."
- **Human-centric business:** run by people for people, with the focus on culture, collaboration and goal alignment.
  - Flat structure, everyone involved in key decisions, a lot of effort on motivation and personal alignment with the mission.
  - Examples: creative agencies, high-touch consultancies, typical startups where culture fit matters as much as skills.
  - The value comes from the friction and chaos of human collaboration. Replacing the team with AI "will rip the soul out of your company." AI doesn't care about your mission, doesn't need to feel heard and is bad at nuanced cultural decisions.
  - In this kind of company you can augment your team with AI but not replace it.
- **Process-centric business:** runs like a machine on SOPs, guidelines and checklists.
  - Each person gets a specific task with clear requirements, a deadline and dependencies. Decisions are made at the top by the owner and the team executes.
  - Simon's business has always been heavily process-centric. His biggest hiring selling point was "no meetings, no ceremonies, work whenever you want as long as stuff gets done."
  - The flip side was little culture. He rarely involved the team in big strategic decisions and didn't require passion for the company vision. The team cared only as far as they needed context for their work.
  - He says this is why his AI transition worked: an AI agent loves clear instructions, SOPs and checklists, and needs no pep talk or Zoom meeting to feel included.

## Checkpoint 2: Are you a leader or an operator?

- Simon says the common advice that a successful CEO must be a great leader (rallying the team, empathy, resolving conflict, motivating people) is not true.
- **Leader:** superpowers are empathy, communication and culture building. He names Steven Bartlett, Dan Martell and Gary Vaynerchuk as examples.
- **Operator:** gets things done, is obsessed with systems, data and logic, strong at systems thinking, often lower on soft skills. His example is Elon Musk, who cares about "the physics of the problem, the efficiency of the line, and the output of the system."
- A great operator is usually not a great leader and vice versa. Natural leaders hire COOs or project managers to handle systems; natural operators hire team leads to handle people.
- Simon calls himself an operator, not a natural-born leader. He loves building systems and optimizing workflows but has never enjoyed motivation, conflict resolution or emotional alignment.
- Operators tend to end up running process-centric businesses, and leaders human-centric ones.
- For an operator, "AI is your dream employee": it needs logic and instructions, not empathy or motivation.
- A natural leader who thrives on personal connection will struggle to manage an AI workforce, because you can't motivate an AI, inspire it with a vision or take it to a one-on-one to resolve conflict.
- His conclusion: a leader running a human-centric business should not try to replace the workforce with AI and should invest in becoming a better leader instead, because that is where their ROI is. An operator running a process-centric business is in the perfect position to replace the team with AI.

## The RIP model: which tasks to hand to AI

- Even with the right business and the right personality, Simon says you can't fire everyone at random. He uses a framework he calls the RIP model (Repetitive, Predictable, Isolated) to pick which roles or tasks to replace.
- **R, Repetitive:** the task is identical every time. Examples: copying data from an email into a spreadsheet, formatting a monthly report, categorizing support tickets. "If a human is doing this effectively like a robot, an actual robot will do it better, faster, and cheaper."
- **P, Predictable:** there is a clear right or wrong answer.
  - Good fit: writing an SQL query that pulls active users from the database.
  - Bad fit: replying to the biggest enterprise client who is threatening to churn after a server outage. AI can write a polite apology but "can't read the room," doesn't know the client and doesn't know he needs an honest phone call to be saved.
  - "AI thrives on formulas. It fails on nuance."
- **I, Isolated:** the task can be done in a vacuum without tribal knowledge. Simon says most founders get R and P right but ignore I, and that this is where "90% of AI implementations fail."
- **Rule:** if a role's tasks are repetitive, predictable and isolated, you can replace it today. If it misses even one, at least keep a human in the loop in that workflow.
- Think about replacing tasks, not people.

## Why isolation matters: the tribal knowledge trap

- Simon uses AI to write code every day and says it is excellent at clean, high-quality code, but it assumes your code base makes sense, which most don't.
- Example failure modes: AI randomly refactors code, removes old functions or deletes an unused database table. It doesn't know about a Zapier automation set up 2 years ago that reads that exact table to run payroll.
- AI doesn't know the history of the company, the invisible dependencies or why a weird decision was made years ago.
- If a task needs deep integration with other moving parts of the business or deep historical context, it is not isolated. Replacing that role with AI means it "won't just fail," it will probably break things and "make a big mess."
- You can only give AI tasks that need no prior knowledge, or where the needed knowledge can be included briefly with the task itself.

## How the 5% succeed

- The failing 95% try to replace leadership, tribal knowledge and strategy with a chatbot.
- The successful 5% act as operators: they strip out the repetitive, predictable, isolated tasks and hand them to agents so humans can focus on "the things that actually require a pulse."
- Simon admits he has helped fuel the hype, with videos on building AI agents, social media content writers, SEO blog writers and AI support systems, and says this video is a caveat to that.
- As a worked example of a task that passes the RIP test, he points to a news research agent he built that creates an isolated workflow doing the work of a human researcher.
