## Pricing: why $5/month was a mistake and how "lean pricing" works

- Simon launched his social media marketing SaaS FeedHive with a price of $5 per month, reasoning that "a lower price and a better product" would make everyone come.
- He calls this wrong for two reasons:
  - Cost: FeedHive is a low-touch product (users sign up themselves) but integrates with third-party social platforms, so bugs and failures are inevitable. That demands solid customer support, which costs money. It also operates in a super competitive market, so growing fully organically without substantial marketing spend is unrealistic. $5 per user per month can't fund that.
  - Perception: when a product prices itself far below competitors, the first thought is "that is a shitty product." Most people won't believe a claim that it's also better.
- He warns against the opposite trap too. Some SaaS gurus say raising prices fixes everything; it doesn't. Products that position as "exclusive and premium" with prices twice as high as established competitors, without actually being premium (which most early products aren't), will backfire.

## Lean pricing: raise average revenue per user 5% a month until you meet resistance

- Start at a reasonable price point, a little below your biggest competitors, but not too much.
- Then aim to increase average revenue per user (ARPU) by 5% each month until you meet resistance.
- Targeting ARPU rather than the sticker price gives more room to experiment. Levers:
  - Reduce the discount for yearly sign-ups, or nudge more users toward monthly billing (trade-off: may hurt retention).
  - Push users to a higher-priced tier by adding benefits and features, or by adjusting the price gap between plans.
  - Add a more expensive plan at the top to act as a pricing anchor.
  - Raise prices outright.
- Resistance shows up as stagnating or falling new sign-ups, or people telling you it's too pricey compared to competitors.
- He suggests a spreadsheet to calculate how much churn you can accept from a price increase while still growing overall revenue.

## Execution: Build-Measure-Learn and the two ways founders break it

- Simon frames good execution around the loop from Eric Ries's *The Lean Startup*: build, measure, learn. A short feedback loop lets you create something, test it, measure effectiveness, adapt, and iterate.
- Failure modes:
  - **Stuck in learning:** endless research, trying to back everything with data, analysis paralysis. Founders rely on public data rather than their own, build a long-term plan, and never create anything, so they can't tell whether the idea works in their context.
  - **Stuck in building:** common among technical founders. They plan "just an MVP," a few hours turns into weeks or three months of coding, and no user has seen it. They have drifted far in one unvalidated direction, and launch day is usually disappointing.
  - **Over-execution (lottery playing):** build, launch, and if it doesn't catch on, start over with something new instead of adapting. They iterate only on the build step. He calls it "throwing pasta everywhere and hoping that something will eventually stick." Some indie-hacker influencers promote this and a few won millions, but he calls them "lottery winners" and not an approach to model.
- His recommended middle path:
  1. Do shallow initial research.
  2. Set initial goals and write a hypothesis for how you'll reach them.
  3. Build something quickly and get it in front of users.
  4. Compare results against the goals (you probably missed them, above or below).
  5. Hold results against your initial assumptions, adapt, and repeat.

## Don't fear existing markets and big competitors

- Many founders think a successful SaaS must be groundbreaking, like Airbnb, Uber, Tinder or Bolt, which created new markets. Simon says disrupting the market is needed only if you want to build and IPO a unicorn.
- For a small SaaS doing a few million a year, finding huge competitors is "a super good sign": the market is big, users are already looking for a solution, and you've skipped much of the market research and initial validation.
- Proof from FeedHive, which entered a market with a handful of huge competitors plus hundreds of small ones:
  - More than 10,000 users on the platform.
  - Turning over more than $300,000 a year.
  - Growing around 10 to 15% every month (as stated in the talk).
  - Winning users from the biggest competitors every day, because those tools are "old and clunky," built on outdated technology.
- His angle: solve the same problem with less friction and adapt faster to new features and changes on the social platforms.

## Leave your comfort zone and do the hard thing

- Founders tend to keep working on what they're already good at:
  - Developers and product people keep adding one more feature or making things faster, when deep down the missing piece is marketing.
  - Designers keep redesigning the landing page or polishing the product, while the core doesn't solve the user's problem well.
  - Marketers keep optimizing funnels, running tests and building multi-channel retargeting campaigns, while the product neither looks good nor solves the core problem.
- His view: staying in your comfort zone is never the solution, and with today's tools there are "absolutely no excuses." Developers can learn marketing from YouTube; marketers can learn enough to build with no-code and low-code tools; designers can learn both.
- If you truly can't move out of your zone, find a co-founder with the same stakes. He warns against outsourcing the parts you're bad at: "it's a trap I can almost guarantee."

## "A good product sells itself" is a myth: you need a go-to-market strategy

- Most SaaS founders are technical, and many have a barrier to marketing. Some are simply too product-centric; others feel entitled, hate being marketed at, and think marketing is beneath them. Telling yourself you don't need marketing is "the one and single perfect way to set yourself up for a huge failure."
- No-code tools (Webflow, Bubble) and libraries (React, Tailwind, Chakra UI) make great products easy to build, so almost everyone can build a high-quality one. A better product is therefore not a competitive advantage.
- What he rarely sees is founders with a clear go-to-market strategy, distribution channels nailed down, and a scale plan. He means an actual long-term strategy, "not just we're gonna launch on Product Hunt."
- His claim: founders with this will win "even if they have a shittier product than you."
- He admits making all these mistakes himself; his first SaaS attempt "failed miserably," and FeedHive was his second.
