## Leaving freelancing for FeedHive and the $50K team mistake

- Simon is a self-taught software engineer. He freelanced for roughly 10 years, starting with simple WordPress and Shopify work and moving on to React and AWS cloud builds.
- He launched his first SaaS product, FeedHive (a social media management tool), in 2021, when "being an engineer that could build stuff was a massive advantage" because AI could not yet code for you.
- He cancelled all his client contracts as soon as FeedHive had about $300 MRR.
- He then went too big too fast. He was so confident in the product that he hired a full team, including Twitter influencers from his network.
- Over 3 months he spent around $50,000 on that team, while FeedHive barely moved and reached about $2,000 MRR. He had to fire everyone.
- He kept building solo for about another year and a half before he broke even on that initial loss.
- He says those 18 months were agonizing, and he kept thinking he would go back to consulting. He credits his savings for getting through; without them it "would have just been a really really big waste of money."
- The lesson he draws from his own story is that he was "completely overconfident" about how long things would take.

## Surviving the Twitter/X API price shock

- When Elon Musk bought Twitter (renamed X), a $42,000/month enterprise-grade API tier was introduced. Simon heard about it only about two days before it took effect.
- Rumors had floated for a month, first around $100/month and then $42,000. Simon dismissed the $42,000 rumor.
- The tools hit were the Twitter-centric ones: Hypefury, Typefully, Tony Dinh's Black Magic and others. Some bigger ones could afford the API because Twitter was their whole product. Black Magic was making about $14-15K/month, far too little to pay for it.
- FeedHive was at about $50K MRR at the time. It could technically have paid, but it would have swallowed nearly all revenue, and it also had a team and ad spend to fund.
- Simon decided not to pay for the API. For a while FeedHive let users enter their own API key to keep access, but this was a poor experience. The $100/month entry tier was not worth it for most users when competitors still offered Twitter.
- Result: FeedHive lost about 30% of its users within days of announcing it. It took about a year and a half to recover.
- He says he is glad he made that call, because FeedHive had already added LinkedIn and Instagram and become a multi-platform tool. Without that diversification, "we would have been in a pickle."
- Today only the occasional user asks for Twitter/X support.
- The same episode also led to Abbase (see below): support was swamped with the same three questions, about Twitter, 95% of the time.

## Building a product portfolio by letting demand pull you

Simon now runs four SaaS products aimed at founders and small businesses: FeedHive, LinkDrip, TinyKiwi and Abbase.

- **LinkDrip** (link shortener plus automation) came from user requests. FeedHive users wanted Bitly or Switchy integrations.
- **TinyKiwi** (a mini Canva-style graphics tool) came from Canva closing its integration option just before FeedHive could add it. Simon messaged Yosef, TinyKiwi's builder, on Twitter to propose integrating it. Yosef did not reply for about 3 months, then said he wanted to sell it, and Simon bought it immediately. TinyKiwi now opens inside FeedHive's composer.
- **Abbase** (AI support tool with live chat, ticketing and AI-assisted replies for human agents) was built to scratch his own itch. It is a separate product, not integrated into the others. Competitors include SiteGPT and Chatbase, which showed him there was clear demand. His one human support person uses it to answer faster.
- Integration example: in FeedHive's composer you can shorten a link with LinkDrip, add UTM tags, and use a custom branded short domain, all without leaving the editor.

His advice on portfolios:
- Start with one product, "then let the demand pull you from there." Expand only if users or audience ask for it.
- Some builders (Pieter Levels, John Rush, Tibo) run portfolios, and he thinks newcomers see this and wrongly assume it is the way to start. He does not agree.
- What he likes about his own path is that each new product extends from his existing users and audience, so he did not start from zero on distribution.

## Pre-selling a lifetime deal to validate and fund a product

How Simon launched LinkDrip:
1. He spent a weekend building a landing page.
2. He posted about it on social and showed a popup inside FeedHive announcing the new product.
3. He sold it as a lifetime deal in pre-sales: back the product early and own it forever.
4. Within about a month he made around $75,000 from pre-sales.
5. He used that money to hire developers on Upwork to build the actual product.

- He calls this "a classical validation by pre-sales": the money arrived before the product existed.
- The existing FeedHive user base was the distribution, so there was no cold start.
- He had also sold FeedHive itself as a lifetime deal on AppSumo years earlier, shortly after launch, so this was not his first lifetime deal.

## FounderStack: bundling four tools into one lifetime deal

- He bundled all four products into one offer, FounderStack (founderstack.pro). It is a lifetime deal for the whole bundle only, and you cannot pick individual tools.
- Pricing: $1,299 is the normal price, and it was discounted to $799 at the time. Subscriptions to each tool are still available separately.
- Two more SaaS products are planned for this year. Anyone who buys the bundle now automatically gets them when they launch, so buyers effectively pre-buy access.
- He acknowledges the objection to lifetime deals: it is dangerous to sell something with ongoing costs for a one-time payment.
- His cost logic: he calculated server cost per user, and at $799 or even more at $1,299 it would take the average user "decades" to become a losing investment.
- He advises against his earlier AppSumo pricing: the cheapest plan sold at $49, and he would not recommend doing that.
- His rule is to package the products so the bundle carries enough value to justify a high price.
- Most buyers do not use all four tools equally; they mostly use one, so cloud cost is not multiplied by four per customer.

## Handling AI costs in a lifetime deal

- AI is the expensive part of a lifetime deal and the one to be careful about.
- His solution: every user gets a monthly allowance of AI credits as part of the deal.
- After the allowance runs out, users bring their own OpenAI API key to keep using AI features.
- This caps his exposure on AI cost while keeping the deal attractive.

## Product quality as a competitive edge in a crowded category

- Simon's positioning against incumbents like Hootsuite, Sprout Social and Buffer: they are "old and dusty tools."
- Agencies, which have employees sitting in the tool for 8 hours a day, especially value FeedHive being fast, snappy and quick to navigate.
- He notes that small page-load delays add up when someone clicks around all day.
- He also says that FeedHive supports many platforms and content types (memes, short video, text) without feeling overwhelming.
