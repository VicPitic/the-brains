## Starting from zero: build a minimum lovable product, not just an MVP

- Simon says the most common mistake in SaaS is treating "build a product, then get users" as a sequence. Founders end up "trying to acquire users for your product instead of trying to build a product for your users."
- Because ad targeting is so good, founders assume someone out there will want exactly their version. Simon says this can work, but most founders get further by building as little as possible and using the first users to shape the product.
- He prefers "minimum lovable product" over "minimum viable product." The product should be early-stage but give a "wow effect" to the first users.
- His reasoning: SaaS products pop up everywhere and "everyone is chasing the users," so early users need a real reason to jump on an unfinished product.
- He says he mostly speaks about things he has done first hand: starting and running small, bootstrapped, self-serve SaaS. He runs FeedHive, LinkDrip and Eightbase, with a team of nine contractors and over $1M combined ARR.

## The four acquisition methods

Simon says any online user acquisition has a channel, usually a cost, and a method. He sorts methods into four:

- **Outgoing (outbound):** the founder reaches out to users via cold DMs, cold email or cold calls.
- **Incoming:** content (blog posts, YouTube, social, speaking) that attracts people who then sign up self-serve.
- **Referrals:** current users bring in new ones, through affiliate programs or a viral loop (for example subtle branding inside the product).
- **Advertisement:** like incoming, but you pay for the placement, via Google or Facebook Ads or by sponsoring creators.

He uses a different method at each of three stages.

## Stage 1: first 5 to 100 users (outgoing plus ambassadors)

- Use the outgoing method on your close network: early colleagues, people you have worked with. Tell them about the product and ask them to join.
- Give the first users a lifetime account for free. If the product has high friction or a high cost to get started, consider paying them to use it.
- Simon disagrees with "never give it away, you're devaluing your product." In his view, at this stage "you are accurately valuing how important these first users are because they are fundamental to the success of your product."
- Put the first users in a small community (Facebook group, WhatsApp, Discord). For FeedHive three years earlier, it was a Twitter chat group with about 20 people.
- Listen and let them shape the product. This gives real feedback instead of guessing, and makes users feel they are part of the project.
- To grow from roughly 10 users to 100, turn early adopters into ambassadors. Give them a voucher or discount to offer friends ("I'm a partner in this project, I can get you in for free or half off"). It is a referral method, not a formal affiliate program.

## Stage 2: up to roughly 1,000 users (incoming content)

- Once the product has been shaped for two to three months and is ready for strangers, switch to the incoming method: blog posts, YouTube videos, social media, and speaking or booths.
- He calls this stage "very long and very hard" and demotivating, but the effort compounds. He estimates allocating a year or even two years of organic content can reach somewhere between 200, 300, 500, maybe the first 1,000 paying users.
- Outbound with a sales team can work, but it is no longer a self-serve SaaS. He says it suits VC-backed companies with money for a sales team, and he stays in the bootstrapped self-serve genre.
- Keep the stage 1 early adopters close, because they stay valuable in later stages.
- Advice for the 10K MRR phase: grind through outreach to the first 5 to 25 users, then show up on social media every day, write blog posts and make videos even through long stretches of low engagement. "Just keep going."

## Stage 3: scaling past roughly 1,000 paying users (ads and affiliates)

- Keep doing stage 2 content. Add two scalable methods: advertisement and affiliate marketing.
- **Ads:** reuse content that performed well in stage 2 as ad creative, and create new ads too. He says this is his preferred method and he has used it for all his products. Ads are one of the highest-cost channels, so he waits until stage 3.
- **Ad math:** use the data from stages 1 and 2 to calculate average lifetime value (using churn). Cost of acquiring a user with ads must be below average lifetime value. If the math works and you are in growth mode, you can be willing to pay close to the full average lifetime value for one new user.
- **Affiliates:** turn current users into affiliate partners using existing affiliate tools. In growth mode, a generous program can work; he has seen tools pay up to 50% of recurring revenue. If you want more automation and less grind, offer less (in-app credits, gift cards, lower payouts), accepting that affiliates will promote less aggressively.
- Stage 3 "can keep going indefinitely" with no limit on new users.
- He cautions not to start affiliates too early, since you probably don't have people yet who want to recommend you for money.

## Learning ads cheaply before you need them

- Ads were a big challenge. He says it is hard to get the creative format right and to understand how the platforms work, and they "burned quite a lot of money" on things that didn't work, especially with FeedHive, his first SaaS.
- His advice: start experimenting with ads during stage 2 without expecting conversions. Set aside a small budget (for example, "for the next 6 months, we're just going to blow this money on learning how to do ads") in a sandbox-style environment.
- Skill at content makes it easier to find ad creatives that perform. By the second SaaS, and now Aibase (the third), ad roll-out has become "significantly easier over time."

## How Simon runs his team and why he stays bootstrapped

- **Delegate tasks, not areas:** he describes himself as "the single point of failure." Contractors (found via Upwork and Fiverr) do graphics, video editing, some writing and development. He sets up the ad campaigns, assembles final YouTube videos, and approves every code PR (he is a developer by background).
- His reasoning is partly financial: task-level contractors are affordable but weaker at planning and strategy for a whole area. He recommends small bootstrapped founders "get their hands dirty" and be in charge of everything, even if they delegate execution. He says other teams do well with independent owners and he doesn't claim his way is the only one.
- **No investors:** he became an entrepreneur "so that I wouldn't have a boss," and investors (who often reach out to successful bootstrappers) would become one. He dislikes giving a board seat for little money: "Now, not only do they have a boss again, they also have a boss they can't fire."
- He says he'd reach big numbers like 10M ARR slowly and steadily, and would likely never reach 100M ARR his way. Founders who want to go very big may need funding.

## Advice for founders at higher scale, and on persistence

- **Keep showing up:** past roughly $100K per month, some founders think they can lean back into a pure strategy role. Simon says this doesn't work: keep appearing on YouTube, podcasts and stages, even if uncomfortable. It also shows the team the founder is still fully involved.
- **Content is underused:** he thinks many SaaS companies still "sleep on" content. Be on podcasts, YouTube and blogs, where people hang out.
- **What he'd tell his 20s self:** stop chasing shiny objects and "easy money overnight" ideas. Pick something you enjoy and can get good at, and keep grinding. Give it "at least two, three years before you start expecting to see actual tangible results," and choose something you like so you don't give up.
