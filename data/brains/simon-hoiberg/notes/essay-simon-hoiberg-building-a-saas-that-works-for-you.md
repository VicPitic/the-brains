## Building reusable systems so one person can run several businesses

- Simon treats every output as a building block. After each YouTube video he strips it apart and asks whether any part can become a reusable asset or a checklist that makes the next one faster. His rule: "every time I put something out there, there needs to be something more to it than just the result."
- He got the habit from software: turn something you built into a modular package (NPM, Gradle) instead of one-off code. He says few developers, even senior ones, do it, and he used it as a freelancer to deliver faster for clients, never reusing code owned by another client's project.
- Result: he works on "one thing" and it feeds the whole ecosystem. Because he did not start with everything at once but built each layer on the previous one, the output looks huge to outsiders.
- Example of the compounding in his YouTube workflow: a library of hundreds of short stock clips (him at a computer, with a mouse), thumbnail starter files in Photoshop, and a checklist-driven pipeline. The first 2-3 videos "took forever".
- He runs his channel on about 10 hours a week, including thumbnail, topic research, scripts, filming and editing, which he still does himself. He cautions that this sits on top of the building blocks and that a beginner should not expect 10 hours.

## Validating a SaaS in a crowded market with a small community

- His first SaaS attempt, Sigmetic (GitHub-integrated performance tracking for software teams, inspired by sales leaderboards), failed. His lesson: he tried to innovate and imported thinking from his sales background that did not fit how engineers work.
- For FeedHive (social media scheduling, started a bit over two years before this 2023 interview) he did the opposite: pick a market that is already established and pre-validated. He listed email marketing, social media management and project management as candidates, noting that project management still produced new winners (Monday, ClickUp) despite old incumbents.
- His reasoning: if the market is large enough, a small slice of users will prefer your tool because of small differences. He calls it risky but workable. LinkDrip (link management with automation) followed the same logic: competitors were adding simple link shorteners to their tools, so he went deeper into Zapier/Airtable-style customizable automation.
- Validation steps for FeedHive:
  - After about 6 months of building an audience on Twitter, he asked people in closed chat groups why they were not scheduling their tweets.
  - Complaints: Buffer and HootSuite felt old and unmaintained; Hypefury had excellent UI/UX but did not match what they wanted. One concrete gap: a calendar view for the queue instead of a list queue.
  - He formed a Twitter group chat of about 30 of those users and built "in secret" for around two months, talking to them daily with questions like "how should this be?".
  - He treated the 30 as representing a small customer segment inside a bigger market, not guessing.

## Getting into private communities through audience, not outreach

- Asked how he got into closed groups, he says there was no strategy: he had about 30,000 followers on Twitter by the time he started FeedHive, after about six months of being active.
- Other creators invited him into small Twitter groups because his content resonated. He only started a group himself for the FeedHive validation.
- His takeaway: first be active online and build an audience; community access follows. Arvid's phrasing, which Simon confirms: build "opportunity surface" by showing up.

## Lifetime deals: when they work and how to run them

- He sold an early-access lifetime deal for LinkDrip, framed explicitly as part of validating interest before the product was fully built (a quick proof of concept only). He notes "nothing beats having people pick up their credit card and extra pay" as validation, and that it brings cash up front.
- Do it on a limited basis; he does not see lifetime deals as a way to build a long-term sustainable business, especially with ongoing server and support costs.
- Before launching, "make that Excel sheet" and check the finances. Hosting can be engineered to be very cheap and support can be made self-serve. Lifetime users also churn. Avoid a lifetime deal if each user carries high variable cost, e.g. AI/OpenAI usage, which can get expensive fast.
- Biggest problem in his view is reputation: a lifetime deal signals financial trouble. So communicate clearly why it is limited and why you are doing it, otherwise "people start talking".
- His definition of "lifetime": access for as long as the product exists.

## Letting customers leave: export and portability as a trust signal

- LinkDrip lets users change a link's destination instantly, so printed links and QR codes stay alive. It can also A/B test (a dice roll between destinations) or change the destination after a set number of clicks or a certain time.
- He plans (not fully in place at the time) JSON and CSV export of links so customers can move to another redirect service. He argues knowing your data is yours can be a deciding factor for signing up.
- Caveat: the link's domain is tied to the service, so for print he recommends QR codes, which can be moved.
- Arvid adds the framing, which Simon agrees with: giving customers a way out builds trust compared with locking them in.

## Building in public as an ecosystem, not a funnel

- Simon rejects classic funnel thinking where every post pushes people down one path to one product. He sees an "ecosystem" or "playground": hours of free content (YouTube, Twitter), plus products they may stumble on: FeedHive, LinkDrip, a newly acquired small SaaS called Tiny QE, info products, and the YouTube channel itself.
- Because of this he is relaxed about sharing numbers that might look unflattering. People who drop off one product may find another, so no audience member is "wasted". He treats the audience as reusable building blocks too.
- He is more cautious for founders who are all-in on one brand-owned product, because sharing there can hurt the single business.
- Safe-to-fail framing: he sits between "all-in" and "tiny small bets". He asks of every new bet "does this fit into the web?" If LinkDrip failed, it would still yield YouTube content and cross-pollinate other products. Failures are content too.
- He also plans for the worst case: if his products went down, he wants an audience he can invite into a new branch of the ecosystem.

## Running a YouTube channel that teaches rather than converts

- He admits the channel works as a marketing channel and generates a significant number of leads for FeedHive, LinkDrip and Tiny QE. But the purpose when making content is to empower people intimidated by SaaS: no VC money, no investors needed, you can build a micro SaaS from home alongside a job.
- He believes channels where every video has an ulterior sign-up motive have slower growth; he avoids heavy funneling.
- Content rule: include things people cannot find elsewhere, grounded in his own experience, rather than repackaging information from the internet. Topics are "100% lesson-driven" from what he is doing, so he never runs out of ideas.
- Production: he writes scripts for five videos at a time, shoots in one weekend (about 10-12 videos' worth of footage), and schedules almost three months ahead. He has killed already-produced videos when he learned something that made the advice outdated.

## Avoiding burnout: joy-driven work, async teams, an empty calendar

- His philosophy is "joy driven": he gamifies life around intrinsic motivation, not discipline, saying motivation can be a strong fuel for handling hard situations. He thinks people burn out from being forced to do work whose benefit is unclear, not from working a lot.
- His calendar is nearly empty: he works in his own flow and picks tasks he is excited about that day, keeping tasks as independent as possible.
- Team of five, with a culture of asynchronous written work and no meetings. He avoids interrupting team members, since "rarely" is anything too important to write in Slack.
- When something goes wrong he can drop everything for firefighting because nothing is scheduled. FeedHive depends on third-party social APIs that fail often, producing support tickets from frustrated users. He steps in himself to protect the support team, since they did not sign up for crisis management and he did.
- Each time he is forced to push through something he dislikes, he reflects and designs a process to prevent it recurring, for himself and for his team.
