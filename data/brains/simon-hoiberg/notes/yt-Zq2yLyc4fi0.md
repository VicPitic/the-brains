## Why most lifetime-deal objections are wrong: Simon's case from $160K in sales

- Simon's SaaS products had been stuck for almost two years, with no revenue growth. New users kept getting offset by high acquisition cost, expensive ad placements and churn.
- A few months before this video he ran a lifetime deal (LTD), the move other founders told him not to make. It lifted his revenue by more than 56% so far.
- Common founder objections he lists: it's unsustainable (one payment against infinite server and support cost), the audience is demanding and entitled, and it will cannibalize current subscribers.
- The user-side objection is trust: how does a buyer know the founder won't take the money and shut down?
- His claim: three things make these concerns go away: a high price, tight cost control, and a strong public reputation.

## Evidence base: Founder's Stack, FeedHive on AppSumo and LinkDrip pre-sales

- Founder's Stack launched about two months before the video. It is a bundled lifetime offer for four tools (social media, AI support, link tracking, graphics design), built by his team over four years. It also includes two new SaaS products not yet launched, due later that year.
- FeedHive ran a two-month campaign on AppSumo in 2022 and ranked among the top five best-selling products in that period. It generated more than $300,000 and onboarded more than 5,000 lifetime users.
- Later in 2022 he pre-sold lifetime deals to early adopters of LinkDrip before launch. Many of those users are still active three years later.
- This gave him real data on how lifetime users behave and what the economics look like.

## Lifetime users do churn

- Myth: lifetime users never churn because there is no subscription to cancel.
- Reality: they churn for the same reasons subscribers do:
  - They go out of business.
  - They outgrow the tool and need something bigger.
  - They outsource the work to an agency with its own tool chain.
  - They move to a shiny new tool that solves the problem differently.
- Instant churners: some buyers (his "Jonathan" example) buy as an investment for a future business, never redeem and never become users. In the data they buy and leave immediately.
- Once these users are counted, the average lifetime user churns before the average subscriber. In his cohort comparison, lifetime users who do activate stick around slightly longer, but the instant churners flip the average.
- Takeaway: you are not stuck with all your lifetime users forever, so the job is to pick a price high enough that the average one never becomes a losing investment before churning.

## Rule 1: Set a high price point (Founder's Stack at $799)

- Founder's Stack costs $799 for all four tools. He calls it the sweet spot: still a great deal for buyers, and safe given current cost per user.
- Results: about 230 buyers and more than $160,000 in revenue.
- Break-even horizon: given his cost per user, it would take roughly 37 years before this group of buyers starts costing him money.
- His point: on paper, a one-time charge against ongoing costs eventually goes negative, but most founders wildly exaggerate how near that horizon is. In 37 years, buyers won't be using the tool and "it's at least not going to be my problem by that time."
- The long runway comes from two things: the high price and low cost.
- Warning example: a $39 lifetime deal on a product that uses AI heavily has a high cost per user and is a quick route to bankruptcy. This has happened many times.

## Rule 2: Keep costs tight with simple, self-serve, low-touch products

- Sell simple but specialized tools that are fully self-serve with very low support needs.
- Avoid demo calls, presentations, sales meetings, manual onboarding, designated customer success agents and account managers. "It's way too expensive."
- Invest heavily in the onboarding experience and have many guides, tutorials and videos so users can help themselves.
- Trouble starts when you price too low and the product costs too much to run. When planning, use safe, conservative cost estimates.

## Cannibalization: the lifetime deal barely hurt subscribers

- Subscribers who left: 10 cancelled their subscription and replaced it with Founder's Stack. That is only about 4.5% of Founder's Stack buyers.
- Subscribers gained: at least 15 new subscribers found him through the Founder's Stack website, so the net is +5 subscribers.
- His theory, which he says he can't prove conclusively: the high price creates distance from the monthly plan. Many users on the $29/month FeedHive plan only need that one tool, so $799 for four tools isn't compelling, even though one is a one-time payment and the other recurring.
- The new subscribers may be people trying the tools for a few months before buying the bundle, or people who heard about the tools through the promotion but only need one.
- Takeaway: if the price distance is big enough, you probably don't need to worry that your whole subscriber base will jump to the lifetime deal.

## Rule 3: Build trust by being the public face of the company

- Deal sites like AppSumo, PitchGround and DealMirror made it tempting for bad actors to run an "LTD pump and dump": list the tool, hype the roadmap with insane promises, take the cash, then shut down about 3 months later citing bankruptcy. Well-meaning founders who overreach also hurt trust.
- As a result many LTD buyers are skeptical.
- His fix: build a strong reputation, with the founder as the public face of the company:
  - Be on social media and YouTube.
  - Show up in people's emails.
  - Demonstrate responsibility and that you will give users what you promised.
- If you hide behind the company, are invisible or unreachable, selling this way is hard if not impossible.
- Closing advice: whether lifetime deals or subscriptions suit your business is your call, but don't reject the idea blindly. Make sure the three things are covered: high price, tight costs, strong reputation.
