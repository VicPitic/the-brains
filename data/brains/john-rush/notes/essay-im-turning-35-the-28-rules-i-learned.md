## Validate first, sell features before building, and chase users not investors

- John says he "wasted a decade building stuff nobody needed". He thought incubators and VCs counted as validation. They did not.
- He sells features before building them. He runs DMs with 10-20 users every day to talk through feature ideas, sees what resonates, and builds only those.
- Without followers, he suggests asking on HN, Reddit, or by searching X for relevant posts and replying with the question. People answer if the question is easy to understand.
- He says to kill your ego: "I must want what the user wants, not what I want. My taste isn't important."
- Chase users, not investors. Investor interest in him is at its highest now, when he is least interested in VCs.
- He expects to fail. Almost every startup fails, so he advises failing faster to reach the second and third iteration, and continuing until you find what works or find that nothing does.
- Drop a bad project within 1 year. Either the idea is too wrong to pivot, or the team is good individually but can't work as a team.

## Pricing: no free tier, and don't be too cheap

- No free tier. John launched tools with free tiers and got sign-ups, but few converted. He treated free sign-ups as KPIs, bragged about sign-ups and visitors, and even raised VC money on those stats. He still never reached PMF, because his main feedback came from free users and the product became "a perfect free product".
- Free and paid users often need different products. He now goes "paid only" until the product is validated, and things went much better.
- Don't price lowest. He used to check all competitors and set the lowest price, thinking it was a key advantage. He says the $5 and $50 audiences are totally different.
  - $5 customers: "pain in the ass, never happy, never recommend you to a friend, leave in 4 months."
  - $50 customers: "polite, give genuine feedback, happy, share with friends, become your big fan if you solve their request."

## Distribution: global market, SEO, daily posting, and landing pages

- Chase the global market from day 1. Pick a small niche in a global market rather than a large niche in a local one.
- Do SEO from day 2, as early as possible. He ignored it for 14 years and calls it his biggest regret.
- Post on Twitter/X daily. He started in March 2023 and it is his primary source of new connections and growth. He wishes he had started earlier.
- Write every single day. He restarted writing on X about 10 months before the essay and loves the process and the feedback. He has a spelling and letter-order issue and uses Grammarly to correct it.
- The landing page is the least important thing early on. Pick a simple template, edit it in a no-code website builder in under an hour, and move on. "Your first 100 sales happen outside of the website."

## Team and hiring before PMF

- Never hire managers before PMF. Hire only doers. Many people know how to manage, few can "get sh*t done barehand".
- Hire only full-stack devs. A team of developers is the least productive setup for an early-stage product. One full-stack dev builds the whole product.
- Outsource nothing until PMF. Early on, everything needs to be done in a slightly different, more creative way that fits the vision. Outsourced people get no love for the product, treat it as another assignment, and focus on their own promotion or next job.
- Hire only people you would want to hug. His Danish cofounder told him this in 2015. If you don't want to hug someone, you dislike them on a "chemical/animal level", and conflict and a breakup will follow. A startup can take up to 10 years, so build it with people you have that connection with.
- Scrum is a scam. A team that must be nagged every morning "as if they were kindergarten children" will fail. His good work came from grownups who managed themselves, syncing on goals and plans over chat.

## Bootstrap, use boilerplates, and build B2B

- Bootstrap. He raised money more than 10 times (preseed, seed, Series A). Each raise was a 3-9 month project with weekly meetings and lots of distraction. He could have bootstrapped but didn't know it was an option.
- Use boilerplates. He wasted years of dev time and millions of VC money building basic things: another sidebar, another dashboard, payment integration. Pride made him insist on 100% original code from scratch.
- Build B2B, not consumer apps. Consumer apps are like a lottery, with about 0.00001% making it big, and even with many users there is a monetization challenge. He spent 4 years on consumer apps and regrets it.
- Build for an audience you love. He found he had no love for corporates, consumers, or developers as audiences. He loves indie founders because they are risk-takers and partly kids at heart. Switching his focus to indie makers raised his joy "100x".

## Avoid corporates, conferences, and hype

- Don't work or partner with corporates. They promise millions of users, but "every single time none of this happens". You talk to regular employees who waste your time, destroy focus, shift priorities, and bring no users or money.
- Tech conferences are a waste of time. They cost money, energy, and time, and you rarely meet anyone useful. Most attendees are loyal corporate employees sent as a perk, with very few fellow makers.
- Don't get distracted by hype. He lost 1.5 years to crypto and met scammers and thieves, including close friends who turned into thieves because it was so common in that space. He wishes he had stayed on his mission.

## Where to put money, and time horizon

- Invest in your own startups and in friends, not crypto, stock market, or property.
  - He calculated that investing all his money in his friends' startups would have meant about 70 investments, and 3 became unicorns. Even 1 would have made the bank.
  - Since 2022 he has put all his money into his products, friends, and network.
  - Without startup friends, invest in yourself. Walk away from get-rich-quick options.
- Expect it to take a decade. At 20 he thought it took a few years, so he kept postponing family and kids until after an exit. He wishes he had married and had kids earlier.
- Spend time with family and friends. He missed the weddings of all his best friends and family, thinking the world would end if he didn't ship on time. He now says spending 10% of his time on family and friends would have had practically no negative impact on his startups.
