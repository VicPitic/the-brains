## Where John sees startup ideas now: growth and marketing tools for solo builders

- John sees two trends. More "solopreneurs" build on the side (a corporate job plus weekends and evenings), and others move to cost-effective countries and spend a year or two building in public. He says this movement was very small about two years ago and is at least doubling every year.
- Access to building is the best ever. You don't need to fundraise, find a co-founder or hire a team. You code it yourself, or use no-code.
- His conclusion: "it's easy to build stuff but it's not easy to grow." 99% of makers can build, but only a few can grow, sell and bring in customers.
- So the low-hanging fruit is tools and services around growth and marketing. His own role is to build tools that make this crowd more powerful with fewer resources.

## Micro-tools: start from how you would sell it

- John's way to find ideas is to start with how you would sell it. Don't build first; see what people are asking for.
- Example from his own path: people kept asking how to get SEO backlinks, how to grow, how to raise domain rating. Others answer with a seven-step list. Busy makers (many with full-time jobs) want to save time.
- He wraps that list of actions into a tool that does one thing. He calls these micro-tools.
- The goal is for people to recommend his tool under those posts: "I did that using this tool." A tool that does one thing is easy to remember and recommend. A tool that does many things is hard to remember.

## Idea: "startup drafts" (validated ideas with a waitlist and prepayments)

- Problem: more people want to build but don't know what to build. They pick random ideas and fail. John says most people fail right at the start, with no traction and "not even one sign up."
- Idea: someone comes up with ideas, validates them with users, collects a waitlist and maybe prepayments, then sells the "startup draft" to a builder. Much of the risk is gone by then.
- Pricing: people would pay up to $1,000, and with word of mouth maybe $2K, $3K or $5K, to skip the first period.
- Pipeline, with his example numbers:
  1. Generate about 100 ideas through keyword research and hot-topic research on Reddit and Twitter.
  2. Technical validation: check organic traffic, potential, and whether the .com domain is free. About 30 survive.
  3. Human validation: build a waitlist and promote it with ads or paid influencers. About 10 survive.
  4. Ask waitlist members to prepay (his example is a 70% discount). About 3 survive.
  5. Sell each survivor with its audience, prepayments, idea and landing page, for $2,000-$3,000 each (about $6,000 for the ones he used as an example).
- Cost: roughly $1,000-$2,000 to run, so margins are good. Every step can be automated: domains and ads via APIs, landing pages via builders with APIs, ideas via AI and scraping. He says such a system could run 1,000 ideas a month and yield about 30 winners.
- Why it beats marketplaces: on startup marketplaces you get no users, revenue or validation for the same price. Building is the easiest part of a startup. With users, prepayments and validation, he says it is worth at least $10K.

## Buying influencer posts cheaply

- John prefers paying influencers over ads because it's cheaper. He says you can pay $300 and get 100K views on a post on Twitter now.
- Prices have fallen. Two years ago he saw at least $1K for about 30,000 guaranteed views. He kept asking the same people every few months and prices dropped as more people entered and competition grew. Some deals are now $100 because he buys often.
- How he finds them: direct messages on Twitter and LinkedIn. They reach out to him more often than he reaches out to them.
- Negotiation:
  - They start high (about $700-$800). Bargain down to a few hundred.
  - Even if they say no, they often come back about four weeks later with an empty one-week slot, because $200 beats nothing.
  - Keep many influencers in your pipeline so a slot is always open.
  - After 5-10 purchases, agree on a low fixed price.
- New experiment: pay influencers a fixed amount every month, with a 12-month commitment that he won't cancel without notice. It's like Nike's deals with football stars, but at micro or nano scale. He gets a set amount of activity on their account (a post, part of a listicle, a reply). The price goes very low for him. The risk is that they lose reach, but he hasn't seen an influencer fully disappear.
- He plans a tool around this, which people on Twitter call "reinventing AdSense for micro startups."
- He thinks the cheap-attention window won't last. Influencer supply is growing nearly exponentially. Using social media is also hurting his building time, and he expects many people to turn into full-time influencers. He compares it to Spotify, where the cost of music dropped toward zero. Early users of influencers will still gain momentum over those who don't.

## Why directories work

- John has been passionate about directories for years. Two years ago his team called them obsolete ("cool in 2017"). He disagreed but only started last year.
- His logic: as quantity grows, the share of quality drops, so choosing gets harder. There are hundreds of CRM tools, tomorrow thousands, and old ones don't get deleted. People want places where selection, categorization or enrichment has been done for them.
- People trust curators who show real experience in a field, and he sees more people starting to charge for good directories. He doesn't charge for his own. His example is Pat's Starter Story, which he calls a directory of startup stories, with Pat picking the best founders.
- A directory doesn't have to be a boring list of items. It can be a list of anything. You can monetize both sides: people who want access and those who want to be listed.
- AI angle: AI needs to find the best items to recommend. Reddit works partly as a directory (reputation, votes, an ordered list). He expects the best directories to be picked by AI for search results, which would give a directory huge power and make people pay to be in it.
- Building a directory takes little tech and a lot of time on content quality and promotion. He says it needs time rather than money.

## Directory idea 1: localize a working directory into another language

- Many directories exist in English and very few in local languages. Example: it's hard to find a directory of SaaS tools made by French people in France.
- Method:
  1. Find a directory that works well in the US.
  2. Search for the same thing in another language (e.g. French) on Google.
  3. If nothing good ranks on top, build it.
- Reputation is solvable over time: learn the topic and the tools, make podcasts with founders of the tools, and so on.
- He argues non-English speakers are not "not in tech." Many prefer their own language, build in it, have large markets, and go international later.

## Directory idea 2: pick the niche by keyword data, not intuition

- Method:
  1. Use Google's free Ads Keyword Planner to search keywords around random topics.
  2. Find a keyword with high traffic.
  3. Search it on Google. If no directory is on page one (only blog posts), build a directory around it.
  4. Google likes directories, and he says you eventually reach the top.
- He uses no personal judgment: "my intuition never works." The directories he had high hopes for failed and the ones he didn't succeeded. His theory is that if he likes an idea, many others do too, so it gets built by everyone.
- Keyword ideas come from ChatGPT. He tells it which products he has (he launched directories to drive traffic to his dev tools such as Dev Hunt and Flat UI) and which audience he wants, asks for 20 ideas, tests all 20, and found one great one.
- Run about 10 ideas because it's cheap and fast. Drop seven and push harder on three.
