## Why John avoids consumer apps and prefers B2B

- John lists "doing consumer apps" as his first regret from building 30 startups over 20 years (VC-backed, bootstrapped, apps, SaaS, B2B, B2C).
- He says the failure rate for consumer apps is 100x the B2B rate, "nearly a lottery".
- His reasoning: in B2B you can build a lifestyle business making 20k a month. Consumer is usually "either huge or nothing", because you give the product away free to most users and only monetize properly once you reach millions of users. Most never reach millions, so most fail.

## Why he regrets raising VC money and chasing investors

- When his first round closed he "felt like Marc Zuckerberg", with journalists interviewing the team. Most of those startups later failed "by being funded too early".
- Fundraising ate about 40% of his time on every startup. He usually succeeded at raising, but questions the cost.
- Now he does no investor outreach (none in 2 years), yet VCs come to him because he has good traction and they search for such projects daily.
- Takeaway: build traction first and let investors come to you.

## Hiring and team structure: stay small, hire doers

- Founders should do most of the work until product-market fit (PMF). Employees and contractors won't have enough love and passion for the project.
- Large teams used to be a badge of growth; John no longer sees them that way.
- Don't hire managers: he has "yet to see any useful manager in a startup". Hire only doers.
- Don't hire teams of specialized developers. He says nothing is less efficient for a startup. One fullstack dev now does 5x more progress than a team of 12 did before. "Avoid 'teams' at all costs."
- Hire only people you "wanna hug". A Danish cofounder told him in 2015 that if you don't want to hug someone, you dislike them on a chemical/animal level.
- Scrum is a scam in his view. Teams that need nagging every morning "like kindergarten" children fail. His good work came from grown-ups who manage themselves and sync on goals and plans over chat.
- Don't outsource development or marketing. Vendors were good, but outcomes weren't, because startups are so hard that an outsider is very unlikely to do a good job.

## Marketing regrets: SEO, content and social media

- **SEO:** nobody in his network did it. They thought it was too late and postponed it forever.
- **Content marketing:** he never took blogging seriously and calls that a big mistake.
- **Social media:** his biggest regret. He started on Twitter only a year before writing (May 2024) and reached 20k followers. He wonders whether starting 20 years earlier could have meant 1M followers, and thinks it could.
- Takeaway: start SEO, blogging and social posting from day one, as compounding channels.

## Validation, free tiers and what to build

- **Skip validation = mistake.** He used to assume what the audience needed. It almost never turned out true. His best projects were ones he expected to fail, and the failed ones had his highest hopes at the start.
- **No free tier in B2B.** Free users were the wrong crowd. Their feedback served the rest of the "free" crowd, and paying users turned out to have different needs.
- Adding a free tier later after no sales was also a mistake. His rule: "If nobody wants to pay for my product, I have to fix it or find another audience for it."
- **Use boilerplates.** His teams spent the first 3 months coding auth, admin panel, CRUDs and similar basics, a huge waste. Once he started using boilerplates, speed went up 10x.

## Focus traps: partners, shiny objects, dead projects, conferences

- **Corporate partnerships:** he partnered with large billion-dollar corporations many times. They promised millions of users but wasted his time, shifted priorities, destroyed focus, forced heavy spending on security and compliance, and brought in no users or money.
- **Shiny objects:** he fell for crypto hype, got "super rich, then lost it all", and lost years.
- **Holding on too long:** he kept believing in projects after years of no traction, hoping something magical would happen. It was a waste of time.
- **Tech conferences:** a total waste of time. Most attendees are employees sent as a perk for loyalty to their corporation.

## Rest and quality over quantity

- He worked too much, took no holidays and spent little time with family and friends. This was destructive to his creativity.
- Once he started taking time off, he became far more creative.
- His closing principle: "Quality > Quantity."
- He says he got about 10% of things right across his startups, and the essay focuses on the 90% he regrets.
