## Do everything quick and not perfect: the 10/90 rule

- John has been building since 2009. He says it got easy after about 10 years.
- His key skill is finding the "10 to 90" split: 10% of the action gives 90% of the outcome.
- He applies it to every task, even writing an email: "I do everything quick and not perfect."
- The compound effect is that he ships in days, not weeks or months.
- He found that this is easy to say and hard to do on every task. So he turned the habit into specialized AI tools, one per task, that other people can plug into their own process.

## Agents beat copilots

- John says most people use AI as a copilot. He thinks that is a good use but won't make a founder "crazy 100x". Used as an agent, it "works when you sleep".
- Copilots are like any other tool: users have to learn how to use them. AI raises expectations, so one mistake and people think "I do it myself next time". John says most copilots are abandoned quickly.
- An agent is a black box. You give it a task, it does everything without interference, and it asks you only when it lacks data or answers. You wake up and the work is done, which keeps impressing people so they use it more.
- He compares a good agent to a good employee or an obedient child.
- A year earlier he predicted agents for everything within six months. Twelve months later he says the only agents on the market are, probably, ones he built. His agents are viral and people pay for them.

## Keep an agent to one button

- John says the hardest part of building an agent is resisting the urge to add buttons, form inputs and interfaces.
- The only button an agent needs is "turn on".
- He says other agent builders started well, then added many buttons and drifted back to a regular UI. He has kept his agents to one button so far.
- Users will scream for buttons because they have never used a tool that works on autopilot. John says "never listen to the users" on this. His intuition is that once they see the value over days, weeks and months, they stop asking.

## Why people don't adopt AI, and how to fix it with demos

- John says people talk about AI far more than they use it, roughly 99 to 1. They repost, ask and sign up, but don't use it, because changing habits is much harder than people think, even for a 100x productivity boost.
- On Unicorn Platform (AI website builder), most users did not use the AI much at first.
- Usage jumped after he built something that went viral and recorded how he did it, including entering the prompt and the AI producing the whole page. People who had seen AI before then started using it.
- His conclusion: people who have tried AI keep using it, while people who never tried hesitate until they see something viral that shows exactly how to use it. "Show, don't tell."
- He says real AI adoption is still far off.

## Mars X: reuse micro-apps, use AI to connect and modify them

- Mars X is a dev platform where you build from micro-apps. You never reinvent existing pieces; you only invent the new part or connect pieces in a new way. He says this saves about 99% of the time.
- Most things already exist, but people rebuild them because they can't discover them.
- Inside Mars X, AI is used for discovery, for modifying pre-built micro-apps (his analogy: you need a 30-inch wheel, not the existing 20-inch one) and for connecting them.
- His pitch is "code plus no code plus AI".

## How John gets launches seen

- He is very active on Twitter, so he can push something out and get an initial reaction fast.
- He also posts on other platforms where he has followers. He says his audience is smaller than most, but it reacts quickly, and good products then spread.
- He credits virality to a mix of this audience, the one-button agent design and demos.

## Expect nothing from launches, enjoy the process

- John says most products he launches "will die and fade into oblivion". About 95% of the code he has written never saw the light of day, especially in his first years as a maker.
- He advises people to expect nothing from launches. His analogy is a parent: you hope your kids do well but aren't sad if they don't. He is happy during the process and very happy when something works, because his expectations are zero.
- Money as the reason to build is, in his view, "the best way to fail". He stays motivated by problems: seeing something that can be improved and isn't yet. He says he has to resist starting a new project every day.

## How to find what to build

- Entrepreneurship in tech is about solving problems: see something inefficient, add code and AI, make it more efficient.
- He tells young people who ask for an idea to look around rather than invent from inside. Younger founders bring ideas "from totally inside" and he pushes back.
- Get bothered by real problems in daily life. He says he is bothered by about a hundred things a day, so if you aren't bothered by any, you aren't looking around.
- His closing advice: "Build stuff fast."
