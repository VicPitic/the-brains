## Funding, investors and partners: why John regrets chasing them

- **Raising VC money too early.** It shifted focus from "happy users" to headcount, media coverage, conferences, LOIs, partnerships, networking and the next funding round.
- **Chasing investors.** For every startup he spent 40% of his time fundraising. He succeeded in most cases, but questions the cost.
- **Inbound beats outreach.** He has done no investor outreach in 2 years, yet VCs come to him because he has good traction and they search for such projects daily. His rule: don't chase VCs, make users happy and VCs will find you.
- **Betting on partners.** He partnered with large billion-dollar corporates across several startups. They promised millions of users but wasted his time, destroyed focus, shifted priorities, forced spending on security and compliance, and brought no users or money.

## Hiring and team structure: stay founder-led and small

- **Hiring too early.** He compares employees and contractors to "an average nanny for your kid": they do the bare minimum and take no risk, so there is no innovation. Only founders have the incentive to take risks, so founders should do all the work until they have traction.
- **Hiring managers.** He has never seen a useful manager in a startup. Managers may suit corporations, but a startup should hire only doers.
- **Specialized developers.** A team of frontend, backend, db, devops, design and QA specialists is very inefficient for a startup. Today one fullstack dev makes 5x more progress than a team of 12 did before. He says to avoid "teams" at all cost until at least $30k MRR.
- **Scrum.** He calls it a scam. Teams that need daily nagging "as if they were children in kindergarten" fail. His good work came with grownups who managed themselves and synced on goals and plans over chat.
- **Outsourcing development and marketing.** The vendors were good but the outcomes weren't. For outsiders a startup is just another assignment, their goal is to look professional, and they never disagree with your bad ideas.
- **The "hug" test.** His cofounder, an old Danish man, told him in 2015: if you don't want to hug the person, you dislike them on a chemical/animal level. Every time John ignored this rule he paid a high price later.

## Marketing and distribution regrets: SEO, content and social

- **Ignoring SEO.** No one in his network did SEO. They all treated it as something for later and postponed it forever. Paid ads were easy and predictable, and having too much money in the bank spoiled them.
- **Ignoring content marketing.** He thought blogging was a full-time job, but one hour a day is enough to do it well.
- **Social media is his biggest regret.** He started on X only 2 years before writing and is nearing 100k followers. He wonders whether starting 20 years earlier would have meant 1M followers by now, and thinks it would.

## Validation, product bets and when to quit

- **Consumer apps.** He regrets doing them: failure rates are about 100x those of B2B, "nearly a lottery."
- **Skipping idea validation.** He assumed what the audience needed, and it almost never turned out true. His best projects were ones he expected to fail, and the failures started with his highest hopes.
- **Holding on to a bad project too long.** He kept believing after years of no traction, expecting something magic to happen. It was a waste of time, money and prime years.
- **Shiny objects.** He fell for crypto hype, got very rich, then lost it all. It cost years and nearly depressed him.

## Pricing and building speed: start paid, don't code from scratch

- **Free tier in B2B.** Free projects attract the wrong crowd, give irrelevant feedback and pull the project off course. Freemium is okay at some later point, but he says to always start paid, with a 30-day refund, no questions asked.
- **Coding from scratch.** His team spent the first 3 months building basics like auth, admin panel and CRUDs. Once he used boilerplates, speed went up 10x.
- **Own IDE.** He later built his own AI coding IDE optimized for code reuse, calling it the best decision of his life.

## Time, events and personal sustainability

- **Tech conferences.** He calls them a total waste of time. Attendees are mostly loyal corporate employees sent as a perk, and decision makers are on stage, so you never meet them as an attendee.
- **Overworking.** He worked too much, took no holidays and missed weddings and birthdays. This hurt his creativity. Once he took time off for other active things, he became far more creative: "Quality > Quantity."
