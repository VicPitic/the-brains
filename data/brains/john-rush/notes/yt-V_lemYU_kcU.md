## How John builds startups: validate by hand, then split 50/50 with a co-maker

- John built 26+ products in about three years, serving almost a million users. Unicorn Platform has 600,000 users; the other products combined have about 400,000.
- He spent 10 years building VC-backed startups and concluded he doesn't like it. In 2022 he switched to self-funded, bootstrapped startups: "I want to please the user not the investors."
- His process:
  1. Find an idea from his own work or frustration.
  2. Share it on social media and see whether people resonate.
  3. Build a very simple MVP himself with no-code or vibe-coding tools. Sometimes he builds nothing and does the job by hand: "I'm the tool."
  4. Sell it to about 10 people to check they will pay.
  5. Deliver the service and watch whether they like it, want to keep paying and bring friends.
  6. If so, find a builder (usually on Twitter or in his friend circle) and offer a 50/50 split as co-maker. John does product and distribution; the co-maker does coding and support.
- Most products are John plus one person. They hire no one until at least 50k MRR.
- Neither John nor the co-maker takes a salary. They split revenue or profit, and the only real cost is AI usage, which appears once there are users and revenue.
- He says he has no system for idea generation: ideas come randomly out of his own frustration, he fixes the problem for himself, and he launches when he's happy with it.
- Running many products removes risk. If OpenAI ships something that kills two of his startups, he doesn't mind because the others survive. He says he reached financial freedom "a long time ago" because costs are low.
- He sees more founders heading toward owning many small and mid-sized tools.
- Without VCs or employees, he says he can follow his intuition: "my best ideas and best products. I couldn't explain them at the start."

## Building in public: why it works and which platform to start on

- Building in public means sharing everything (good, bad, the process) before you even have an idea. John compares it to a reality show.
- It became popular 2-3 years ago because customer acquisition costs got so high that attention is expensive to buy with ads. Social media is where the attention is.
- By launch you already have an audience waiting. Followers are loyal and forgiving because they watched you build, and the marketing is a byproduct of sharing the process, with no extra campaign needed.
- He started in 2023 with SEO Bot, which grew popular, then repeated the model for every later product. His latest, Tiny Ads (a small ad network), crossed $10k in revenue in 50 days, also built in public.
- He argues introverts do better. Extroverts come across as salespeople who oversell, while an authentic, clunky, shy builder inspires other builders. "People don't want to see perfect stuff anymore."
- Start on X, not Reddit or YouTube. Anonymous commenters there are harsh, and the build-in-public community on X is supportive because everyone is in the same boat. Go to Reddit and YouTube only when you're big. For the same content he gets very few bad comments on X.
- LinkedIn has copied X's algorithm, so it is now interesting and rewards entertaining content. Two years ago he had to repurpose content for LinkedIn; now the same post works on both.
- Rule: focus on one platform until about 4-5k followers, then expand to all platforms. He set 4k because the smallest profile among the 20 most popular people in his niche had 4k followers at the time. He thinks it's around 10k now.
- Why one platform first: growth needs engagement as well as content. Post, reply to your own replies, then reply under other people's posts. "You have to create this social currency where you give first and then you get back."
- He has answered every DM and reply he has received, about 50,000 answers, and says he will keep doing it.

## Directories as a first product, and his AI growth stack

- He recommends starting with a simple product like a directory before anything ambitious: "Don't start to building Apple right away." Small steps give frequent small wins, and he says people enjoy the process more than the outcome.
- He has built at least 35 directories. They take roughly one evening to launch, then improve over time with more items and information.
- Build a directory on a topic you know better than anyone, such as motorcycles. Group items, add filters, and make them easy to search so a less expert person can find what they need. Niche content spreads quickly within its niche.
- His own AI-tools directory works because he tries AI tools daily and can rank them well among many similar tools.
- A directory also builds an audience, because it shows you understand the topic and gives people visible results rather than claims.
- His stack for directories and growth:
  - **Build:** Unicorn Platform (AI website builder). Framer and Webflow also have AI now. You can skip hiring designers and do the MVP yourself.
  - **Distribution:** Listing Bot, a web agent that finds forums and directories where a product fits, registers and submits the product. Web agents control a browser (forms, bookings, purchases) and are slower than a human but good for repetitive work.
  - **Content:** SEO Bot generates blog articles on autopilot from a URL and sends them to the CMS. Cheaper alternative: ask ChatGPT or Grok for articles and paste them into the CMS yourself.
  - **Data:** an AI scraper visits a list of URLs, pulls pricing, features, pros and cons, and outputs a CSV for the CMS. Previously he hired people on Upwork, who often did good work on the first items and sloppy work after.
  - **Monetization:** Tiny Ads finds sponsors and advertisers for a directory, reaches out to them (emails, social accounts), lets them place ads, and the owner collects the money. Manual sponsor outreach is a lot of work.
- His refund workflow in Zapier: every support email goes to an LLM that detects refund requests, checks the database for a real user by email, and refunds automatically if they are in the first 30 days, no questions asked. Complicated cases are forwarded to him with the details.
- Make, n8n and Zapier all have AI that builds the workflow from a plain description, so he says you don't need a course.

## One-button products and automating the work

- Every product he builds aims at "one button" or none: SEO Bot has no real interface, you turn it on and it does everything. He says he is "super lazy" and is the user of his own tools.
- His obsession with automation comes from watching his entrepreneur parents work extremely hard. His goal is to make sure business owners don't have to.
- He estimates 75-80% of his work is automated now, targeting 90% by the end of this year and 95% by the end of next year. The remaining job would be the creative part: coming up with ideas and directing AI agents as if they were tireless employees.
- His pattern: build with AI, drive traffic with AI, monetize with AI. All 26 products use AI to some degree; some, like Listing Bot and SEO Bot, are heavily AI.
- He runs no calls, only chats.

## Productivity: no system, a pitch-first focus

- He uses no project management software, documents or plans. His rule: use memory to prioritize. If he remembers a task the next day, it deserves work. If he forgets it, people didn't complain enough or it wasn't important.
- His only tool is Discord, with one channel per product where he and the co-maker talk. It keeps a scrollable history.
- He doesn't do calls (a "15-minute" call becomes an hour) and replies to emails with short messages. He says nobody has ever called him rude for it.
- Don't spend effort on things that don't matter yet:
  - Logo from ChatGPT or a stock library.
  - First landing page from a template in about five minutes, while he knows people who spend weeks. A new website is invisible, and anyone you send it to has already bought the idea.
- Put about 90% of early effort into the pitch, because most pitches fail and nobody ever sees the product. Practice the pitch heavily.
- Principle: "Put 1% effort, get something out, don't care. Be embarrassed. It's fine." Then focus on whatever matters at the current stage, which changes at each stage.
- He doesn't chase billion-dollar outcomes, so he can relax some days and let growth be slower. He launches products, then works long hours when traction hits (Tiny Ads brought bugs, support tickets and features) to keep momentum.

## Validating an idea: audience first, product last

John's reverse-order steps, from his answer on coaching someone with an idea such as a voice agent for children:

1. Start with the audience. Check whether you can build one for the idea, since that is harder than building the product. "Try something that's hard first."
2. Try to sell before you build. You'll learn you need more skills, and may need to test 100 versions of your pitch.
3. Talk about the topic on social media. If the content gets no attention, nobody cares about the problem or your pitch is bad. Even with few followers you can test on Reddit by finding the relevant subreddit, sharing the idea, and changing the pitch until people upvote and ask questions.
4. Expect this to take years, not weeks.
5. Talk to the people who respond until you know exactly what to build.
6. Then build a very simple MVP yourself with no-code or vibe-coding tools, even if the final product is complex and will need real developers later.
7. Bring it back to your audience and iterate for about a year based on feedback.

- After traction exists, anything is open: keep building solo, hire, raise money or find a co-founder. "As long as you have traction everything will be open all doors will be open."

## Skills, luck and keeping up with AI news

- Skills: build one superpower you know better than others (design, coding, business), be a lightweight generalist elsewhere, and find the intersection of that skill and technology. His own was coding, then business knowledge from many failed products (most of his first 15 years failed, which showed him typical founder problems). He suggests that fields like biology, chemistry or agriculture combined with tech may beat crowded computer science.
- If you think you're not great at anything, he says you haven't tried enough things.
- Luck: he has seen lucky people make a lot of money and end up depressed, doubting they deserved it. He prefers earned results, which give self-respect. Throwing many darts and staying in the arena produces results that are only partly luck.
- His AI news posts, in three steps:
  1. Remember the week's events and list them from memory.
  2. Give the list to his tool Social Boat to research details and exact numbers (for example, the price of Windsurf's sale to OpenAI).
  3. Rewrite it himself so it is interesting. AI makes things boring, and AI-written irony overshoots, so he adds the strange, funny, sarcastic tone himself.
- Sourcing without doomscrolling: every few hours he opens X's Explore tab to see what is hot and leaves without entering threads or the feed. Daily on Reddit he uses the "best today" filter.
