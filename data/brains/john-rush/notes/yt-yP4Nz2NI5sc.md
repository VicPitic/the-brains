## How John became a maker: equity co-founder first, own products later

- John started in 2007 while studying computer science. He found the assignments boring, asked his professor for real work, and joined a government-ordered project his university had won.
- He found that building something people actually use is far more fun than building assignments.
- For roughly the first five years he had no ideas of his own. He found people with ideas and built quickly with them, acting as a technical co-founder.
- He was paid in equity, typically 5% to 15% of the company. He recommends working for equity: "people trust that you do because you're interested in the project", and incentives are aligned.
- By his 10th to 15th project he knew more than his co-founders about marketing, HR, fundraising and investor meetings, because he sat in on all of it. After five years he started his own products.
- His rule: keep doing and shipping constantly, and good ideas eventually come. Find the intersection of what people want and what he likes, so he can go fully into a project.
- He prefers "maker" to "entrepreneur", because entrepreneurs manage people and he outsources management so he can make.

## MarsX: building the platform that built everything else

- John built MarsX out of his own pain: every project took time, had bugs, needed constant maintenance, and was hard to continue when developers left.
- Around 2017-2018 he tried many no-code and low-code tools. They give high leverage at first, then fail once the product gets complicated and you want control over the code.
- So he built a system where you start with no code and transition to pro code.
- It was an internal tool for him for the first years, then friends used it, then friends of friends, and it grew into a sizable startup.
- Figures he gives: almost $2 million ARR, hundreds of projects running on it, millions of users across those projects combined.
- MarsX became his leverage: "I built this wheel and then I built all the cars on top of it."
- He runs three types of projects: simple information sites (directories), SaaS tools, and platforms that others build businesses on. His platforms are a movie-industry one started about 10 years ago (boring, big, funds his other work), MarsX, and Unicorn Platform.

## Prioritizing: only the urgent, plus spontaneous projects

- John uses an "urgent/important" approach and works only on urgent things: what is too late if done tomorrow. He keeps no plans, to-do lists or calendars.
- About 70% of his time is reactive: his many products "scream" at him through customer complaints and bugs, and he works on whatever screams loudest.
- About 20-30% is spontaneous work with no plan. Example: an hour before the talk he noticed there are many Next.js boilerplates online, found a domain, bought it, built a landing page and a directory, and launched it right after the call.
- To keep priorities clear, launch unfinished. Even one early user will name the two or three things bugging them, so he never has to choose what to build.
- He launches "way before you're embarrassed, even way before it works", tells users it's early, and they accept that.
- He tracks his projects by looking at his GoDaddy domain list, and found forgotten projects with good domains to relaunch.

## Finding ideas: build for your own problems, or reverse-engineer traffic

- **SaaS tools:** he builds for his own problems, such as being unhappy with email tools or SEO tools. If it is great for him, it is likely great for many others. He builds it as a personal utility first, with no domain or design. Some he used for years before launching, and some he forgot to launch.
- **Directories and information sites:** he starts from the consumer. He researches Google keyword traffic and looks for high-traffic keywords with no product, where traffic goes to blogs.
- Directories suit this because the window is short: if he spends three months building a tool, the keyword opportunity is gone. He has days or a week.
- Example: he found high traffic for "all GPTs" just before OpenAI's GPT launch, bought the domain because it was free and unpopular, and filled it with GPTs after the launch. People now call it the best GPT directory.
- Process for the easy ones: keyword, landing page, content, tweet.
- Directories give him about half the traffic to his other projects.

## Validating on Twitter in 30 minutes

- John's only validation is keyword traffic plus the reaction to one tweet. After about 30 minutes of engagement he can tell whether an idea is good.
- Twitter is so strong now that he doesn't need other channels. A year earlier he also used Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn and conference pitches.
- His best projects got over 100,000 views when he had only a few thousand followers, because Twitter shows tweets beyond your network.
- Example: DevHunt. He disliked that Product Hunt is closed source and that some products bought bots. He tweeted asking whether an open-source alternative was a good idea, the tweet exploded with almost no followers, and he found people to build it open source. DevHunt's traffic is doubling every month.
- Even with 100 followers it works: "if nobody reacts out of your hundred friends, your idea is actually bad."
- Validation works because his audience is the same as his target customer: founders, makers, SaaS tools. He keeps other audiences, like movie-industry, in separate channels.
- His intuition for SaaS tools aimed at business makers is now near 100% accurate. Earlier, his intuition was often the opposite of the outcome, so he used the crowd to correct it.

## Launch marketing: the movie playbook

- John's marketing comes from the movie industry: anticipation, teasers and trailers, then burning about 80% of the marketing budget in the two weeks around launch.
- He applies the same strategy: heavy promotion concentrated around launch, since that is the crucial period.
- He now tweets mostly as marketing, since his intuition already tells him what will work.

## Twitter for makers: stay on one topic

- John says he was everywhere before Twitter and wishes he had bet on it from the start. He recommends Twitter as the place for makers.
- Algorithm advice: talk about only one topic, makers and building. Comment only on maker posts so your feed fills with them, then reply and post.
- Never post about crypto or politics.
- He uses about 200 muted words to keep his feed clean.
- He posts twice a day and only about things he has done himself.
