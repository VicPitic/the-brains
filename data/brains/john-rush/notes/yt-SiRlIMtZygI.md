## Building agents that start as internal tools and go public later

- John says all of his products start as his own internal agent. He runs each one for 1-3 years before it goes public.
- Being the only customer makes a project easier to run. He can move fast, take risks and has no users complaining.
- Once a product is stable and he stops iterating much, he brings in friends who run startups. Then he launches it publicly. "Eventually everything I use internally will become public."
- He deliberately creates "suffering" for himself by doing accounting, design, development, growth and paperwork himself instead of delegating. That lets him judge whether an agent is good enough. He still does his own accounting for this reason.
- His agents are "so simple on the surface and so complicated under the hood". The goal is to turn one on and have it work like a self-managed employee that asks him questions and then goes back to work.
- He has 24 projects, mostly websites, with combined revenue of around $3M. Three products bring in most of it.
- He bet early on AI getting much stronger (he automated things with GPT-2 and GPT-3, and bet bigger when 3.5 launched). He chose to bootstrap small companies and automate the 20% of work that gives 80% of the result: first operations, then growth, then development.

## SEO Bot: an agent with almost no interface

- SEO Bot is at $1M ARR. It was doubling month over month before summer 2025, then stalled at about $110k a month over the summer. He expected growth to restart in September.
- It runs on autopilot. It sends weekly emails saying what it did: articles, how-to guides, mini tools that drive SEO traffic, and newsletters from collected news.
- It asks him for input only when unsure, for example "I have 10 items, pick five". Over time he moved from involved daily, to weekly, to monthly, to about once a quarter.
- From the start the interface had no buttons, only a checkbox to turn it on. He added controls later because agencies and pro users asked for them.
- He argues people want a lot of control only because they have too much time, such as an employee with eight hours a day for SEO. He believes long term people don't want that much control.
- Setup: give it your URL. It analyzes the audience, finds low-competition keywords, builds an article plan and publishes about one article a day. It syncs with Framer, Webflow and WordPress, so you never have to come back.
- It costs about $20 a month, versus about $5K for an agency.
- To manage SEO churn, he emails weekly KPIs. Views show up within a week and clicks later, so users see things are happening. Results now show in 2-3 weeks, where they once took 2-3 months.
- His customers are mostly busy founders, not SEO professionals.

## Connected products and cross-selling

- His agents call each other. SEO Bot can ask Rapify (a vibe-coding tool for little tools) to build a tool and send it back, like a marketer asking a developer.
- Roughly half of his new users each month come from one of his other tools.
- Some tools make only about $1K a month, but they are cheap, have trials, and feed users into his other tools. "It's very rare when people leave my universe."
- He does the same with directories. Most make about $1K a month and some make $10K. He values them for driving traffic from people who would not otherwise find him.
- He is not rushing growth. He wants each agent at roughly 100 to 1,000 customers, enough to test and A/B test, and avoids more because of support load and legacy to maintain.

## Finding ideas

- 99% of his ideas come from scratching his own itch. The second source is seeing what many people ask for.
- Google autocomplete hack:
  1. Type a phrase like "website builder for" and read the suggestions (small business, photographers, artists, nonprofits).
  2. Spend about an hour building a list of 50-60 ideas in a spreadsheet.
  3. Run each through a keyword research tool to see traffic.
- Example: "website builder for artists" showed about 10K visitors a month, with several similar phrases at about 10K each, roughly 100K in total. He also tested "tux heaven" at about 100K searches with low competition.
- Why niches work: VC-backed companies chase the same big markets. Small niches are "not a billion dollar idea" but are enough for a regular person to build a million-dollar business, so many are unserved.
- Don't guess. "The intuition never helps."
- He first asks whether he wants the product himself. If he does, he keeps going even if others don't, and he may shelve it for a year while he works out why.

## Validating an idea

- He tries to kill the idea. "If you're in love with your idea... you will interpret the data in a wrong way." Better to kill 20 ideas and build the 21st.
- Step 1: build a simple waitlist landing page in a landing page builder (Unicorn Platform, Framer or Webflow), not a vibe-coding tool. Builders have better SEO and are made for landing pages. Vibe-coding tools are for apps with logins. Collect only name and email.
- Step 2: drive traffic.
  - Post on social media and Reddit.
  - Reply to people discussing related topics, even loosely related ones (musicians and software, for example).
  - DM the same people.
  - Cold email them.
  - Do about 100 outreach actions in total.
  - Optionally send paid traffic (he used Google and Meta ads).
- Reading the result: zero signups is a useful answer. One, five or ten signups means you have something. Sometimes it takes off.
- Example: for Dev Hunt (a Product Hunt-style launchpad for dev tools), he tweeted that he'd build it if 100 people signed up. He got 100 signups, and people shared the link to help reach the number.
- Social platforms no longer weight follower count much. Interesting content gets shown to a few people and spreads if it works. He did this before he had followers.
- Step 3: pre-sales. Offer a 70-90% discount to buy now.
  - If under 10% of the waitlist pays, he treats it as a bad sign. It usually means the wrong audience (students, for example, signed up but wouldn't pay a dollar), and he says you'd have to kill the idea.
  - Pre-sales also reveal pricing and model problems. Listing Bot was planned as a monthly subscription, but people wanted a one-time payment. Count Visits was offered as a $100 lifetime deal, but people preferred starting at $9 a month.
  - Emailing waitlist people the payment link gives you a thread where they explain why they're not buying.
- Launch timing doesn't matter much. If you got 100 waitlist signups once, you can repeat it later. He treats first waitlisters as validation, not real users.
- He has never had indicators say yes and then flopped. He has had the opposite: everything said bad idea, then it worked.

## SEO from day one as validation

- Most people start SEO late. He starts SEO work on day one of a product, then waits a few months.
- SEO traffic is a second validation: it shows whether you can reach attention, not only whether people want the product. He says he rarely sees people fail to build something people want. The failure is reaching them.
- To test several ideas, he publishes SEO articles for each and leaves them for a few months. Some URLs get zero traffic (keywords too expensive to compete on), while others rank easily.
- This can beat keyword research alone, because keyword numbers don't tell you who is searching.
- Put waitlist banners in the articles as another test.
- On skipping SEO because it pays off a year later: "if you would have just done it... it would be paying off by now."

## His five growth channels

He says these five bring about 80% of early growth.

1. **Social media.** The best channel for small products. Popularity also brings backlinks, newsletter mentions and directory listings, and influencers talk about you for free. Test whether you have talent for it. If so, it may be the only thing to do.
2. **SEO.** Build backlinks and domain rating, and publish articles yourself or with a tool. About one hour a day for a month gives decent output. Outsource to agencies if you have money.
3. **Affiliate partners.** Use a tool like FirstPromoter with a partner portal, unique links and codes. Be "crazy generous" on commission: 50% if the product has no underlying costs, 25-30% if it does, versus the typical 10-15%. He calls it one of his best investments.
4. **Be everywhere.** Directories, launchpads, forums, Reddit, Quora, social media. People may find you, and LLMs crawl those comments, articles and listicles, so you may end up in their answers. Some of his products suddenly got nearly all their traffic from ChatGPT, Claude and Grok in the same week.
5. **Free tools as lead magnets.** For a website builder for artists, build an album cover generator. Users enter an email to get the PNG, and the email promotes your paid product. They click at a high rate because they already trust you.

## Directories and reputation in the AI era

- Directories made by people with influence will matter more. AI systems rank by backlinks, which are easy to game, and they now use Reddit upvotes to filter. Directories help humans filter out trash when a category has too many options.
- He says to partner with people who own attention and build products around it. A tool built by someone with a reputation stands out when everything built with AI is equally good.
- "Influencer" can be small. In a niche of 10,000 people, talking about it consistently can be enough to go viral within that group.
- Tools: Lovable for prototyping and MVPs (also Bolt, Cursor), his own coding tool for full products, and Make, Zapier and Lindy for automation.
