## Why John left VC-backed startups for bootstrapping

- John spent about 20 years working with startups, starting at 15, mostly in the VC-backed scene: raising money, joining incubators, hiring many people, going to conferences, bragging about headcount, then raising again.
- He found that cycle boring and too far from actual users. He wanted love and feedback "directly from users", not from journalists.
- He discovered Build in Public and scrolled the feed for about 10 hours. He liked that people bragged about having few or no staff, because fewer people means better margins and easier running.
- He dislikes employing people because "you're taking some Freedom away". VC investors also expect layers and structure. That was a key reason he left.
- He builds one product every month or quarter and keeps adding. He ended up with 24 products and about $2M in yearly recurring revenue (the host's figures, which he did not dispute). One product, Unicorn Platform, had 130,000 users at the time.
- He says he has never had more passion than in the past two years, because users send real screenshots and kind words.
- His big goal is not revenue. He wants the bootstrapper/indie scene to go from well under 1% of the SaaS world to 10-20%.

## Growing on X from 72 followers

- He never used Twitter before. He started because he wanted a direct, real-time channel to people, with quick feedback.
- Step 1: copy what accounts with big followings did. He used Twitter's filter to see their old posts, and found it easy to repeat. This got him to roughly 2,000 followers.
- Step 2: after that, "become yourself". Post things that are interesting about you, not just the content.
- Don't post what you don't want back. A joke or a Google-bashing post can go viral, but it attracts the wrong followers. People follow others who look like them.
- Decide who you want as followers (for him, founders who care about users) and post what they would also post.
- In B2B, don't worry about people outside the indie bubble. Indie makers run all kinds of businesses (a coffee shop, a cinema). "Build it and they will come" doesn't work; "build a good product and they will come" does, but others have to say it's good, and their outside networks bring more users.
- To protect against account loss (his X account was suspended for a week in May after a post with about 5M views), he is building a newsletter and a LinkedIn audience of about 10,000 people. He thinks LinkedIn people take things more seriously.

## How John gets feedback and builds with users

- Building in public shortens the feedback cycle, and he believes shortening the cycle is the only way to build a great product. Indie makers are rough in DMs, which helps.
- Feedback from free users, friends, and people you ask is bad for you. Someone asked for feedback "becomes like a professor" and tries to sound smart. Good feedback comes from people far from your circle who volunteer it.
- He never asks for feedback. He creates a channel instead. Most of his products have no public sign-up page, so people DM him on Twitter for an invite. That opens a conversation, and almost everyone eventually gives feedback (maybe tomorrow, maybe in a year).
- He talks to about 100 people a day, and calls them his unofficial product team. His Twitter chats are mostly about what he is working on that day. He shares what he does rather than asking questions.
- Small decisions matter more than people think, because product quality is the quality of hundreds of daily decisions.
- Everything is in his head: no notes, no diagrams, no Notion-style tools.
- He moved his own YouTube habit out of the way because it was the real focus-killer, not the Twitter tab.

## Bugs, Sentry and launching early

- He has used Sentry for 10 years and runs it on all his products. Sentry sends errors to a Discord channel where he chats with his developer, so errors appear in the middle of conversation and can't be ignored.
- When he forgets to set up the Discord webhook, the bugs get reported by users first, and he hates that.
- Fixing a bug before the user writes is a trust builder: "bugs are good if you kill them in front of the user". Replying "a fix is already on the way" makes him look ahead of everyone.
- He leaves small imperfections, such as spelling mistakes on landing pages. He doesn't create bugs on purpose, but he isn't afraid of them. Bugs bring people into your chat, show how you respond, and make them comfortable sending feedback and feature requests later.
- Launch early versions. If someone can try it, everyone should be able to. New people are born every day.

## Niche strategy and Unicorn Platform

- Building for a large market is a lottery. Narrow as much as you can: one problem, one type of company (team or solo founder), age, and so on.
- No market is below about $1M a year, and in a niche you may win 50-100% of it. Wide markets have 1,000 competitors, many funded and buying ads, and even SEO is paid and hard.
- Grow out of the niche by adding features. For example, start with individuals, then add team, collaboration and permissions features. The first group are word-of-mouth ambassadors, and some get hired into large companies and bring the product along.
- Unicorn Platform was a niche product that eventually hit the ceiling of that niche and stopped growing. He then removed filters, widening to a bigger niche, and it started growing again. His image: with 20 filters you find one person on earth; removing one filter gives 20, and you keep eating the market from inside out.
- Nothing he builds is disconnected. The vision is a toolset for busy founders who are short on time. He bets this group will take over the SaaS world because they build personal "startup machines" that can move faster than teams.
- He does not plan to look for other niches. He bets this niche becomes the mainstream and he powers it.

## Product ideas from his own problems, and buying back time

- He runs each new product internally for a long time before releasing it, because release brings support and uptime burden.
- If something takes him 10 hours a week or month, he swaps it for a tool. "My problem is my fuel." Without a problem, his ideas would be guesses.
- He tries to be the best example of his user, because no one is busier than him, so if he pleases himself he pleases everyone.
- Pay to buy time back. He mentions a friend with many followers who refused a $100/month tool and would do it by hand. He calculates the hours lost against $100.
- Developers do too much by hand because they love building. He has no time for that.
- He asks "how can I do this 100 times faster?" task by task. He says productivity and innovation go together, using the analogy that New York to London takes five hours because transport was invented, not because horses were beaten harder.
- At 25 he would stubbornly push a wrong approach. Now, with less energy, he asks a minute in whether he needs to do it at all. He thinks rich founders are not young, and says "you are 47 when you can make a million".
- He says he works about 6 hours at the desk plus about 4 hours of loose work (DMs, comments, thinking in the shower). He wakes around 10, spends time in the garden with kids, and stops to see friends.
- His advice: the long-term goals should give you joy, while short-term actions should be somewhat painful. "Don't do things that you like doing" in the small moment.
- He regrets many times hiring people for skills over character. He now only works with people he would be comfortable hugging, and says he failed "maybe 100 times, at least" by ignoring that.
- He A/B tests his Unicorn Platform landing page daily and records conversion, which is stable with high traffic. A recent test showed templates moved higher (not just examples of what others built) converted better.
- As a joke, he said he grew his button one pixel per day until conversion reversed.

## Where VC and startups are heading

- VC will shift from pre-seed and seed to companies with traction and revenue. Successful bootstrappers will mostly end up raising, which then works as a liquidity event rather than a necessity: take cash out, and delegate more.
- He is not raising, yet says more VCs have contacted him than ever, saying they are bootstrapper-friendly, with no board seat or even a board.
- He doesn't need money because he doesn't spend on growth, and building is cheap.
- Startups used to be built around a product. Now the founder or the machine is the product. Products may become uninteresting within a year, so founders who can keep producing relevant products win. He compares this to Elon Musk building the Gigafactory, the factory rather than one car.
- He expects investors to back people (like a founder who can build anything) rather than one product.

## Product Hunt, Dev Hunt and an Indie Maker Village

- He built Dev Hunt, and takes Product Hunt's direction seriously, as indie makers' success is his success. He believes Product Hunt should act like a community with real, taggable people who engage in complaints, not a black box that spits out winners.
- He argues indie makers are a small share of the population but drive a big share of activity: comments, good ratings, real feedback. Without them "the party may turn so boring" that nobody dances.
- On low-effort products (e.g. a Notion-page template made in an hour), he partly agrees with the CEO, but says the community should decide, not moderators. Moderators can be fooled with a pretty website.
- On Dev Hunt, voters must add social handles. They remove votes from fake accounts and fake handles, and the voting rules are public. His recommendation to Product Hunt: work much harder on the quality of votes, remove cheaters and fake accounts, and let the community decide.
- He thinks indie makers should be more open to cross-marketing, because otherwise they are "small kingdoms" a big enemy can destroy.
- He predicts a burnout epidemic among makers, because they sit at the same computer for work and leisure. His answer is a physical Indie Maker Village. He built a small version in Turkey years ago, with about 20 people.
- The plan has three stay types: up to five years' residency (many makers from developing countries want a passport path), 3-month incubation stays, and short hackathon weekends.
- Residents would do shared real work together (build a house, plant vegetables). Villagers do one activity together, so people copy each other's doing, rather than learning theory.
- He plans to presell days (one day costs X, refundable, usable later) to gauge interest and says he already has a list of hundreds of people who would come. He aims to build many such places around the world.
- Success would be measured by combined MRR and website traffic of residents, like Mark Lou's leaderboard.
