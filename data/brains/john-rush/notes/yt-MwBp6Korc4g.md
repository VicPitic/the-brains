## Why John went bootstrapped and solo after 10 years of VC-backed startups

- John ran VC-backed startups from 2010, with large teams. They raised money, ran in the USA and scaled internationally. This lasted about 10 years.
- His biggest pain was managing people. He says he likes people but not at work, because "at work you have to tell them what to do", and people use niceness as an excuse not to do things.
- All his companies had fewer people than competitors, and he always automated what he could, first without AI.
- Around 2020 he tried an early GPT (he thinks GPT-2 or GPT-3) in a B2C "concierge for tourists" startup. It connected to local taxi and pizza systems and translated on the fly. COVID lockdowns and closed borders killed it, so he moved to B2B.
- He left VC because he disliked the model and the San Francisco meetings. His thesis for competing with VC-backed startups: "90% of the money goes into the people in early stages", so automate the people with tools.
- His first project was automating coding itself, because it gives the biggest leverage for building every other tool. He spent 2020 to 2022 on it, then entered the market in 2023.

## The solo-founder bet: build software for founders, not employees

- He bet around 2021 that attention was shifting to a world where talent matters more than money. Ads are expensive and getting more so, and people trust authentic content over paid.
- His prediction: solo founders and tiny, talented teams will out-compete giant companies with hundreds of people and millions in funding.
- Second assumption: almost all software is built for employees, who have time to learn tools. Nobody builds for founders, who never have time and want to "click the button, that's it".
- His approach: build the agents for himself, iterate until he is happy, then ship. Because he is "such a good representation of the new audience", others from that audience love the product.
- He tests across his many projects (corporate, consumer, directories) so the tool does not only work for one use case.
- He has had a fixed list of things to automate since 2021. He only reorders it by which one brings the most gain. In 2023 SEO across about 20 websites was the biggest pain, so he built that agent first.
- Status as of the interview: 2 years fully in, 7 agents on the market, about 26 projects in total. Some agents are, he thinks, best in class and growing faster than he can handle. He has slept under five hours for about a year and treats the pain as a signal of what to automate next instead of hiring.

## Agent timing: ship only when the tool saves about 90% of the time

- He accounts for LLM progress so he does not build what the model will soon do out of the box (e.g. RAG, JSON output). That is why he kept many early tools unlaunched.
- Founders need to at least double productivity to change habits. His bar: the message must be "it saves you 90% of the time". Early agents were 50/50 or 70/30 human oversight, which was not enough.
- Timeline: everything in-house in 2022, closed in the first half of 2023, some agents opened in beta at the end of 2023, most opened publicly in September 2024.
- GPT-4 (April 2023) convinced him the bet was right, but it was unstable for systems that must run predictably. Function calling and JSON outputs arrived in summer 2024. He says stability matters because agents run on autopilot.

## Where agents work and where they don't: creativity and social content

- Agents are least suited to creative work. AI is good at average creative output, but "the average never wins" in the attention world, and algorithms reward the best content even more over time.
- Even if AI gets funnier, humans move higher. He compares it to chess: AI plays, but people still watch humans, and people will care about the origin of content.
- Not all content needs creativity. SEO content just answers a question, so it suits agents.
- He runs social media mostly himself. His agents only help distribute content he created.

## Content strategy: long-form compression and the chat-driven workday

- He does not think attention spans are shrinking. He thinks people are lazier and want content that is easy to read and also useful.
- Content that is both fun and educational is hard to make. He bets on long-form, partly storytelling, partly jokes, with things readers did not know and will bookmark. He says he was one of the first to do long tweets and deep dives on X.
- Compression wins attention: people share content that saves them time. But compressing requires real understanding. He can compress agents and startups because he has used them 10 to 20 times across scenarios.
- His day is about 90% chatting. He answers short questions from his agents (yes/no, pick one of five) and prompts tools. Replies on Twitter arrive in the same channel, so content creation fits in without a separate block of time.
- For learning his material, he points founders to his X Highlights tab, with a week of reading including the replies.

## Why solo and micro startups will multiply

- Implementing today's agents everywhere could lay off perhaps half the workforce. Many of his products out-compete companies of 50 to 100 people.
- He argues new niches open up. If a product takes a month to launch and costs about $1K a month in agents plus his own salary, markets once too small (below a billion dollars) become viable. He cites roughly $1M a year as a fine ceiling for a niche product.
- He estimates 99% of the world is not powered by software, and expects the number of people building startups to rise from perhaps 10 million to hundreds of millions. His guess is most people will build startups or be a single employee overseeing agents.
- He compares it to Instagram. People once thought few would create content, but over 50% of social users now do, and he estimates about 20 in 100 do it seriously.
- Today the market punishes founders (no incentive, no insurance perks). He expects that to flip as agents lower corporate salaries and raise requirements.
- Niches the best founders skip stay open, like rabbit YouTube content that is average but sells pills. Small niches mean little competition from top talent.

## How to pick what to start: unpopular niches and services first

- Most people pick what is popular (AI wrappers, chatbots) and face heavy competition. His rule: avoid competition today and tomorrow, since being first means little when others enter and prices drop.
- Pick an unsexy niche you are willing to understand deeply, such as flowers, schools or tractors, and aim to be among the best in it.
- His method: sell a service to the niche first. Services are easier to sell than products (one transaction, price-first buying). While doing them you learn which part to turn into a product, and you pitch ideas to real buyers.
- He says coming up with an idea in isolation almost never worked for him. Validation: buyers keep asking "when is it done?" Then failure risk is very low. Without it, failure risk is very high.
- Details matter: the audience, format and size, not only the one-liner.

## Getting the first sale and the founder mindset

- Services solve the credibility gap. You can do the work free before being paid, like a designer redesigning a site and tagging the owner, or an SEO expert writing an article or roasting a profile and sending it.
- He says to expect about one in five recipients to buy. Once someone buys a service, you are "in their house" and they trust you enough to buy a product even with no users.
- Mindset: it is a 10-year game and the first 3 to 4 years are a "training camp". Fast success stories are, he says, mostly luck, perhaps 1 in 99 or worse.
- Do not pick big fights early. Like a boxer, spar with the punching bag first. Starting too hard means a near-certain early failure that makes it hard to continue.
- Easier fights: services, free products, non-AI products, directories and job boards. In year one, chase attention before revenue, then go for small-ticket revenue.

## Directories as the easiest starter product

- A directory lists items in a niche (AI coding tools, MVP builders). In a world of infinite choice, people who help others choose are useful.
- He predicted in 2020 that directories would be big, because AI makes building easy, so near-identical alternatives multiply and buyers fear picking badly.
- Value comes from curation: good descriptions, highlighting the right things, filters and categories that narrow 500 items to 50, 5, then 1.
- Why he is bullish:
  - Easiest tech product: it can start in about half an hour with no-code or AI code, then takes time to collect items and write descriptions.
  - Easiest to promote: SEO. No Twitter audience or cold sales skills are needed, and the items themselves act as content for Google.
  - Competition is avoidable with the right niche, and the traffic is free, so the only cost is time.
- The monetization logic: connect buyers with sellers. Find buyers through content or directories and sellers will come to be shown to them.
