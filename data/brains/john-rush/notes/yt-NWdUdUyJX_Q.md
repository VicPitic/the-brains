## Bootstrapping vs VC: how the incentives change the founder

- John's path: bootstrapped his first startup and sold it, spent 8 years in VC-backed companies, then returned to bootstrapping for 7 years.
- His main difference: VC-backed KPIs rewire the founder's brain. In his first bootstrapped project he was obsessed with customers and profits. In VC-backed companies he was obsessed with the next round, his network, media coverage and conferences. Users and profits ranked last.
- This is the norm, not just his habit. You raise pre-seed, then immediately have to raise seed, then the next round.
- In a VC-backed startup, "the money lays in the exit". There is no other money there.
- He came from bootstrapping and assumed the company would eventually start making profits. He was wrong, and says he was "sabotaging my companies" with that assumption.
- His goal now is to help solo and bootstrapped founders run businesses on their own terms without VC money. He argues no VC means a better work-life balance, including for family life.

## Finding product ideas: map the jobs people do and replace them

- John planned to bootstrap forever, so he needed a cheap way to run companies. The biggest startup cost is people and salaries.
- Method:
  1. List the people you usually hire in a startup.
  2. Break each role into micro jobs. He came up with about 120 typical jobs.
  3. Build a product to replace each job, ordered by best ROI (small effort, high output).
- He generated all his ideas in one day from this map and now builds roughly one product every few months.
- Result: everything built from the map worked ("zero failures"). Everything built outside the map failed. His example is a tool for hosting AI-generated websites, which nobody bought.
- Why it works: he is the user himself. "I'm the ideal user of my products and if I love it, why would others not love it?"

## The 25-product portfolio: seven tools earn 90% of revenue

- Seven tools make about 90% of revenue. The rest make about 10% and mostly bring users in.
- He calls most of his tools "marketing assets rather than revenue assets". Some earn little but bring leads that get channeled into paid tools.
- The seven:
  - Unicorn Platform: website builder and the core of the system, with almost 600,000 users. The founder journey starts with a website.
  - SEO Bot: generates blog articles.
  - Listing Bot: lists a product on web directories.
  - Index Rusher: helps pages get indexed.
  - Count Visits: analytics across web, Stripe, Paddle and SEO.
  - SaaS Emailer: emails to SaaS users, plus campaigns.
  - Tiny Ads: launched the week before this interview, an ad network only for B2B SaaS and B2B tech. He calls it his best launch ever.
- A bigger audience speeds up every launch: everything he launches now grows faster than a year or two ago.

## Competing in crowded markets by picking a niche

- Competition doesn't scare him. Every market and use case has so many details that a product perfected for one niche can look like the others while being very different.
- Unicorn Platform example: many website builders exist, including Webflow and Framer. Those can do far more but are easy to break without design taste, so you need a designer.
- John targets busy solopreneurs who aren't designers and just want to launch a website quickly.
- His rule: "people should not be afraid to build something that looks like all the other products as long as they don't compete with all the other products." Typical Unicorn use cases are different enough that Webflow can't serve them, so he isn't competing with it. Trying to build everything for everyone (such as flower-shop sites) would lose.

## One shared codebase (Mars X) for all products

- He built Mars X first, a stack that all his products run on. He built a product to build the other products on.
- It contains reusable micro apps. A new product reuses them, so about 90% is already there and only about 10% is new.
- Improving a micro app improves every product that uses it. His 25 products behave almost like one product with one codebase, not 25 separate repos.
- Everything is TypeScript/JavaScript.

## UX as the competitive edge in B2B

- John's thesis: almost every problem already has a product. People don't realize it because the UX isn't clear.
- Old B2B users were paid employees who read manuals and watched videos. Today people expect everything to be like an iPhone, even at work. If a tool isn't obvious, employees tell the boss it doesn't do the job, and the boss doesn't check.
- Almost all B2B tools ignore UX and focus on features. He does the opposite: fewer features, extremely easy to use. When users are shocked in a good way, they share screenshots on social media.
- Launch example: for a tool built with a friend, he made them redo the whole UX before launch. Goal: "people will screenshot this and share it on Twitter praising the UX". The tool has a chat/terminal-style interface and went viral because of that UX. He notes people won't publicly praise an AI SEO tool itself, since that is slightly embarrassing.
- Tiny Ads onboarding is a casual WhatsApp-style chat instead of the usual forms and many steps. People loved it.
- Fewer steps isn't automatically easier: "sometimes more steps is better than less steps." He compares UX to music or film.
- Good products are now easy to produce with AI. UX is psychological and AI doesn't help much, so UX remains a reason his products win.

## Marketing and paying for growth

- Marketing is only him: his own tweets and face, 100% of his social media. Some products he never tweeted about.
- He built Tiny Ads because he wants to pay for growth and has profits to reinvest. Past random investments never worked out, so he puts money back into his products.
- Regular ads: he disliked their performance and the work of running them. Traditional ads work better for B2C than B2B because they center on social media.
- He prefers organic content and paying influencers, which he thinks are cheaper than paid social ads.
- He uses Tiny Ads himself to drive sales. The key is making $2 for every $1 spent on ads. That completes the picture, so he can reinvest profits and also take revenue-based loans, for which he sees low-interest offers, and put the money into ads.

## Advice for developers who want to build products

- Assess your strengths honestly. Empathy for the user and content creation (understanding people's psychology and writing to their mood) are very hard to learn, much like being funny.
- If you are naturally good at content and storytelling, go solo: "there is very low chance to fail" and everyone he knows like that is doing well.
- If you are good at coding but not storytelling, find a storytelling partner and team up. The chance of success is much higher. If you go solo anyway, make money elsewhere.
- Being the CTO isn't a defeat. Marketing and being the brand is a lot of work and stress, and he is sometimes jealous of people who just code.
- Content work has emotional highs and lows (million views, then none). Coding is steadier, and AI now finds most bugs.
- Long-term goal: a holding company that owns hundreds of leading products in their verticals and outlives him, running for 50 or even hundreds of years.
- Even if you're employed, build something you own. The joy of ownership is worth it even without income.
