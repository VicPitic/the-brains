## Idea validation blueprint: pain, waitlist, pre-sale, manual delivery

John uses the same process for all 20+ of his products.

1. **Start with his own pain at work.** He looks for an existing solution first.
2. **If nothing exists, talk about the pain publicly.** If other people reply "I have the same pain, I wish there was a solution," that is the green light.
3. **Launch a waitlist.** The target is 100 signups.
4. **Email the list a 90% discount pre-sale before building anything.**
5. **Build the MVP only after 5 sales.**
6. **Deliver the solution manually, with no product yet.** He ignores margins at this stage. He only cares that customers are satisfied.
7. **Iterate on the manual service until customers like it.** It is easier to iterate with no code, because he only changes his own routine and service.
8. **Find a co-maker, spend two to three months building, then do a big launch on Twitter.**

## Founder-market fit: build for your own work

- John's advice: "The most important thing when you build a startup is the idea and the founder idea fit."
- He says people go wrong by hunting for random ideas in random spaces. They should build something they understand.
- He went to the extreme and built things for his own work, which he understands "really really well."
- He is sure that any job has hundreds of problems software can solve. His advice is to look around you and find the pain points in your job.
- Building for something you are passionate about or have expert knowledge in gives a head start.

## Co-maker model: 50/50 partners who build

- Finding a co-maker is as hard as finding a co-founder.
- John befriends other makers over months or years. When he needs a co-maker, he knows who to ask. For a social media tool, he would approach founders who built something similar and failed.
- His pitch is an idea that has already passed validation, with pre-sales done and some users in place. He says that makes it "really easy" to convince them.
- Ownership is split 50/50. John handles operations, legal and accounting. The co-maker handles coding and support.
- To be a co-maker people want to work with, build in public. It lets others evaluate you and see what you have in common.

## Marketing: product first, then SEO, social, directories and cross-promo

- John says good marketing is a good product. The ideal is a product that drives sales and word of mouth on its own.
- Great products rarely exist on day one. You start with an average product, use "boring" marketing to bring the first users, then use those users to improve the product.
- Channels before the product is great:
  - **SEO** is first.
  - **Social media** is second. He posts on X, LinkedIn, Substack and Facebook, repurposes the same content everywhere, and does it daily. He shares at least 30 tweets a month, sometimes more.
  - **Directory listings** are third. They work well if the product is hot and interesting. All his products are AI-related and easy to package so people click them on directories. A "clickbaity" product drives directory traffic more easily.
  - **Cross-promo** is the fourth, and he calls it unique to him.

## Portfolio cross-promotion: premium tools plus traffic tools

- John runs 20+ tools. Some are premium tools that make money. Others exist to bring in traffic and channel it to the premium tools.
- Every user who tries one product ends up trying at least one more. Some try all of them. Cross-promo works because people already trust him and know he delivers.
- He does two things:
  - He links tools to each other.
  - He integrates tools with each other. Example: in SEO Bot there is a "boost my domain rating" button that sends users to Listing Bot, which helps boost domain rating.
- Users flow natively from product to product and end up in his whole ecosystem.
- Revenue is concentrated. Seven products bring most of the revenue, and the others bring the audience and channel it to the paid products.

## The portfolio and how he got into bootstrapping

- Scale: about 26 startups, roughly 1 million B2B users combined, and about $3M ARR combined.
- **Unicorn Platform:** a website, landing page, waitlist and directory builder for busy founders, with 600,000 users. In 2022, after about 10 years in VC-backed startups, he asked the founder to sell him the whole startup and stay on for a year as his bootstrapping mentor. He paid almost $1M.
- **SEO Bot:** about $100K MRR, grown in under 12 months, and one of the first AI SEO agents on the market.
- **Listing Bot:** finds relevant directories and lists your product there.
- **Nova:** an AI project manager he built to manage projects with his many co-makers.

## AI use, tools and margins

- **Building:** he built his own AI code generator for SaaS products, no-code tools and AI agents. He calls AI coding probably the best application of AI today. His stack is JavaScript and Tailwind, which he says AI handles very well.
- **Design:** logos and images are made with AI, and he mostly does not use designers.
- **Research:** he used to hire people. Now he gives a task to o3 or Grok research mode and gets a complete data table after about 10 minutes.
- **Marketing:** he says this is where AI gains him the most profit, since marketing otherwise consumes a lot of resources. He uses it for SEO, emails, copywriting and ads.
- **Models in products:** he uses Claude, Gemini and OpenAI APIs inside his tools. He keeps switching because the best model changes monthly, so he uses all three and lets users choose.
- **Other tools:** Grok for learning, because it connects to real-time tweets. Discord for chat and project management. Apple Notes for writing, after trying everything complicated.
- **Margins:** SEO Bot earns about $100K/month and pays about $30K for APIs, roughly 70% margin. Non-AI tools have about 90% margins.
- **Support:** he keeps human support in all products. Unicorn Platform's main cost is support agents. His reason: "AI doesn't have the soul." People chat with humans and build relationships.

## Build in public and VC vs bootstrap

- **Build in public:** he first saw it as free marketing. The bigger value is a direct channel to the audience and users, which helps him build better products.
- **Failure rate:** before building in public, about 90% of what he did failed. After, only about 10% of products fail, because he adjusts them before shipping.
- **VC vs bootstrap:**
  - VC is about exits, and everything is optimized for an exit. Bootstrap is about profit.
  - VC pays for growth. Bootstrap relies on product-led growth.
  - VC grows headcount to raise valuation. Bootstrap cuts headcount, ideally to solo, since then there are no costs.
