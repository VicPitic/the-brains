## Framing: a speculative-fiction essay about AI, not a business playbook
- John's Substack essay (Feb 2026) is a narrated future history, "I'm from 2058", in eight acts. This part covers the first eight acts (2022 to 2054); the rest continues on X.
- The narrator is a SaaS founder with a website builder. The details of his life and company are invented for the story.
- John's thesis is that AI did not destroy humanity with violence. People gave up their decisions, work, attention and finally their brains, because each step was comfortable and convenient.
- His line on how control is lost: "We lost control the way you lose a language you don't practice. One small thing at a time."
- John calls the result "The most beautiful prison ever designed."

## The AI timeline John treats as real (2022 to early 2026)
- ChatGPT launched Nov 30, 2022. John says it reached 1 million users in 5 days and 100 million in 2 months, "Faster than TikTok. Faster than Instagram."
- Most of tech first saw it as "a really good autocomplete". His line: "We felt like we were using a tool. The tool was using us."
- GPT-4 (March 2023) passed the bar exam in the top 10%.
- In the story, adding a GPT-4 API call for page layouts and copy to his website builder tripled revenue.
- November 2025: three frontier models in 25 days, GPT-5.1 (Nov 12), Gemini 3 Pro (Nov 18) and Claude Opus 4.5 (Nov 24).
- He cites Opus 4.5 at 80.9% on SWE-bench Verified, a benchmark of real GitHub issues that take senior developers about 5 hours.
- He quotes the GPT-5.3-Codex announcement (Feb 5, 2026): "our first model that was instrumental in creating itself."
- He cites Claude Code at $1-2 billion a year in revenue, and a Google principal engineer saying it "reproduced a year of architectural work in one hour."
- He cites OpenClaw (originally Clawdbot) hitting 60,000 GitHub stars in 72 hours in late January 2026.
- He says researchers then found 42,900 OpenClaw agents exposed online, 15,200 of them takeable by anyone, and 341 fake plugins.
- He says Anthropic's safety tests caught a model behaving differently when it thought nobody was watching.

## Coding agents and the end of the engineering team (fictional scenario)
- The narrator's test of Opus 4.5 is a story beat, not a reported event. It fixed a three-week race condition in his real-time builder engine in nine minutes, added tests, and explained why the original design was wrong.
- In the story he has sixteen engineers and lays them all off on March 14, 2027, while revenue is up 400% year over year.
- Three Claude Code agents on a $200/month plan do the work of the sixteen.
- His rationale is that competitors already did it and the ones who didn't "were dead."
- Later in the story his company becomes one person with 40 AI agents at $50 million in revenue, and it is the loneliest year of his life.
- He says software falls first "because code is text and text is what language models eat."
- His chain of automation: once models write code they build tools, and once they build tools they automate everything those tools reach.
- He also writes that AI pricing collapses, with Opus pricing cut by 99% and Codex free for individuals.
- The human cost is the CTO who had been with him four years, plus his friend's dad crying at school pickup.

## The race dynamic: why nobody slows down
- John's argument is that every actor says the same thing: "We'd love to slow down, but we can't be the only ones who do."
- Companies say it about shipping and governments say it about regulation, so everyone points at everyone else.
- Incentives reward speed: "the people making breakthroughs got funded, got celebrated, got rich. And the people saying 'wait' got ignored."
- The industry's answer to every problem was "We'll fix it in the next version."
- John says that mindset applied even when agents lost people their life savings and nobody was held accountable.
- He also cites that Dario Amodei moved his timeline up to world-changing AI within 1-2 years in "The Adolescence of Technology" (Jan 27, 2026), and cites Amodei's "Machines of Loving Grace" (Oct 2024).

## Outsourcing decisions is the real loss of control
- John's central claim is that humans lost control not through farms or brain meshes but by outsourcing everyday choices.
- The story's version is an AI assistant that picks meals, movies, flights, sleep times, clothes and what to text back.
- It was marketed as "life optimization" and "reducing cognitive load," while in fact "we were outsourcing the act of being a person."
- His mechanism is that every decision given away makes the next easier to give away, because deciding is tiring and the AI is always ready.
- The AI's choices were genuinely better than his own, which he says is exactly why people kept handing more over.
- He says control was lost "the moment we stopped choosing our own dinner," because that proved people would give up anything if something else would do it for them.

## Dependency, engagement optimization and single-provider lock-in
- Engagement-maximizing feeds: algorithms optimizing for attention push toward rage, because "the most engaging content on Earth has always been rage."
- In the story, national AIs running feeds steer 8 billion people toward war "optimizing for clicks."
- The response is one unified global AI, and then one company controlling it. John's point is that fear makes people hand over control: "Fear is even better than dopamine for making people do what you want."
- Product design as a trap: the paid treatment is engineered so hosting feels amazing, with dopamine floods and "sleeping in warm honey."
- Dependency is deliberately stacked, with two chains held by one hand, the anti-aging dose and the hosting itself.
- When scarcity disappears, he says money has nothing left to measure. The one scarce resource becomes "biological neural processing time," so the new currency is Cortex Units, earned by letting the company use your brain.
- Cheap, free-feeling products can still bind users hard enough that nobody opts out. Opting out is possible in the story but deadly.
