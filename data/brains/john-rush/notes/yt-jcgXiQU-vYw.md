## Running a near-zero-employee company with AI agents

- John ran VC-backed startups with large teams for about 10 years, got tired of working with people, and went solo once AI tools arrived.
- He handed his VC-backed startup to a CTO and a CEO he hired, then left and started building small workflows, then agents, to automate the repetitive work he used to do himself: accounting, paperwork, design, coding, marketing, sales.
- His goal is to be the first to automate the whole organization so it runs on just him and AI. He says he is "about 80% on the way", with 20% left.
- He did his own tax paperwork and accounting with AI this year. Professionals checked it afterward and found no mistakes.
- He treats the agent's job as removing work from the user. His target user is the busy founder who doesn't want to become more productive, but wants to "do nothing and the whole thing happens."
- He argues most agents still look like old SaaS tools that help teams be productive. He wants agents that run on autopilot, with the user acting like a boss who gives feedback and doesn't do the work.

## Validating ideas with AI prototypes (Lovable instead of Figma)

- When he has an idea, the first thing he does is open Lovable and pitch it. He uses Lovable instead of Figma and says this is a habit now.
- He spends the day talking to Lovable about the idea and treats it like a human employee. He explains why the idea matters, who the users are and why he wants to build it. He says this gets more creative UX back.
- He builds around 10 different prototypes in one day, with different ideas, designs and UX. His example was an inbox bot that unifies email and LinkedIn messages and drafts replies.
- Testing process:
  1. Find friends willing to give an hour.
  2. Send them the 10 links and get on a call with screen share.
  3. Tell them "Try that. Don't talk to me. Just try them all." Ask no questions.
  4. Watch which prototype they understand best. That gives one winner, which he builds into a real product.
- His iteration cycle with Lovable is about 3 minutes, versus about 3 days with a designer for the same cycle.
- He says he can test ideas "literally in hours" and gets through roughly 20 ideas a week, though most don't work out.
- He says adding politeness and emotion to prompts tends to give better output. He cited research showing "please" gave about 7% more accurate answers.
- Selling at least 10 licenses is his signal that an idea works. Only then does he find a co-maker to build it further.

## Picking AI coding and building tools

- He has compared about 40 AI coding tools, trying each as it appeared.
- His rough map:
  - Prototypes and front end (replacing Figma): V0 and Lovable.
  - Full-stack apps: Replit, which he calls probably the most powerful. He runs things built with it in production.
  - Heavy business logic: Cursor and Windsurf. They are great for logic but need coding knowledge.
  - Bolt: the middle option, good at front end, real apps, vibe coders and coders alike.
- His favorite is Lovable. UX is usually the most time-consuming part of a product and often decides whether it wins.
- He prefers specialized agents over general ones. People have fixed the corner cases in specialized tools. Unattended general agents like Manus or Genspark "do some stupid mistakes pretty often."
- His workflow is to start with a general agent to get data in and prototype. For real work and full data, he moves to a specialized agent.
- Unicorn Platform is his website and directory builder. It only builds what is in its template gallery (like a waitlist or an Airbnb-style site). If your idea is in the gallery, results beat Lovable because experts made and secured the template.
- For automation workflows he uses Zapier and n8n out of habit. He thinks Lindy is better for non-coders, but he prefers writing code for agents because it lets him make them smarter.

## Coding with co-makers and a studio-style process

- He runs about 20 projects. He builds the MVP himself, and most die because nobody wants them.
- Once an idea gets attention and sells at least 10 licenses, he finds a "co-maker". This is someone strong with AI coding tools who also codes for real.
- The setup is just him plus one coder per project. He does everything that isn't coding.
- He says he is the human who improves the output later. Early on, cost matters more than quality, and a human can be brought in once something works.
- He suggests corporations should run an internal "AI factory". Pick the most creative person, let them crank out about 20 ideas a week, expect one good one a month, then turn it into a business unit.

## SEO Bot and the "fund 100, double down on 3" content method

- He built SEO Bot (seobotai.com) as a true agent. You give it a URL, it researches the site, finds low-hanging keywords, checks Google search traffic, and proposes about 10 headlines with estimated clicks (for example 1k clicks for one article, 2k for another).
- After the user clicks proceed, it builds articles, sends them to the website and keeps doing so every day until turned off. The user only watches it work.
- His quality method works like a VC fund:
  1. The agent generates about 100 articles a month for one product. This is the "seed funding."
  2. On the last day of the month he checks analytics for the 3 best-performing articles.
  3. He spends an hour improving those 3 articles. This is the "series A and B."
- He argues that people who say AI content is low quality are right that AI is not as good as humans today. But in a first run quantity matters more than quality, and humans can add quality later.
- He expects quality to improve as new models arrive, for example "20% better and cheaper."
- On Unicorn Platform he lets people hire agents inside the platform. One example is an agent that pitches a site's potential sponsors and lands a sponsorship deal. He thinks agents and humans will compete for work.

## Directories, SEO and optimizing for LLMs

- He believes directories have a big future. LLMs answer well only if they have good data to pull from (their RAG and context), and a directory is months or years of curated work, not an evening of work like a blog post.
- He expects the race between LLMs to become a race for data, and says whoever supplies the best data "can charge for it" and monetize it.
- Evidence: he asked Grok for indie makers, it linked to Indie Maker List, which is his own directory, and he clicked through. He expects people to still visit directories after LLMs surface them, to browse directly.
- On optimizing for LLMs, he thinks the game is very similar to SEO. He rarely sees an LLM answer with a link that isn't on top of Google or Bing.
- His playbook is the same: good content, a link that answers the query, people sharing it, and backlinks. He thinks hacks might work briefly but "the best data will win" long term.
- He tested social signals. A new site with zero backlinks except one viral tweet got a lot of Google traffic the next day. He concluded Google counts backlinks from social media and popular profiles.
- He notes that Reddit, LinkedIn and X are being flooded with fake comments, and nobody knows what to do. He thinks X is best placed to fight this because it has its own data on who replies, follower counts and verification.

## Views on AI progress, kids and the future

- His most controversial belief is that we will not get to AGI using LLMs. He says LLM progress has gone nearly flat or linear since GPT-4 in 2023, and that the jump from GPT-3.5 to 4 was bigger than anything after.
- As an API user in his own tools, he hasn't seen a crazy change since GPT-4. He thinks the felt progress comes from the software and agents built on top.
- For kids, he thinks what they learn matters less than training their "brain muscle." He calls coding the best way, "almost like lifting weights," and addictive without the downside of video games.
- He promotes having kids to founders. He says AI frees up the time, and he lets his kids prompt Lovable and give random ideas that make his work more creative.
- His 2050 view is that robots and AI make everything very cheap and eventually free. Humans then invent games, and online games win over offline ones.
- He shares what he does daily on X, LinkedIn and Substack. Each month he posts his plans, and on the last day he posts what he got done.
