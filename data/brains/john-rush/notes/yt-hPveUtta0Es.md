## Why John says to start with directories

- He calls directories "one of the lowest risk ways to start a startup" and the best first attempt at an internet business. Starting with SaaS is "the worst idea" because failure is almost certain, and people then feel bad and go back to a job.
- In a directory, failure is hard. With a stable 5K visits a month on a B2B directory, you sell four or five ads and make 3-4K a month, and SEO tends to keep growing even if you do nothing.
- The reason: directory keywords are non-competitive. Nobody pays for them and no VC-backed company competes for them.
- Why directories will matter more: as content grows, quality matters more. People trust a well-curated directory with reviews and ratings, and will outsource decisions to directories as Google results fill with hacks.
- Examples of directories growing big: Starter Story is a directory of starter stories making "a million" a year. Product Hunt began as a plain list of products sent in a newsletter, with no voting.
- Realistic scale: about 5K MRR without luck. His average directory is there, and he believes 15-20K is possible with more time. He sees 20K as the current limit and treats directories as side projects, not main ones.

## Finding and validating a directory idea with keyword research

- He starts with keyword research, not gut feel: "even if you think that certain idea is great... other people might not think so."
- Tool: Google Keyword Planner (made for ads, but it shows keyword traffic). He spends hours in it every day throwing in ideas.
- Live example: "business ideas" has almost 1 million searches a month with low ad competition.
- The domain should exactly match the keyword. His directory "nextjs starters" was an exact match and had a lot of traffic within three months.
- If the exact domain is taken, use a synonym. Google now matches meaning, not exact words. For "business ideas" he tried small, little, tiny and micro, and bought mini business ideas for $9.
- He never buys expensive domains: "it's always available just be more creative."

## Building the first version fast, with no-code and AI

- He has coded for about 15 years and still says to go no-code. Reasons: you launch faster, and marketing (landing pages, experiments, A/B tests, blog, programmatic SEO) is built into modern CMS tools. In hardcoded Next.js or PHP, every marketing change is manual, and hiring someone to write pages is harder because it all lives in code.
- He builds on his own tool, Unicorn Platform, which he pivoted into a directory builder because most sites on it are now directories.
- Version one is only a page with a name and items. Ratings, user features and the rest come "later in the month or even a year," since they'd be empty anyway. He told a Discord member (directory makers group) who wanted 20 features for a lawyers directory to skip them.
- He uses AI to generate the structure, menu, text, subtitle and FAQ, then moderates the output for quality.
- Everything is built around SEO: more text, items and pages mean more chances to rank. Featured items can get longer pages than ordinary ones.
- Speed rule: "if it takes more than one evening then you're doing it wrong."
- FAQ method for free keyword research:
  1. Type "how do I..." into Google and read the autocomplete suggestions.
  2. Put each question in the directory's FAQ and answer it. Every FAQ title is a possible search query.
  3. Example: "how to start a business as a teenager" is a keyword he wouldn't have thought of and that is probably poorly served.
- Don't build full pages first. Add a quick item, watch Search Console, and only build a full page for queries that get traffic.
- General approach: do as little as possible to validate each assumption, then put real human time into the assumption that wins. AI is fine for experiments, but humans make the winner great.

## Promoting a new directory in the first days

He runs these four steps before investing in SEO. Their purpose is validation: if people see the directory but don't click, the idea or the domain is bad.

1. **Reddit.** Google "[topic] reddit", find threads where people ask for places or URLs, and reply with the link. For the business-ideas directory he found over 100 such threads. He plans only 1-3 replies a day to avoid bans, because Reddit has URL rules beyond subreddit rules.
2. **Other directories.** List yours in relevant directories. They are big and can send traffic, but they must be relevant.
3. **Social media replies.** Search X for the same questions and reply with the directory when it truly answers them. Example: Fireship's viral tweet about self-hosted software had about a million views, and John's reply with his self-hosted software directory got about 30-40K views.
4. **Social backlinks.** He says Google does look at social backlinks even though it doesn't say so. After that viral reply, his Google traffic grew the next day across all blogs and items. He says he has seen this about 50 times and that it beats website backlinks now.

He does this search himself each morning, not with a keyword-spy tool, because X search is good and more native.

## SEO for a new directory, including the AI agent and the publishing cap

- SEO is the key growth channel for directories and comes after the promotion steps above.
- He built an SEO agent that scrapes the directory, researches keywords, proposes article ideas with estimated traffic, writes outlines and articles, and publishes them back to the site. He programmed into it the actions he used to do by hand. Example output: "10 low cost business ideas for 2025".
- He prefers a dedicated tool over prompting Claude or ChatGPT, since SEO is also internal linking, structure and keyword research, not just content.
- Learning tip: watch what an SEO agent does and repeat it manually.
- Publishing cap for a fresh, low-rating domain: publishing many articles at once can look like spam and Google may never crawl them. Maximum about 50 articles in the first month, more in month two. He usually publishes about 10 in month one.

## The 30-day test, then the data phase

- Routine: day one to build and promote on Reddit and social, another day for SEO and more promotion, then stop and move to the next directory. Come back after 30 days and check traffic.
- Bet on 5-10 directories at once, so one failure isn't disappointing and SEO waiting time is shared. "Never fall in love with the idea."
- If there's no traffic after a month, he says it's very hard to fix, because something is fundamentally wrong (the domain, or a keyword that looked easy but isn't). He has never turned a zero-traffic directory around. Don't "push the dying horse."
- After 30 days he picks the best performer and moves to phase two: data quality. The first 30 days are only for validating SEO traffic and interest.
- Why data matters: experts who open the directory can quickly tell whether you know the topic. His LLM models directory was first filled by a hired person and was missing Mistral. An expert would see that and know the owner doesn't understand the space.
- When the data is high quality, influencers share the directory with their audiences, and it becomes organic as others follow. This happened with all his good directories.
- Ongoing work is small: keep running the SEO agent and check the space weekly or monthly.
- Next growth wave: be early on hot items. He scrolls Twitter daily, and when a new item is relevant to one of his directories he adds it quickly. Google has little else to show for that new item, so the directory can capture the search traffic.

## Ways to monetize a directory

1. **Banners and sponsor spots (strongest for B2B).** He emailed owners of GPTs on his all-GPTs directory with 30K items, offering to feature them at the top, and many said yes.
2. **Affiliate links**, best when items cost a lot. $10 items need many clicks; items at $500 to a few thousand pay well. His Next.js boilerplates directory uses affiliate links to expensive boilerplates.
3. **Selling data.** Never private data, only anonymized data or insights, sold to corporates interested in distributions and percentages.
4. **Paid listings.** Works where listed parties benefit, e.g. a nannies directory where each nanny pays to be listed (he mentions charging $50 or $500 per listing).
5. **Paid access for buyers.** Show the top five entries without contact data or photos, and charge to unlock the full database, either for life or monthly.
6. **Brand sponsors (his current favorite).** Once a directory is big, big brands pay for affiliation rather than clicks. DevHunt, the biggest after Product Hunt, has ongoing sponsorship deals with brands like Clerk and Supabase. They pay monthly or weekly and likely forever as traffic grows. Sponsor links are smaller and annoy users less than banners.
7. **Channel traffic to your own SaaS.** SEO is much harder for a SaaS with a random brand name competing against VC-funded companies bidding on keywords. He wins traffic on directories and sends it to his SaaS products, and says this was "the best idea I ever had in marketing." Almost all his traffic now goes to directories first, then to products.
8. **Sell the directory.** He sold his all-GPTs directory to cheap.ai (the name is spoken in the transcript as "cheap. a"), which had exactly the same audience.

## Buying and selling directories

- Why a SaaS should buy one: his math is a directory with 100K views a month grows to about a million views in a year. Channeling at least 3% gives about 30K clicks a year. Paying about 20K for it is under a dollar per click, which he says beats newsletter ads and other channels.
- Prices (from deals he has seen): start at 2K. Small ones go for 2-5K, average ones 15-20K, bigger ones up to about 100K. He has never seen one sell for over 100K. There is no revenue multiple system because revenue is not the main factor; audience and traffic quality are.
- Where to find them: Acquire.com doesn't accept directories because they lack revenue. Smaller marketplaces list them, and he named one as Micro-something (transcript says "Micron iio", uncertain). He says the best way is to research traffic, audience and countries on Similarweb and make a direct offer. Founders surprised by an offer may accept right away, while owners who list for sale often ask a lot.
