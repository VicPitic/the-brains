## Start with B2B pain people already complain about, and offer the solution on the spot

- John calls this the single best way to find an idea: find a pain many people mention that has no solution, then reply with a solution right where they complain (X, Reddit, blogs, comment sections).
- He prefers B2B pain over b2c. In b2c, "consumers are really bad at changing their habits", so even a solved pain often goes unused. In B2B, better tools mean more profit, so people change habits more readily.
- He says people don't see ideas because they don't browse with their eyes open. They should put their brain in "hunting mode" for weeks, build a long list, and not stop at the first idea.
- Example 1: a tweet about open source alternatives to paid tools led him to build an open source alternatives directory. He shipped it within hours and it went viral because he answered the problem fast.
- Example 2: a tweet asking who builds MVPs on a fixed price drew many "I'm looking for that person too" and "I'm that person" replies. He saw the whole marketplace in one thread and built a directory.
- If the build takes days or weeks, put up a waitlist. John says people overestimate intent: most won't click, they bookmark, and those who click are happy to see a waitlist.
- He says you can also mock up low-fi screenshots and say "leave your email to be told when it's launched". He sees validating before building as good for everyone.

## Tweet many ideas, and don't fear having no followers

- Pitching an idea publicly beats DMing it to one person: one person can't say if it's good, the market can.
- If you try 50 ideas, one will probably be good, and you don't know which.
- No followers is an advantage: if a tweet goes viral without an audience, the idea is good.
- His Devun launch (open-source Devin alternative, in his words) went viral with very few followers. Before it he had tried about 20 other ideas with no reaction.
- Bad tweets cost nothing: if nobody sees them, there is nothing to be embarrassed about, and you can delete them.
- He never schedules tweets, because he has to be inside Twitter to feel the right mode.

## Clone what works into a new market, or tweak a proven idea

- **Localize:** find a hot new tool in the US (new there means unlikely to exist elsewhere), then clone it in a market where it doesn't exist and localize it. Examples: Airbnb and Uber clones in local markets, or a "where to watch this movie" app built around a country's own streaming services.
- Clone the whole app, with all its best details, not just the idea. US apps usually have strong design and UX.
- Idea for a service: a daily email of the top 3 new apps from every country, each app sent only once and only apps missing from your market. He says it would also show trends outside your bubble.
- Travel is another source: he says nearly every founder story starts with "I went there, saw that, came back and did this".
- **Tweak a proven idea:** change the pricing (monthly to one-time, expensive to cheap, no trial to trial) or change the audience (dog lovers to cat lovers, makers to flower sellers). He has also made something more expensive and says it worked really well.
- Examples of cheaper clones: many Shopify-style competitors, and an open-source Bloomberg terminal alternative (Bloomberg was around $1K a month, in his words) that went viral.
- He would do this safe route for a first idea today, and says the longer something has no competitor, the lower the chance that anyone builds one.

## Keyword-led ideas using Google Keyword Planner and autocomplete

- John uses Google Keyword Planner (free), Google search and autocomplete only. Other tools make him less creative, because they suggest so much that he loses control.
- Spend hours, not five minutes. He says he spends at least an hour on it daily and sometimes a full day.
- Start with seed words you know. If you have none, ask ChatGPT for 100 variations. You need domain familiarity to judge ideas.
- Look for high traffic and low competition. Then Google the keyword: if the top 10 is only SEO blog posts, nothing real exists.
- Directory idea: he seeded "directory", saw "directory submission services", realized people pay for what he did by hand, and built listingbott.
- Emailer example: he searched email-related words, found a domain "s emailer" available (from "s email" with high traffic) and built a simple email tool for himself.
- Autocomplete trick: type part of a sentence such as "landing page builder for" and read the suggestions. He also mentions AnswerThePublic for questions.
- Domain exact-match plus related long-tail keywords can also win traffic.

## Automate your own job, and get a first customer from inside it

- John says millions of ideas live in jobs. Any job can be improved with software, and his own ideas came from automating his work (landing pages, blog articles). His end goal is to automate himself out of his work.
- Any job works, even manual labor. He gives a kindergarten teacher losing track of children's belongings and scheduling events as examples.
- Being an insider means you can use the tool yourself, and bosses usually say yes to a free trial.
- B2B tools don't need a catchy pitch: if they save an hour a day, users tell colleagues.
- Think about your first customer before the idea, because ideas never work as imagined without real users. Ads "almost never work" for an idea without its own traction.
- His first idea was a marketplace for student books at his university.
- Caution: solving your own problem is risky if you lack experience. The habit you replace must be weak enough, as with note apps vs Apple Notes.

## Single-feature micro tools and a descriptive name

- Find a popular tool, find where its users talk, and look for a group using only one feature. Talk to about 100 users: roughly 5 use just one feature.
- Build a micro SaaS with only that feature, price below the big tool, and name it plainly. His examples: listingbot, and a simple visit counter named for counting visits.
- Why it works:
  - the idea is validated before building
  - the name matches the search query, which helps SEO
  - it's easy to remember and recommend
- People recommend focused products: "people don't send you to a restaurant that also serves coffee, they send you to a coffee place".
- Example: browse Webflow users' profiles, find people building waitlists on it, and build waitlister.com. They feel they overpay and won't recommend Webflow for that.

## Ride tomorrow's wave, not today's hype

- John rode crypto and NFT waves and said it nearly worked, but the swings wrecked him. His friend's AI website builder boomed and then fell. He says most AI tools have about 10 times less traffic than a year ago.
- His rule now: "always work on things of tomorrow". Build in something small today that he expects to be big in a year or two.
- OpenSea was founded in 2017, was ignored until late 2019, and in 2020 was a huge share of NFT trades. The winners predicted early.
- His own bets: bad no-code (2018), boilerplates before people knew them, and directories, where he became an expert as the wave grew.
- Being early builds expertise, then content, then audience, and you know what to build. The market respects whoever was there before it got hot.
- He says he works about 5 hours a day of "exercise" work plus relaxed thinking, and has no stress because he has no competitors. His biggest regret is working too much.

## Start easy, play the long game, and use ChatGPT as a negative filter

- Remove the "next Steve Jobs" romance, at least for the first idea. Practice with safe, simple ideas first, as musicians start by playing covers.
- Indie makers are rarely successful on the first try. Build a directory first: try 4-5 directories and one will work. Then a micro SaaS with at least 1K revenue, then a serious SaaS, a platform, and a two-sided marketplace.
- Plan 5-10 years (he also says 10-20 years). Avoid running out of money (savings or a part-time job), health, or passion. Aim lower to get small wins and stay motivated.
- Copying: he wouldn't copy another indie maker's tool, but copying big corporations' products (such as Google Analytics) is fine. Copy ideas, not implementations. He sometimes avoids looking at competitors so he starts with a fresh mind.
- ChatGPT for ideas: he says its suggestions are predictable and average. If it suggests an idea, he removes it from his list, because it already exists.
