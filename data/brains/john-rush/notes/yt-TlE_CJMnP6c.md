## John's agent portfolio: revenue, team model and equity splits

- John says he is at around $3.5M ARR across his AI agents. He launched the first one a bit more than a year before this interview (Nov 2025). Before that he spent time "in the trenches just experimenting and building stuff."
- A few products carry most of the revenue: SEO Bot, Listing Bot, Unicorn Platform and a few others. The rest are free or just starting out.
- SEO Bot: almost $120K MRR, "been doubling month over month after the launch." It had about 6 months of closed beta with people he knew, then a public launch about 12-14 months before the interview. Plans are $19, $49, $199 and $499 a month, with about 4-5K customers. At the time of the Stripe screenshare the product had been at roughly $100K in a month.
- Team model: every product is a team of two. John comes up with the idea, builds the first MVP, runs the pre-sale, and designs the UX. Then he finds a co-maker, usually on Twitter or in his network, to build it with him. The co-maker builds and John promotes, and they help each other.
- SEO Bot is split 50/50 with his co-founder Vitalic (CTO, with John as CEO).
- Listing Bot is built by John alone. A co-founder handles moderation (watching the AI, fixing mistakes, catching hallucinations) on a 90/10 split in John's favor.
- He holds products forever and does not sell. His reasoning: each product is part of an ecosystem that drives people to his other tools, even if it earns nothing.

## Growth channel mix and the user-chat flywheel

- John's SEO Bot growth breaks down as roughly 30% SEO, 30% cross-promotion, 30% social media (building in public, users sharing) and 10% everything else.
- Dogfooding: he uses SEO Bot on all his other products, shares the results, and that helps both the product he shows and the tool he used. Example: he launched lmodels.org from zero and grew its clicks with his own tools. Because he keeps launching new products from zero, he can show fresh results each time.
- Cross-promo works because all his tools target the same audience. A customer of one product ends up using the others.
- He says he has never asked users to post screenshots or tag him, and thinks asking would "maybe 10x my revenue over a few months."
- Customer loyalty comes from listening. He says he has almost 1,000 chats going on with users, "I don't think anyone on earth has more chats than me." He comes back with fixes and improvements, and users become ambassadors.
- He builds for builders first because they give good feedback and effectively join the product team. Once they love it he raises the price and moves toward teams and companies.
- He treats directories as marketing channels. He owns 25-35 directories, most make no revenue, but they drive traffic to his other tools.

## Listing Bot: one-time pricing as a deliberate start

- The tool does two things: deep research to find every web directory relevant to a product (ranked by relevance and domain rating), and web automation that registers and fills out the forms to list the product.
- Directory database: about 20K directories now, with at least 500 new ones added each month. He estimates about 100,000 directories exist, including niche ones for e-commerce stores, mobile apps and pet shops. The goal is to find them all.
- Revenue: over $50K a month, one-time payments, "basically thousand people buying it every month." There is no recurring revenue, so it needs new buyers every month.
- He ignored advice that he must have recurring plans. It began as an experiment with a Stripe payment link, so users pay before seeing the next step. He calls this a "really high bar" that few people pass, and it still brings in a lot of users.
- Version two, coming soon, lowers the bar: $50 a month for 20 directories listed each month.
- His strategy is "just like Tesla started with Roadster": start expensive with a really good service (AI plus human review of everything), then reduce human labor, go fully autonomous, drop the price, and let users moderate themselves.

## Capping products at about $100K MRR and fixing support first

- John's pattern: grow a product to $50K MRR, pause and move to other tools, come back and reach $100K, then pause again.
- He deliberately avoids going much past $100K because it would force him to move upmarket to enterprise clients who want demo calls and his time. He wants products fully async. He returns only once he can automate the bottleneck.
- SEO Bot's bottleneck is support: "almost 80% of our time go for support." Customers connect their CMS and "everything just falls apart all the time." Nothing new gets built or marketed.
- His fix is a support agent that acts rather than just answers: it tries to fix the problem, gets into the database and changes things.
- SEO Bot sells credits that customers load and spend on articles. Revenue comes through two Stripe channels: self-serve and enterprise invoices.
- Revenue per employee: with two people on SEO Bot, the host computed roughly $720K a year per employee.

## Tiny Ads: building an ad network because paid growth never worked

- John says he had only done organic growth. Every time he tried paid on Google or Facebook, it never worked, so he built his own B2B ad network for small B2B startups so he could spend on growth.
- Result: it launched April 15 and reached $5K MRR and $10K revenue in 35 days, with almost 1,000 publishers joining to serve ads.
- He ignored people who said an ad network is "a dream for big guys." He compares it to 2022, when people said developers would never leave VS Code for an AI coding tool.
- His view on the model: Google's ad game runs on VC money, where spending $10 to get $5 back is fine. He asked why not build a system where you pay $1 and get two back and the other side makes money too: "you serve the ads you get paid. The other side just pays for the traffic."

## Why John bootstrapped and built Mars X first

- Background: studied computer science, AI and UX, then spent 10 years in VC-backed companies. He started seven as CTO with co-founders in Norway, then went to San Francisco (500 Startups, Altimus).
- Why he left: he felt his whole game was to serve investors and chase exits, when he wanted to serve users and chase a legacy, a generational company that lasts beyond him. As a bootstrapper he doesn't optimize for an exit or next round. He owns it all, funds himself from profits, and uses profits to build the next product.
- Vision from 2020: an ecosystem of 100 tools run with almost no people. His first step was an AI coding tool, Mars X (marsx.dev), because building 100 products otherwise requires thousands of people.
- He spent 2-3 years building it alone on weekends and evenings while running a VC-backed startup, had a working version in 2022, then used it to build all his later products.
- End goal: every tool a busy bootstrap founder needs across building, operating, growing and monetizing.

## SEO advice: page-level backlinks, internal links and AI search

- Context: the host's site has about 35,000 programmatic "[company] revenue" pages. They rank well but clicks fell about 50% as AI results raised impressions. John says AI made it easier to enter the SEO game, so far more people are competing and traffic is harder to win.
- LLM search: almost all LLMs now use live internet search (mostly Bing), so ranking on page one of Bing means showing up in most of them. They no longer need to be in a training set. John's long-term bet is that all LLMs will search the web in a similar way, so focusing on topping Google solves the other problems too: "the SEO game is also LLM game at the same time."
- Page rating beats domain rating: a site with a poor domain rating can have a high rating on a specific page, and Google looks at the page's URL rating first. Ahrefs data for a URL is often incomplete, so don't trust a zero.
- Backlink tactic: pick a query where a competitor outranks you, then build backlinks to that exact URL using the exact anchor text of the search query. John says he has done this several times and eventually took the number one spot. He calls it "the most typical serious work you can do on SEO": go page by page and beat competitors with backlinks to that exact page.
- For programmatic SEO at scale, he suggests owning a network of websites and automating the backlink part.
- Internal links: "go crazy with links." More internal links is better than fewer. Create blog posts that link back to the company profile pages, and link any mention of a company name (like Zapier) to its revenue page, giving hundreds of internal links. He noted dynamic pages may not be seen by Google.
- Ranking logic: good content gets you onto page one. To reach the top you need precise anchor links to the URL, both internal and external.
- Citations from AI tools may be nofollow links and won't necessarily send traffic.
