## Depth over breadth: learn every founder skill on one project

- John says the common indie hacker belief that launching many MVPs will eventually produce a winner is wrong. A founder who builds MVPs for 20 years only learns to build MVPs.
- He argues founders must learn all the skills: building, SEO, cold sales, UX, design, copywriting, marketing, distribution. With every skill, the odds of success go from about 2% to 20%, then 50%, eventually close to 100%.
- Experienced founders like himself, Peter Levels and Marc Lou rarely launch projects that fail, because they have learned the skills.
- Tactic: try at most three projects, pick the one you like most, and go deep for years. Once the product is built there is no more code to write, so you are forced to do marketing, content, SEO and sales.
- If you do launch several projects, push a different skill in each one (development, design, SEO).
- His own path: one cinema software company for 10 years, then one project (Unicorn Platform) for almost 2 years with no other launches, then more projects. He did not build 24 projects at once. "Never build second project unless your first project is successful."
- Imitate the process, not the outcome. He stopped posting MRR about a year ago because it makes beginners feel they are missing out and copy the wrong thing.

## When to quit a project and how to read early signals

- John says quitting too early means success depends on luck.
- Give a project a few months and watch the signals. If after 3 months you have shared it widely and nobody signed up even once, the idea is bad or you can't present it well. Either way, drop it.
- Most makers build something that already exists and makes money, so the idea is already validated. The real test is whether you can promote and communicate it.
- If you get some signals, keep hammering: change the wording, narrow the features, pivot the positioning slightly.
- Example: an analytics tool becomes "analytics for e-commerce store owners". Same technology, different positioning, and it starts spreading in that community.
- Waitlists and MVPs are good at the start to test whether you can promote the project. After you reach the market, focus on making the product really good instead of building another one.

## Build what the business needs, not what you like

- John's tweet: the best way to fail as an indie hacker is to keep doing things you like. Successful people say they love what they do, but not that every daily task is their favorite.
- Stages he describes:
  1. Talk to customers first. You can't predict what people want, "if you could predict what other people want, you could predict stock market."
  2. Then build an MVP and put it in their hands, because people can't imagine a product from a description.
  3. Expect them to hate the first version. Send new versions "ideally tomorrow", then the day after, every day, until they get happier.
- Founders who "worked hard for a year and it didn't work" usually worked on the wrong small tasks (a favorite SDK, a new Tailwind kit), not the wrong project.
- Customers don't buy features, they buy solutions to problems: saving time or money. Features are the vehicle.
- Distribution vs product: he used to think distribution mattered more. He now says it's false. "If my product is not doing well, it's because the product is bad, not because the marketing is bad." People who say they have an amazing product but are bad at marketing have a product that isn't amazing.

## Pricing: a $49 price tag filters for real need

- John says if customers pay $49 a month, they actually need the product and their business benefits from it.
- At lower prices ($9, $19), people buy products they don't need. If people won't pay $49, it's not because it's too expensive, it's because they don't need it. Making it cheaper just gets you customers who still don't need it.
- Customers who don't need the product don't share it, don't become ambassadors and give poor feedback.
- Indie hackers rarely pay more than about $20 a month, which is too little for a sustainable business, so you need many users. He says people need to pay at least $50 for the product to be sustainable.
- Real businesses with a real use case pay for expensive software, don't tweet about it, and simply use it to solve a pain. Those customers are outside the indie hacker community.
- Product Hunt and indie hacker testimonials: friends and fans are testers, not users, even when they pay. A good project has users from outside the indie hacker community.

## Lessons from buying a product for $800k (Unicorn Platform)

- Goal: enter the bootstrap world fast by buying an existing popular project instead of building from scratch. He followed Laravel projects for a year, picked a small one with good press, DMed the founder and agreed the sale on Telegram.
- Problem 1, churn: indie hacker users are attached to the founder, not the product. When ownership changed, churn hit 15% a month, which means losing all users in about 6-7 months. There was no SEO traffic and word of mouth was too weak.
- Problem 2, stability: the product had constant downtime that nobody had reported because the previous buyers had no real users. When he brought in real users with real websites, they left. At one point he brought 10 users in and 10 left the next day.
- His conclusion: it was his own failed due diligence, not the seller's fault. Most users of an indie product are friends and fans who never complain and don't run serious businesses.
- Fix: it took more than 2 years to stabilize the product and build organic channels outside the indie community. It now has 250,000 users (100,000 when he decided it was good enough to launch other projects), low churn and stable revenue.
- He plans to compete head-to-head with Webflow and Framer next year.
- Exits: he doesn't congratulate people on exits. About 90% of projects die after being sold, usually to inexperienced buyers who can't maintain them. Indie hackers selling to each other creates no outside economy, so he now calls himself a startup founder instead of an indie hacker.
- Audience: posting "zero to 30k MRR in 3 months" content attracts people who want fast money and cancel after 3 months. He now aims content at founders playing a long game.

## SEO: traffic plus better copy, and how to start

- John says SEO matters for two reasons: traffic, and the fact that writing for search intent makes your page text match how people describe the problem, so the copy converts better.
- In some of his products over 50% of traffic comes from SEO, in others about 10%. High-intent search traffic can convert very well. He started SEO only the year before and regrets not doing it earlier.
- SEO is a lot of simple, repetitive daily work: content, keyword optimization, backlinks (to the site and subpages), internal linking, technical readiness. He compares it to going to the gym and says it's boring for developers, which is why he built SEO Bot AI to automate it.
- Recommended method: AI plus human. Use a tool as the draft maker and act as chief editor. He produces content with SEO Bot, then manually improves the pieces that get views.
- How to start: connect any AI blog generator (there are hundreds), generate 10, 20, 50 or 100 articles, let it run for a month or two and check Search Console. He says 10 AI articles won't get you punished by Google, and seeing traffic is addictive, so you'll want to do more.
- Long term: SEO is zero-sum, so if everyone uses AI content you need manual work on top.

## Hiring, social media, and not quitting your job

- People: he has employed hundreds over his life. After 3-5 years employees lose steam because it's a job to them, not their business. Someone who must care for a decade has to be a co-founder. His current goal is to automate more so he needs fewer people, who then do better work on less volume.
- Social media: he used to think it was a waste of time for businesses like his and only started using it about a year before. It opened doors to people, friends, partnerships and marketing. He says new founders should be social media native from day one.
- Content experiments: he tried drama recaps in a fast, funny stand-up style on X and got millions of views. He studies stand-up comedians and meme makers to find new formats, since copied formats lose attention. He follows Hormozi's content daily.
- Tools: a chat AI (ChatGPT, now Grok for real-time data) as a co-founder, and Discord for chats, project management, support and community, because a chat feed keeps all context in one place.
- Advice not to follow: don't quit your job. No one replaces a salary with indie income in 3 months, and zero to revenue in 3 months is only possible for people who have done it for years. Keep your job and build on evenings and weekends. If you can't get mild traction in two days a week, five days won't help, and less time makes you focus on the right things.
