## From VC-backed startups to bootstrapping: John's path and why it fit him

- John's first startup was bootstrapped when he was about 15-16. It was a marketplace where smart students helped weaker students (math help, etc.), with payments, filters, accounts and requests. Within two years it had thousands of transactions daily. A bigger competitor bought it. He calls it luck: "it was just a student project for me."
- He didn't know the word "bootstrapping" then and assumed bootstrappers were people who couldn't raise money.
- After the sale he did what everyone did: incubator, VC money, round after round, exit or shut down, then repeat. That ran about 10-12 years in Norway, riding the wave of Norwegian government startup grants that began around 2010.
- He discovered the bootstrap and indie maker world in 2022 and wishes he had done it earlier. Being easy to stay on the VC path gave him no reason to look at other options.
- He says he is "great at being Scrappy" and at producing a lot with few people. That is bad for VC startups, where headcount is the KPI and founders pretend to be bigger than they are (stock photos of "team" members, advisors counted as staff). He felt "like a fish in the wrong ocean."
- Today he runs 24 projects, mainly for busy founders, all bootstrapped, and plans to launch more.
- Some people really do enjoy the VC style: conferences, partnership calls, managing large teams. John says most tech founders are introverts who enjoy building products, and he knows VC-backed founders who privately say the same thing but can't say it publicly because of investors.

## Product-first vs company-first founders, and being honest about size

- John separates people who love building products from people who love building companies (culture, systems, departments, headcount). Bootstrappers are usually the former.
- In bootstrapping you can be a company of one and be honest ("I'm in bed, I'll answer tomorrow"). Customers accept that. In VC mode, faking size makes customers expect Microsoft-level responsiveness.
- Doing marketing, building and support yourself is freedom, not a defect.

## Win by over-delivering to users, not by buying growth

- John contrasts the models. In VC-backed startups the goal is CAC lower than LTV so you can pour money into growth. In bootstrapping, spending money on growth gets you nowhere because you can't pour more money in.
- His bootstrap rule: before roughly 10K-20K MRR, deliver far more to users than they pay for ("go negative on ARPU"). Users who fall in love become the growth engine: they recommend you, mention you online, bring friends and give support.
- Investors push VC-backed companies toward moves that serve the next round, often unrelated to making users happy. That was his frustration.
- Be hungry for money. Every dollar early is hard, and you must be genuinely excited by small revenue to reach the next milestone. He says being very rich early killed his excitement for small startup revenue. 1K and 2K MRR felt exciting again once he bootstrapped. He warns against losing motivation by comparing your profit to other makers' bigger numbers.
- He doesn't watch Stripe notifications. He checks the bank balance, and as long as it's above a threshold he knows things are fine. He checks revenue mainly to create content about it.
- He gets his strongest motivation from thank-you messages from users, not from money.
- Love the journey, not the outcome: "you cannot control the outcome," and he knows people who sold companies and never found themselves again. Don't postpone happiness.

## Why small niche software from bootstrappers will beat VC-backed bloat

- John has bet his whole business on the idea that the world wants "small solutions for small problems."
- VC math forces a market of $1B-$10B, so products become generic and feature-heavy. They have to sell to large teams, which means collaboration and permissions, and that bloats the product for solos and small teams. VC products end up competing on identical things.
- Bootstrapped products position slightly differently (audience, stage, segment), so more niche problems get solved. A 5K-15K MRR niche product would never be funded by VCs but serves a real need.
- Specialized software eventually beats generalist software for each use case. He has watched the bootstrap world for about two years and knows nobody who hit 10K MRR and stopped growing, while he knows many VC startups that hit 10K MRR and died.
- His prediction: instead of 10,000 big VC-backed products there will be 1 million small micro-tools from bootstrappers. AI and tooling help small founders more than large VC teams, and this is accelerating.
- Customers are shifting. A year ago his customers were nearly all solopreneurs. Now half of new customers are mid-sized or VC-backed companies, including about 30 YC companies across his products.
- Buyer psychology he sees: customers distrust vendors that raise money, join YC or hire many people, because support gets worse and failure odds are high. In bootstrapping, the default bet is that the vendor stays in business. The opposite is true in the VC world.
- Open source as a sales tool: when companies worry about a solo founder dying, he says the product is fully open source and they can run it themselves. Open source also keeps pricing fair, since users can self-host if you triple the price.

## SaaS OS and open-source forking with royalties

- Problem: many tiny tools mean many logins, dashboards and subscriptions. John is building a "SaaS OS", an operating system for micro-tools with one account, one subscription and shared settings, where tools talk to each other. He is also building an open API standard so any micro-SaaS can plug in natively. Sign-up becomes a button press instead of re-entering company info and logo.
- Benefits: users get less friction; makers skip billing portals, marketing and operations and focus on their product. He says the SaaS OS solves distribution while makers fill the ecosystem.
- His build playbook: build every essential product an indie maker needs across the journey (build, operate, monetize, grow, market), such as no-code tools, analytics and SEO. Use each on his own projects first; once it works across all of them, launch it publicly. He wants about 100 tools in the OS before launching it, not five. About a year and a half in, he estimated it was halfway through and needed about another year.
- His developer system, Morse X, uses "micro-apps" that can be forked and nested (user login, ratings, CMS and so on). A royalty model would pass revenue down the tree to everyone whose code is used, like music royalties. Forkers add a little on top and share revenue with the original author.
- He expects software to be open source by default ("closed source by default" will sound odd). Code is no longer the hard part; deciding what to build, distribution and UX are. He open-sourced DevHunt and no competitor cloned it, while it gained contributors and trust.
- Example use: instead of one no-code builder for everything, launch 20 forks, each for a use case (cinemas, bakeries), without maintaining 20 separate repos. He expects thousands of slightly different versions of the same software, shortening the innovation cycle from years to weeks.
- Distribution and building are different modes. Doing both is "torture." Forks with royalties let a great builder and a great distributor each do their part without needing to be co-founders.

## Predicting trends, creators vs consumers, and the Indie Maker Village

- John never builds for an existing hype or an obvious trend. He compares it to buying a stock that's already going up. He builds for small, non-obvious, growing trends that few understand yet, so that when the trend grows it carries the product with it. He credits good intuition for spotting what's growing.
- His trend: people who build consume less ("the more you produce the less you consume"). He expects a growing "creator class" whose values and routines clash with a consumption-optimized world, who limit cheap dopamine and want their kids to become producers.
- The Indie Maker Village is his long-term bet (5, 10 or 20 years): a place where the mainstream agenda is creating, a "Silicon Valley for the bootstrap world." He wants it open source with contributors, not a money-making venture. He sees side creation (building a chair) as improving primary work.
- He believes cheap dopamine (YouTube, Twitter, shopping, entertainment) weakens the brain's ability to do difficult multi-day tasks.

## Meaning, money and when to start

- John defines being rich as a strong family and a community of friends who share your interests. Numbers are irrelevant as long as you can keep doing it. Family is a renewable energy source when business gets repetitive over 20 years. Money is a tool to build more business.
- He says he loses at investing (crypto, stocks, property, startups, currencies, 20+ investments, small angel checks of $10K-$100K). He sold Nvidia and Tesla early and lost Bitcoin held on a Norwegian exchange that was hacked. He treats this as not a lesson about investing skill but says product building is where his luck lies.
- Owning a product gives meaning: you earn from the product rather than from hours, the work shrinks over time, and your name is on something useful. He prefers 10K/month from a product over a $1M bank balance. He thinks more people will want income not tied to hours rather than billionaire status.
- On starting now: he argues against saving for years to free yourself later. His lawyer friend (age 38) spends almost no time on his indie project, planning to start after years of saving. John's view: a 50-year-old who has only worked a day job rarely develops the innovation skill, and exchanging your best years for later freedom is a bad bet. His suggestion was to work two days less and use the time on the project.
- He is skeptical of FIRE for similar reasons: many bets must go right, and people who retire early often lose their purpose.
- Computer-job health is a real constraint: he says nearly none of the nearly 100 people in his companies are healthy, so don't assume you'll be fit later.
- Shipping issues hit him hard. When a product is down overnight for US customers while he sleeps in Europe, he feels the damage is permanent. He also says the rollercoaster of crisis and fix produces stronger happiness than products that never break.
- He shares his whole business openly (growth, marketing, building) and plans to turn about 30,000 of his tweets into an open-source book. He answers 80-100 Twitter DMs a day and says Twitter is where to reach him.
