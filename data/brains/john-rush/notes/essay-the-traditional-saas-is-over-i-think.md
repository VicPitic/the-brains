## How John replaced 30 SaaS dashboards with one AI agent

- In about 30 days, John says 99% of his habits changed. Instead of logging in to Stripe, his bank, Gmail and about 30 other SaaS tools, he uses one AI agent connected to all of them via MCPs.
- Morning routine: he opens one chat and asks, "Show me yesterday's revenue, any failed payments, and all questions and bug reports from my customers." No 14 tabs, no context switching.
- His view of the traditional UI: it was a layer between him and the database. AI chat can now do the reads, writes, updates and deletes.
- When he needs a chart or table, he asks the AI to generate it on the fly, or to build a small tool for that use case.
- He vibe coded a "super dashboard" that works with all the tools he uses.
- Key insight: he only uses about 5% of any given tool, and the other 95% is noise. So he took the 5% he needs from each tool and put it in one dashboard.
- He chose his past products mainly for UX. At the database level, he says, products are roughly the same or cover the same use cases.

## Why UX, brand and switching costs stop being a moat

- If UX is no longer a moat, John doesn't care who provides the API. He takes whoever gives the better price.
- He would want his agent to check daily for cheaper alternatives to his tools. If one exists, it would cancel the existing tool, download his data and move it to the new tool, with no migration headaches.
- He expects tools to race to the bottom toward price zero, and says he wouldn't even know which tools he's using.
- His examples: he doesn't care whether email goes through Resend, Postmark or SES, or whether the database is Supabase, PlanetScale or Neon. He only cares that it works and costs the least.
- His conclusion: "Brand loyalty is gone. Switching costs are gone."
- An agent doesn't care about a landing page, onboarding flow, clever feature naming or freemium funnel.
- He says tech has no defensibility left, and that tech is going "from 99% profit margins to 1%."
- Warning: a CRUD app with a pretty UI sold as a SaaS business is in trouble, and "that era is ending."
- The one moat he names is the LLM models themselves (he says Opus 4.6 beats everything right now and he'd pay whatever they ask), but he calls that a game for the big players, not for founders like him.

## What still has a moat: collective data and regulated industries

**Data that improves with more users**
- His agent can switch him from Resend to SES in 5 minutes, move his database from Supabase to Neon overnight, or move him off Stripe to a competitor.
- It cannot bring along the collective intelligence of millions of users. His example is Stripe's fraud detection, trained on billions of transactions across millions of businesses: "That data IS the product."
- His rule: if a product genuinely gets better because more people use it, agents can't commoditize that. "Everything else has no MOAT."

**Boring regulated businesses**
- He would let an agent auto-switch his email provider without telling him, but not his bank, healthcare provider or insurance underwriter.
- In these markets the barrier to entry "isn't code, it's licenses, compliance, and trust."

## Sell the result, not the tool

- John no longer wants to pay $49/month for a social media management tool. He used about 3 features out of 200, and his agent now does those 3 things anyway.
- What he would pay for is a result, for example "we'll get you 50 qualified leads this month from LinkedIn." He doesn't care how it's done or which APIs are used behind the scenes.
- Pricing flips "from 'pay for access' to 'pay for outcomes.'"
- He argues you can charge more than SaaS this way, because you compete on "did it work or not," not on features or price.
- His reasoning: his agent will optimize tool spending toward zero, but it will pay for results all day, because results have a clear ROI.

## Selling full repos for a one-time payment

- For most of his vibe coding projects, John says he'd happily pay $199 for a code repository. He would drop it into Claude Code and have it customize the code, starting from a good default instead of from zero.
- He expects the market for such repos to be big. The recurring payment disappears, but selling 10k copies a month at $199 each is $2M/mo, which he calls "pretty achievable."
- His logic: most users will stop paying monthly for their SaaS, so paying once for a repo becomes an easy decision.

## The agent layer is the next product

- John built the "collapse 30 tools into one chat" setup for himself, but he is a technical founder who has shipped products for years. Non-technical people need someone to do it for them.
- He calls this the agent or orchestration layer and asks whether it can be productized. He thinks it can, and that this is where the next wave of billion-dollar companies comes from.
- His historical pattern: whatever replaces a dying thing in tech is about 10x bigger. Websites killed newspapers but created Google, mobile killed websites but created the app economy, and AI agents are killing SaaS but creating something "we don't even have a name for yet."
- His advice: "stop building tools. Start building what comes after."
- Summary of the opportunities he names: the agent/orchestration layer, one-time-payment code repos, products with data network effects, regulated industries, and outcome-priced services.
