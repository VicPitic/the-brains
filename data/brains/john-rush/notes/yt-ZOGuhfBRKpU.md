## Why John started building directories and why they last

- Friends and partners told John for about five years that directories were "yesterday's thing". In 2023 he decided to build one anyway, because directories kept surviving and every year some new directory got the traffic while he owned none of them.
- His first directory was a GPT directory, built right after the OpenAI demo day. It went viral, and he then built many more.
- Early on, he says, only about five people in the world had built more than one directory, so whatever you shipped beat nothing. Now there are many boilerplates, tools and no-code tools (he counts "hundred of them"), plus directory courses from people who never built one.
- His conclusion: when a space gets crowded, the best quality and best work wins and the rest lose.
- He compares "directories are dead" to the "Drop Shipping is dead" argument. A directory is just a website model, and as long as websites exist, directories will too.

## Spend the most time on thinking, not building

- John says he spent months or years thinking before building anything. Once he started building, it went fast.
- His process: whenever he sees something relevant on Twitter, he adds it to the idea and keeps shaping it until he knows what the directory must be built around.
- The core questions he settles are:
  - What should the directory be organized around?
  - What should it sort by?
  - What is the most important missing information about this type of item that people can't find anywhere?
- If you put that missing information in one place, people can judge the whole list well.
- He says he struggles to explain this to people who arrive with an idea and just go build it.
- Scraping, enriching and implementation are "so easy nowadays" compared with the thinking.

## How LLMs treat directories and why they help them

- John argues LLMs make basic information easy to find, so demand moves to the best-quality source on a topic. LLMs and directories improve together.
- Three years ago, some directories won just by having some items. Now low-quality data comes from an LLM, so winning directories need really good data.
- A directory owner has one topic and unlimited time for research, so LLMs will always lag on depth.
- His prediction: deep search is slow (about 50 seconds) and costly for LLMs, so they will increasingly pull from one place known to be great on a topic and act as a proxy, citing the source.
- He thinks directories benefit more than blogs from this. If someone asks for dog parks in their town and the LLM cites a directory, there is "almost 100% chance" they click through to browse and filter. A blog link only loosely intersects the query, so people rarely click it.

## How to get referenced by LLMs (LLM SEO)

John separates two cases.

- **LLM without web access (training data only):**
  - Every LLM has its own training set. OpenAI used a lot of Reddit and has a deal with Reddit that he describes as a "billion dollar deal".
  - He verified this on his own products. The ones the LLM knew about were the ones he had on Reddit, a "100% correlation".
- **LLM with web search (Grok on X, Perplexity, OpenAI):**
  - They run a regular search, often Bing because of the Microsoft deal. They take the top 10-20 results, crawl them, put the content in context and answer.
  - Grok does the same with tweets.
- **His checklist for all LLMs:**
  - Be on Reddit.
  - Rank on Bing.
  - Rank on Google.
  - Be present on social media, e.g. a tweet answering "what are the best dog parks" that links to you or describes you.
- He says SEO now brings LLM visibility as a by-product, because LLMs only started using search about three months before this recording.
- Caveat: the LLM doesn't just take the top-ranked result and doesn't trust ranking blindly, since that would be easy to manipulate. The picking logic is live prompting that can change any day, so the basics (Reddit plus top rankings) are what hold up.
- He sees user-generated content as the signal LLMs trust most, because it is hard to manipulate.
- He has seen social impact on SEO directly. When a tweet for one of his directories goes viral, the site jumps in traffic the next day.

## Directories as a game for millions of people

- John argues the main reason people join a product is to be entertained. He thinks most directory builders already have income elsewhere and want to do something on their own terms.
- Job of the tool or course: keep them in the process, one thing at a time, "not too complicated not too easy" so they neither feel overwhelmed nor postpone it.
- Some people will be entertained, some will actually build something, and nobody knows in advance which.
- He thinks the audience for directories may be bigger than for SaaS or agents, because convincing someone with a main job to build SaaS is much harder than convincing them to build a directory.
- Google Search Console impressions keep beginners moving. Impressions arrive long before clicks, so analytics alone shows nothing early on. He sees people with no earlier success get excited about their first Google traffic.
- Even about $300 a month is "amazing money" in many places, and it is enough to stay in the game and build more.

## Winning a niche and building something to be proud of

- John asks whether you can become the best educator in a narrow sub-niche, directories. He says it's "100%" possible to be number one, because the existing competitors are the old generation and the market wants a new generation to own it.
- His own directory course and tools sell every day and bring income, but he says it is "nothing spectacular" and not something he would be proud of at 70.
- He says he knows how to turn it into something bigger, but it takes more work than he can spend right now.
- His goal is bigger than revenue: own the space worldwide and let millions of people run a small business of their own. He says that if that isn't the end goal, "then it's just like project to make some money but it's not that interesting".
