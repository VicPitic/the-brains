## Why the Stripe acquisition of LemonSqueezy is mostly good for indie makers

- John sees three upsides:
  - Indie makers can now sell to famous companies. Micro-acquisition platform deals are "usually below 1 million" and rare. He guesses this deal is worth "maybe 100 maybe 50 maybe 200" (million), a guess, since the price isn't public. He thinks it will inspire people to bootstrap in order to sell later.
  - Building in public on Twitter makes makers visible to VC-backed startups, investors and corporations, not just fellow makers. He expects acquisitions like this to become normal.
  - Makers who join a corporation keep their indie connections and become a bridge into that world. He cites KP joining Paddle.
- The main downside is cultural. Many bootstrappers privately would raise VC money or sell if the offer were good, but don't say so because it "makes us look cheap".
- Selling a payments company is easy to defend. It is "nearly impossible to bootstrap a bank or a payment provider".
- He expects the bubble to be "happy and proud", like a friend getting accepted "into the cool league".

## Why Stripe bought LemonSqueezy and what happens next

- John thinks Stripe bought the team, not the audience. Stripe's pitch was about merchant of record, and the internet had been asking Stripe to handle tax, which Stripe didn't know how to solve.
- He guesses LemonSqueezy people will build merchant-of-record and tax handling inside Stripe, and the LemonSqueezy brand may disappear.
- He also thinks Stripe wants to stop merchant-of-record products like Paddle and LemonSqueezy from owning the customer relationship. Most LemonSqueezy users don't know Stripe powers it, so LemonSqueezy could swap Stripe out at any point.
- He says "at least 90% of all acquisitions kill the whole underlying product they bought", so he is pessimistic about LemonSqueezy's survival.
- A possible exception: Stripe may keep LemonSqueezy as a separate entity with extra KYC, so it can keep serving countries Stripe can't, such as India.
- Taxes are the biggest unanswered question for indie makers. He says most "simply ignore the taxes and hope for the best".
- With Stripe, Paddle and LemonSqueezy all big or VC-backed, he sees room for a new bootstrapped payment or merchant-of-record product to fill the gap.
- He prefers bootstrapped vendors because "founders never really change their vision", while a VC-backed company can hire a new CEO who changes everything.
- For a boilerplate today he would default to Stripe: best known, best API, and tax isn't relevant to beginners yet.

## Small products on top of big platforms: wrappers as a strategy

- Indie makers win with small products. LemonSqueezy used Stripe as the processor and built a layer on top because it had few resources.
- That makes such products attractive acquisition targets for the platform they build on. Shopify acquires apps and folds them in as features, and he thinks OpenAI-wrapper startups will be bought too.
- He rejects the idea that being a "wrapper" is bad: "everything is a wrapper and the higher level it is the smarter you are". Users care about the experience, not the underlying layer.

## The indie bubble as a free accelerator

- John argues the indie maker community is a launchpad, not a dead-end bubble. Makers sell to each other first.
  - They are good early users: critical, they pay, they use the product themselves and give feedback.
  - The iteration cycle is fast. You can launch and iterate daily, and once the product is great it moves outside via SEO and social media.
- Plausible is his example. It started in the indie world and is now used by many VC-backed startups too.
- He compares it to YC, where startups first target other YC companies. He calls the indie bubble "better than YC bubble" because there are more people, companies and product types, and no lottery or acceptance limit. It is "a global decentralized acceleration program" that is free.
- He thinks the Stripe deal and press coverage like TechCrunch will make indie hacking much more popular.

## SaaS boilerplates: why he is bullish

- He calls boilerplates "the best thing that happened to the indie maker world" and says SaaS innovation speed has maybe gone "10x".
- He expects boilerplates to get niche-specific: no-code landing page builders, PM tools, chat apps. He says "don't do the generic one" because you won't beat Marc Lou's ShipFast, which makes about 100K per month (the host's figure, which John did not dispute). Build for a niche instead.
  - Examples he sees launching on DevHunt: boilerplates for directories, Chrome extensions and iOS apps. Many show MRR.
- Developers used to be too arrogant to pay for someone else's code. Now they want to save time.
- Boilerplates suit individuals, not teams, so VC-backed teams won't adopt them soon. That helps indie makers move almost as fast as startup teams.
- He would personally take a boilerplate and move fast, even if he doesn't know all the code, because "the advantage looks heavier than the disadvantage".
- Iteration speed matters for newcomers. One iteration a week versus one a month is a 4x difference. People who don't use boilerplates will quit sooner because they won't get users in time.
- For beginners, the real value is the "aha" moment: a launched product on a domain with a brand within a week or two. For experienced makers it matters less, since the bottleneck becomes decision-making, not development.
- His platform Mars has many micro-boilerplates you can combine. A single boilerplate rarely fits all needs. He says he had this idea in 2018.
- Future makers will mostly be attention-winners, not introverted builders. With no TechCrunch, VCs or accelerators picking winners, you have to win an audience yourself on social media, and the extroverts will win it. He thinks "maybe 90%" of millions of makers will be attention-winners.

## Selling on a famous maker's brand, and why great products win

- Selling a boilerplate through the founder's success story is ethical in his view. It is how brands work: the James Bond ad for BMW, Apple's "be different".
- The line is a 100% guarantee. Courses promising 10K a month are "pretty much just lying".
- Being inspired, educated and kept in the mood of making stuff is a big part of entrepreneurship. He credits Marc Lou with doing the most to keep people making things.
- On free alternatives like T3: details make the product different, like a Chinese phone that looks like an iPhone. He hasn't tried ShipFast, but expects its developer experience is better because Marc Lou gets thousands more feedback loops.
- He says he has "never seen great product without users". He tries more products than anyone on Earth, daily, and sees UX and landing-page quality go together: a really bad landing page means a bad product "like 99%".
- In the bootstrap world you win by real marketing, not ads, so great products almost always rise and bad ones rarely do.

## Why Europe could become the capital of indie making

- US: VC culture is dominant. Founders see lots of money, "demand creates supply", and being an indie hacker there "means you're a loser rather than you are smart". Cost of living and health insurance push people into jobs, and there are few part-time or short-hours jobs.
- Europe: founders believe there is little VC money, so they look for alternatives. Work-life balance leaves time for side projects. Cost of living outside capitals is low. John lived on a 2,000 euro a month stipend in Holland. Some people live on social benefits for a year while building a startup.
- VC money can hurt. VC-backed founders build in a bunker and launch late. Bootstrapped founders "launch you talk you launch you talk and you iterate". John says paid growth has stopped working for him over the past three years: expensive, bot traffic, no retention.
- Small, focused startups win against wide ones. He thinks Europe should aim for "the next million average companies with 10 people or 100 people", not the next Google, since huge companies move to the US anyway.
- He sees European design taste and the "ability to be irritated with something so that you want to fix that thing" as an edge, especially among Dutch, German and French makers.
- Problems: Dagobert Renard, an indie maker from France, told him indie making in Europe is "terrible". People don't understand it, and renting an apartment is nearly impossible without a regular job contract. John argues products can solve this, as SafetyWing did for nomad insurance, and Stripe and Mercury now give loans based on transactions. He expects makers to use their indie profile as proof of income for rentals and mortgages.
- Policy prediction: nomad visas and remote-worker tax laws are multiplying, "exponential" in his view, like COVID rules spreading in three months. France has a four-year visa where you only need a startup. He expects most countries to have such rules by the end of next year, then to extend immigrant tax perks to local indie makers so they stay home. The US will do nothing because it already has more immigrants than it needs.
- He urges makers to keep posting about this on social media, since politicians are on it too.
