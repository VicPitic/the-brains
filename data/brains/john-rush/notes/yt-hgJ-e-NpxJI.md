## Why John bootstrapped MarsX instead of raising VC

- John came from the VC-backed startup world. Before 2012, funding was nearly nonexistent among developers he knew. From 2012 to 2018 it was easy, and almost everyone he knew raised a round.
- His observation: founders who started before funding was available and struggled on their own did well when money came. Founders who got money straight away failed far more often. In his 2010 batch of the first 10 Norwegian startups, four became unicorns (he names Kahoot), but later batches did worse and worse.
- He entered bootstrapped, self-funded work in 2018. He already had Angel investments and co-founded startups producing revenue, and he did not want an exit. His reason: after a big exit you have to work out how to store the money, so he preferred ongoing revenue to fund his next ventures.
- For the first two years he worked completely alone. He says solo building is fun only while nobody uses the product. Once users arrive it turns into "micro bosses".
- He pitched MarsX to about 200 investors and had enough interest in 2020 to raise $50-150M for a team of around 400 developers building micro apps. He declined. His reasons:
  - Micro apps are little products that need owners and ongoing maintenance.
  - Many people outside MarsX want to own products and make money from them.
  - A VC-funded company could not share ownership of the platform with the community.

## MarsX: reusable micro apps, decentralized

- MarsX's goal is to accelerate software development by reusing ready-made micro apps. His complaint is that every project rebuilds profiles, search and galleries from scratch, and "a thousand teams" write the same Uber clone. Science reuses prior work, and software does not.
- Reported stats (confirmed by John): about $1.5M ARR, 20,000 developers, 300+ micro apps in the showcase.
- The product has three interfaces on one project: no-code, pro-code and AI. Users can switch between them at any time instead of choosing one up front. Underneath sit hundreds of micro apps. He says only about 5% is left to build what's missing and connect the pieces.
- Why decentralized: centralized platforms trying to build everything themselves struggle because code goes obsolete quickly. Community builders own their micro apps and can co-own the platform, unlike plugin ecosystems where a plugin can be kicked out.
- His 2018 prediction was that IDE, no-code and platform companies would have to involve the community. He now points to Replit bounties, Supabase paying plugin builders and Vercel paying people who build for it. Explaining this was hard four years ago, when people said "if it was good people would be doing that".
- He bet on micro apps because of AI. The idea was that a user tells a micro app what to change and it rebuilds itself for them. He connected GPT-3 to micro apps last year and says it worked.

## How John acquired Unicorn Platform

- MarsX needs about 300 independent founders under one umbrella. Each keeps the upside of being a founder, and MarsX takes away the bad parts.
- He built profiles of the products and founders he needed. One profile was a no-code landing page builder, so he tried every one.
- He chose Unicorn Platform because the product showed care in every click and piece of text, even though it had fewer features and "didn't look like a real company".
- Reasons to buy rather than promote his own micro app:
  - An existing tool with users can be swapped in for MarsX's own micro app and reach market faster.
  - He also wanted the founder (Alex), who is strong at product and marketing, where John is not.
- He followed Alex on Twitter for a year to a year and a half, reading every tweet, from a shortlist of about 10 people. Alex did not know.
- One conversation was enough, and they agreed the deal by the end. Alex had never signalled wanting to sell, and would not have sold for cash alone.
- John says he watched what Alex struggled with and what he loved, and pitched taking the struggles away so he could focus on product and marketing.

## The "label" model for indie founders

- John's analogy is the music label. Singers look independent, but the label handles production and distribution. He applies this to tech: one large label of indie builders can compete with corporations and VC-funded startups, which he calls an unfair fight for lone indie hackers.
- He predicts this will be one of the biggest things to hit the indie tech world in the next 5-10 years. He already sees small unofficial coalitions of 2-10 builders on Twitter, sharing resources and distribution.
- He does not see it as zero-sum: "we're gonna take the pie of the corporations".

## His own acquisitions and exits

- **Icon search (2014):** He had money from startups and wanted to buy a tech company. Prices were then lower, and a million dollars could buy a really good business. He found the biggest icon search project at the time, with a million monthly visitors. Its users were developers and designers, which he saw as a good audience to build on. He spent six months on improvements, found it boring, and sold it for double what he paid.
- His takeaway: at any time many good startups can be bought below market price, because owners may say yes to an offer even if they didn't plan to sell.
- **Apartment-selling platform (2016 or 2017, he is unsure which year):**
  - The platform let a user enter an address and complete the home sale, including signing documents.
  - Building started in November and took three months. It launched on 1 February.
  - Two potential buyers appeared 15 days later, when they had no users.
  - Insurance companies and banks were rushing to get a digital product after a big consultancy report. Owning the housing platform meant they could sell mortgages there.
  - Competing counter-offers led to a sale for "over five million dollars".
  - He says none of those projects worked, because selling a house is a high-trust decision and people stick with a person. They sold partly because they did not fully believe in it. His lesson: sometimes you build something, get lucky and sell it.

## The acquisition market and why it will grow

- John expects the acquisition market to grow more than most people expect.
- Distribution used to be easy. Before 2015 his products never struggled with it: his movie app got 70,000 users within months of launch. Now nobody finds your app.
- People who are good at building rarely love distribution, and the reverse is also true. Builders get a little MRR, want to return to building, and should sell to someone who loves marketing and sales. Sellers on marketplaces like acquire.com often sell multiple projects, and the buyer gets a working asset.
- For non-developers, he calls outsourcing development or hiring it out "the best way to fail", and says he has almost never seen it work. Buying a project is a better route.
- An influencer with about $20,000 could buy a couple of projects, test whether their audience buys, and put more money into the ones that do.
- Small acquisitions didn't happen earlier because developers don't buy other people's code. They have opinions about code and think they could build it in a weekend. Non-technical buyers just want the work done.

## AI: John's history and where he sees the opportunity

- **History:**
  - His first tech project, in 2005-2006 at university, was a handwriting OCR neural network, started because he couldn't read friends' lecture notes. He ran several neural networks and other algorithms independently and accepted a word when two agreed. This reached about 98% accuracy against 99% for the leading commercial products. He never considered making it a business and deleted the program.
  - His next project was Ames AI, monitoring software for systems such as ATMs and vending machines. It applied regression models and neural networks to raw data to predict normal behaviour and flagged deviations against history.
  - Then came a movie recommendation app that scraped catalogs from Netflix, iTunes and HBO and used Facebook data. It could model two people together for a shared recommendation. It now runs white-labeled B2B with 15 million users combined.
- **Recent AI use:** His team used GPT-2 from 2019 and found it too unreliable, for example it could not produce JSON consistently. He thinks building with AI early last year is a main reason everything he does has grown since.
- **Agents:** He built an internal agents project where each agent is programmed for a role, such as lawyer, accountant, developer, or himself. He says it made him 10-20x more productive. The company has no lawyers, no accountants and almost no support staff, and AI covers those roles.
- **Opportunity:** He says the gap is UX on top of AI. Today you copy and paste, wait, and remember how to prompt. His advice:
  - Don't build deep tech, which is complicated and needs resources.
  - Don't build obvious or popular products, such as AI for email, because big brands will add it as a feature.
  - Go to second-wave niches that Google won't build for, such as bakeries, accounting, lawyers and cooking businesses.
  - He sees people who struggled for years now making $5,000-10,000 a month with small AI apps. Some call them wrappers, but "who cares".
