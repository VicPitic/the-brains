## Build only out of your own pain

- John's rule: ideas never come from imagination, only from his own pain. "Nothing I launched comes out of my hypotheses or my fantasy, it just comes out of my own need."
- Process: build it for himself, use it himself, iterate until he is happy, then launch to everyone else.
- Result: since he switched (about a year and a half before this interview), nothing he launched has failed. He expects that to continue as long as he is happy with the product himself.
- Why risk is low: no pain is unique to one human. Worst case he solves his own pain; better case others want the same fix.
- Asking people does not work. They say "yeah maybe" and you learn nothing (he points to The Mom Test).
- Can't find pain? "The reason you don't have pains is that you do nothing." Doing anything (a hobby like football, a project like the village) creates pain to solve.
- Each product creates new pain, which becomes the next product. Building the village will spawn tools for regulations, construction, running meeting rooms, incubation and transport.

## Why imagined ideas failed: his 20-year history

- He started internet businesses in 2004. The early ones worked almost by accident.
  - He began by doing a friend's university schoolwork, charged a friend-of-a-friend, then hired people to help, and eventually built a marketplace connecting the two sides.
  - He sold one business and others made money.
- From 2011 to 2022 he built VC-backed startups out of an incubator. They raised investor money and government grants, got media coverage and won conferences. About 99% failed; only two survived.
- Cause: the ideas came from his head ("what if that group had this problem"), not from a pain he felt. He could not judge how serious the pain was for people he wasn't.
- Fix: go back to the root and take ideas from his own or someone else's real pain.

## B2C vs B2B: where solo pain-driven products work

- B2C: if you have the pain, others have it too, so smart, well-funded people worldwide are solving it. Winner takes all (Airbnb, Uber, meetup.com). Having the pain doesn't mean you can execute. Example: his football-teammates app "Eat Play Football" in Istanbul still works but stayed small, while "Fuy Addict" (his name for the London app) grew huge.
- B2B: pain is fragmented across hundreds of thousands of jobs, each with many pains. Most niches are too small for startups or VCs, so there is little competition and few alternatives.
- Example: a school principal's pain. Principals are rarely entrepreneurs, so if you are one and build for it, you are probably the only person solving it.
- Businesses adopt new tools faster than consumers change habits. Managers decide on efficiency and money, not taste, and have no attachment to old tools. B2B vendors have short lifespans because tools connect to each other, so replacing one creates a ripple of replacements.
- He grants B2B success isn't guaranteed; the difference is in the odds, not certainty.

## Niche, unbundled tools beat general ones

- Thesis: the more a tool can do, the harder it is to use and the lower the quality of each result. A tool niched down can hardcode the details for one use case.
- Examples he built: a directory builder (not a general CMS like WordPress or Webflow) and Count Visits, analytics only for directories and simple landing pages.
- Count Visits advantage: because it knows the use case, onboarding is zero-configuration. It finds the elements, assigns events and shows exactly what a directory needs, whereas PostHog or Plausible require code to collect those events.
- Why now: the flood of new makers with no tool experience. A tool with one button lets newcomers use intuition and get the outcome without tutorials.
- Modern business buyers behave like consumers: lazy, don't want to learn, want things to just work. Old buyers equated difficulty and many features with quality.
- Good vs bad bundling: bundle around one user's job to be done (directory builder plus analytics in one is good). Bad bundling is a huge tool where only 5% fits you (his example: Microsoft products).
- Distribution vs product: distribution can win early (Slack vs Teams) but the best product wins long term. Microsoft pushed Windows for decades and macOS still won. He left Slack for Discord because Slack became too slow on his phone.

## Advice for plugin and platform-dependent makers

- Platform dependence is dangerous: "if platform sinks you sink with the platform too."
- Distribute on at least two platforms (WordPress plus Webflow, Shopify or another). Your real expertise is the UX and business logic, not the platform connection.
- Consider turning a plugin into a standalone micro tool. SEO works well for micro tools now, and a tiny tool (his example: hours-to-minutes converter) that was too small to release before can win traffic on a good domain.
- WordPress isn't dying; it is as huge as ever. The real risk is competition, since a mature platform attracts ever more builders and each earns less (his Spotify artists comparison).
- Look for newer platforms with few builders and high potential. Early Framer template makers made a lot as Framer grew.

## AI in SaaS is still at the start

- Most real AI usage is ChatGPT-style chat and generating code or text, with image generation third. Very few SaaS tools use AI inside the workflow, and the UX for that isn't solved.
- AI tends to sit beside the workflow as a mentor rather than being part of it. He has found few reliable agents, and customer-support bots often fail or get pulled back or supervised by humans after public embarrassment.
- Demos mislead. Two years ago companies shipped AI demos, then found reality was worse and moved into slow, deep work to make things reliable.
- Whoever makes AI reliable in any application "will win big".
- His example: SEO Bot, his article generator. It took a year and a half, not the week he expected. Hallucinations were removed by Googling, checking Wikipedia and fact-checking every sentence. To the user it looks the same, but it doesn't lie anymore, and the output reads nearly human.

## Build in public: why he says everyone should

- It builds trust with users, partners, investors, employees and journalists, and saves you from having to convince each person individually. He sees almost no downside.
- Three groups of founders:
  - Early founders know nothing competitors lack, so it's safe.
  - Growing founders gain hires, investors and press that outweigh copying risk. Most things can be copied by looking at the site or registering anyway.
  - Successful founders will win either way.
- "Your brain gives you the average founder risk... there is no risk for actual founder."
- Attacks come from jealousy or disbelief. Never post something that looks like a lie even if true. He holds back revenue figures that seem too good to be true, or lets them spread out over time.
- Don't post wins or milestones. Viewers get only jealousy or disbelief, and few get energy. Share how you got somewhere and the actions you took.
- Writing it down forces him to collect analytics and understand what he did, which improves his own work.

## The Indie Maker Village project

- Vision: indie makers are lonely (no office, partners or team, few conferences) and have no physical capital like Silicon Valley or YC. He wants to build one, eventually in many locations, starting with one. He'd join someone else if they built it.
- Format: a camp with a fixed program from day 1 to day 30, work during the week and organized leisure on weekends. It is meant to be busy and productive, not a relaxed yoga retreat.
  - Small private bedrooms, managed food (no personal kitchens), a large co-working space with meeting and workshop rooms.
  - Programs and incubations such as growth, SEO, and directories.
- Three rings:
  - Inner ring: short stays up to 30 days.
  - Middle: stays up to 6 months.
  - Outer ring: long-term properties people can rent or buy, fully private, with a path to Portugal citizenship after 5 years, with a school for children.
- Ownership model: "truly capitalistic." People buy property like anywhere else and everything costs money. He doesn't trust communal ownership or shared duties (he cites Burning Man working only because it is short). He expects only about 1 in 100 to buy.
- Status (Nov 2025): still looking for land in Portugal. Laws were strict but loosened in the past year. Plan: buy land and start construction next year, host the first group about two years out.
- The goal includes the building process: he wants a faster, cheaper, more maintainable way to build than bricks, done in public as a case study, plus tools to automate Portugal's permit and legal process with AI. Phases: more villages in Portugal, then every city, connected as a network.
- Physical projects are far slower than digital, but with 24 projects he works on others while one is stuck. He also thinks the delay helps because his audience is growing and more people will be ready by then.
