## Build problem-first: solve your own pain, then your friends', then release

- John treats business "more like art" than a way to make money. He sees a problem in his own work and solves it for himself first.
- Next he solves it for his friends, who are all entrepreneurs. Only after it works for both does he put it out to everyone.
- This makes failure hard. He is already the user, so a product doesn't get dropped because nobody else is using it yet.
- He builds for the solopreneur, not for teams. Most products ship with permissions and many buttons so people live inside the tool all day. John wants "one button where I say start and I just close it and then you know it does the job."
- He judges ideas as if they were someone else's, because people get attached to what they put effort into. His judgment "almost always correlates with the opinion of the other people."
- He keeps thousands of ideas in his draft board, for both content and products. Almost all of them are bad, and the world never sees them.

## Pitch ideas on Twitter before building (the DevHunt origin)

- John bought Unicorn Platform from another founder. It had 25,000 users, and it declined for the first year after the purchase because users were attached to the previous founder.
- His breakthrough with the bootstrapped model was DevHunt, a "Product Hunt for developers."
- The pain: Product Hunt was mostly occupied by marketing people voting for things, so even great dev tools couldn't win. John had his own dev tool, Marzx.dev.
- He tweeted from a brand-new account with a few hundred followers. He said Product Hunt is unfair to dev tools and closed (the algorithm isn't known), and proposed DevHunt.
- The pitch: "if this tweet gets 100 likes I will assemble a team among the people who comment this tweet and we're going to build it together."
- The tweet went viral, with 20k-50k views. His previous best tweet had about 50 views.
- Seven people joined as contributors, and some investors showed interest.
- Lesson: pitching before building is free, recruits help for free, and building in public gives free distribution. He found a way to build products "for free with almost no people."

## Build an audience first to test ideas in minutes

- John's first advice to solo founders is to build an online presence. Followers and content that gets views let you test hypotheses before you spend time building.
- Building takes weeks or months, while a tweet takes minutes. Repeated tweeting gives fast iteration cycles, which makes it more likely you build the right thing.
- Most people don't put in enough time. When they say they get few views, he asks how much time they spend creating versus consuming. The usual answer is about 30 minutes a day, which he calls too little.
- He sometimes spends 10 hours on one piece. One thread about AI coding tools took about 10 hours and passed 1 million views.
- "High effort content will win no matter what", because readers feel that someone saved them time.
- Unless you're famous, "you have to actually kind of work for people for free." The audience should think "I would have paid for it, but it's free."
- His content filter: he only posts a tweet if he likes the draft. If he likes it, he is confident everyone will; if not, he doesn't post it.
- Daily habit: each morning he reviews yesterday's work as a harsh outside critic, in his head only. It spares him public criticism, which hurts more.

## Run 20+ products with slow pace, small scope and no deadlines

- John runs 24+ products. Time per product ranges from about 1 hour a month to 100 hours a month, depending on user complaints, bug reports and market opportunities.
- He has no deadlines and no enterprise contracts, so he moves at his own pace.
- Having many products makes him efficient and good at saying no. He says you can harm a product by doing too much when you have plenty of time and resources.
- Some of his products are used by hundreds of thousands of people. He doesn't push features daily. He improves what exists, since users signed up because they liked it, and extra features may complicate it.
- Cap per product: each stays below $1 million MRR so he can run it without stress and without a team. He may go bigger later, when bored of small and mid-sized products.
- Team approach: he builds alone first for control and honest self-criticism. When traction brings more work, he invites a friend or someone passionate as a co-maker on a 50-50 split.
- He doesn't hire and doesn't think hiring survives the AI era. He expects either very small high-impact teams or co-founder-led products, with AI and tools covering the rest.

## Automate everything repetitive and keep only ideas and strategy

- John's view: about 95% of a founder's tasks could be delegated. Delegating to people means hiring, so he automates instead.
- He expects founders to be left with inventing ideas (strategy, creative work, small ideas within the product), which is the part he enjoys.
- Multiplication effect: a task that's merely annoying for one product becomes "crazy boring" across 24, which is his incentive to automate.
- His agents:
  - Most marketing except social media, run by his own AI agents.
  - An agent for accounting, paperwork and invoices.
  - An agent that acts as him and handles about 99% of communication with his co-makers.
  - Marzx.dev, his AI coding agent, used to build his products.
- Vibe-coding tools he uses: Lovable, Emergent, Factory AI.
- Workflow tools: Lindy AI and make.com. Example: when an email asks for an invoice, auto-reply with a link to an invoice generator and prefill the data. Customers in Germany keep asking for invoices.
- Lovable replaced Figma for prototypes. Speaking to it gives a clickable prototype in place of days of manual work.
- Research habit: a daily time slot to browse Product Hunt, sign up for and try about 10 new agents a day, for UX ideas and tools to use.
- AI spend: he tweeted that his AI tools cost about $20K a month (summed from his Mercury account). He compares it to one great senior employee, who would cost about the same, since the tools replace people.
- Proof it works: he was away from all work for three months and nothing broke. Some things grew less without active marketing, but there was "decent growth on some products."

## Marzx.dev: a closed AI coding tool he uses to build everything

- John started Marzx.dev in 2019, before AI coding was a category. He believes he and the Replit team were among the first to bet on AI changing coding.
- He uses it himself to build his other products, agents and SaaS.
- It has a waitlist of about 200,000 people. It is still closed, and he invites people one by one, mostly friends.
- He hasn't launched publicly because of the support and infrastructure burden. He says it isn't a solopreneur product and would require building a team.
- He has investor offers and chooses not to take them for now. He prefers a portfolio of products small enough to run without a team.

## Bootstrapping versus VC: how John switched

- John's first project, about 20 years ago, was a student marketplace for homework help and tutoring. He sold it after one year.
- He then invested his exit money in startups, as "bad VCs do": too early. He says this gets a cheap valuation but raises failure risk "by 10 or 100x."
- He invested in Norway, so even the winners were small and didn't cover the losses.
- By 2022 he had lost most of his money chasing unicorns, living in London and Silicon Valley, and joining accelerators.
- He found VC values didn't match his. He liked making users happy, not investors, and felt "kind of bad for making them richer."
- In 2022 he started bootstrapping as an experiment. His rule was to fund it for two or three years, and if it failed, go back to raising money and pay himself a salary.
- He says it felt different right away. He had direct contact with users, no middleman, and did the tweets, emails and calls himself.
- In the VC world he had to show a headcount (CMO and so on) to investors. Bootstrapping removed that.
- Having less money made small wins matter. Landing one client at $19 a month changed his bank account.
- He doesn't recommend this path to everyone. It was his thing and it clicked right away.
