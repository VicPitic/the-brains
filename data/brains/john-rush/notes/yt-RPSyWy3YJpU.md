## Bootstrapped portfolio model: 24 startups, zero employees, 50/50 co-makers

- John runs about 24 startups with no employees. Each project has one "co-maker" (usually the technical builder) who owns 50/50 with him. John does most of the marketing and product; the co-maker does most of the coding.
- His day-to-day is a Discord server with 24 channels, each with John and one co-maker. He brings roadmap ideas and user feedback, they discuss, the co-maker builds.
- He bootstrapped after about 10 years of VC-backed startups (seed, Series A, San Francisco incubators, exit-focused). He argues bootstrapping works better because "your incentives are aligned with the users not with investors."
- He prefers many small companies over one large one. One large startup "becomes like a job" with a board and employees, and he loses the freedom to pivot.
- Many products also make him safe: if one dies (e.g. ChatGPT ships something), he has many others, so he doesn't need to read the news.
- He doesn't plan to sell the projects. The goal is a holding company of products for the same audience, run for decades.
- He meets all co-makers once a year (usually June), for example a one-month gathering in Istanbul. They run their own hackathons there. One produced an agent store inside Unicorn Platform that turns on all his products with one click.
- He lives in a forest outside Istanbul and takes about 2-3 months off each summer. During one two-month break he posted nothing on the internet and customers kept coming in.

## One audience across all products: busy founders and compounding lifetime value

- All of John's products target "busy founders": one founder doing most of the job instead of hiring. He sees this as a growing group (even YC-backed startups now brag about small headcount).
- Founders care about outcome, not process. They want few interfaces and ideally one button that gets the job done.
- Because every product shares the same audience, a user who joins one product has a high chance of using five, ten or more of the others. This solves much of his distribution.
- Lifetime value adds up across products. His example: if one product's LTV is $400, then ten products make it $4,000. That lets him spend more on acquisition, and each customer is worth roughly 10x.
- He builds growth and marketing products, not developer tools. Founders paying for growth help will pay and share with friends. Developers "are never willing to pay and they're never willing to share," and VC-backed players subsidize dev tools. He built Dev Hunt, then stopped building many tools for developers.
- Small fees for small problems: a $99/month customer doesn't expect custom demands. Busy founders rarely contact support if the tool works.
- He turns down corporate clients who want SLAs and calls. It costs him money, but he keeps the business small enough to run without that burden, and total revenue across projects stays stable and growing.
- He argues his portfolio can still grow very large because the audience is millions of potential users.

## Idea validation: audience first, then a manual MVP, then a technical co-maker

- He builds distribution first on LinkedIn, X and Reddit, then pitches many ideas. He takes forward the ones that get comments, likes and questions.
- He validates through story, not code: "It's much easier to [change] your story" than to rewrite code. He shares the product story from every angle, even on podcasts, and discusses ideas for months or sometimes years.
- When people ask "when are you building this?", he recruits a technical co-founder who previously built something similar and failed. He brings them the audience and a full spec (features, pain points).
- The co-maker then does a one-to-two-week hackathon, and users arrive right after release.
- The first version is simpler than people expect. It can be a form that feeds a database while he and the co-maker do the work manually behind it. They lose money on time, but staying manual keeps them agile.
- He tries to launch within a month, then iterates on user feedback.
- His complexity rule: he doesn't want more than one person building it. If he can't build a simple MVP himself with vibe coding, he skips the product, since it would require hiring.
- Pain rule: he does a task manually until it hurts, learns the UX, then builds the product that solves it for himself. He did SEO by hand, sleeping little, before building a tool for it.
- Hundreds of ideas fail at the idea level, but every product that got real code is still running.

## Case study: Index Rusher (December 2023)

- He saw Twitter screenshots of Google Search Console with pages partly green (indexed) and partly gray (not indexed). The fixes in the comments were all manual.
- He built a waitlist in about 10 minutes, just before Christmas 2023. One button grants access to the user's Search Console. A Discord notification fired, and he manually sent pages for indexing and analyzed why they weren't indexed. The "product" was John behind a button.
- Sharing it on Twitter made it blow up. Once demand was clear, he found a developer to turn it into a real product with no human involvement.
- Distribution: he searched Twitter for keywords like "page indexed" and replied to complaints with the product. People didn't mind because the reply answered their question.
- He looks for small problems, not large products. Scrolling Reddit, Quora or Twitter for a few hours shows many complaints.
- Some products earn little or are free, but they pull people into his ecosystem and then into his other products.

## Vibe coding: speccing and tool-switching

- For first MVPs he uses Lovable, Replit, or his own no-code tool Unicorn Platform. He says using vibe coding tools is a skill that improves with practice.
- Treat the tool like a human who needs a clear spec. His spec has parts:
  - Business model first: who the user is, why they need it, how often, what they hate about existing products, what they want.
  - Numbered feature list, each with a simplification (e.g. "only email or only Google" for sign-up). AI tries to do things too well and overcomplicates unless told otherwise.
  - A long "do not do" list, his longest section (e.g. no user portal, store data in the browser, use an existing SDK).
- He keeps the prompt in a notes file and refines it over time. He now often gets a working result in one shot with no follow-up prompts.
- He tries the same prompt in three or four tools, because quality varies by day as providers change models and code.
- He moved a learning management portal from Bolt to Cursor when Bolt began breaking things on bug fixes. His view: vibe tools are for MVPs, Cursor is for production, and founders should become more technical and read some code as the product grows.
- Launch MVPs as content, not a pure launch. Teach something about the topic, plug the URL at the end of the thread. On Reddit, describe the problem and the fix without naming the product, and let people ask or click his profile.

## Marketing automation and where growth comes from now

- He automates marketing to avoid hiring one person per product, since mistakes in marketing are cheap. He uses AI for SEO: keyword ideas, headlines, blog articles, directories and programmatic SEO, mostly with his own SEO Bot tool.
- He uses Lindy AI, Zapier, Make and n8n for sequences. Example: find LinkedIn people who are bootstrapping and mentioned SEO, then DM them genuinely helpful content about their problem, not a product pitch, to start a conversation.
- Social media and SEO are getting more crowded, with VC-backed companies pouring money in and far more AI articles (he estimates roughly 10x more than last year). He wouldn't recommend social media to a new founder as a first channel.
- SEO has changed. New sites can get traffic in two to four weeks instead of six months. But Google favors fresh content, so you have to keep publishing. One of his sites lost its traffic entirely and he hadn't found why.
- LLM traffic is rising. LLMs favor niche directories for queries like "10 best bootstrap founders," and he runs many directories.
- He expects specialized, single-topic sites tied to a trusted human and present across blog, social, podcast and YouTube to win.
- He sees a fragmenting economy of many founders with niche brands, since AI makes money less effective for growth.

## Building a personal brand from zero

- Personal brand multiplies a good product but can't replace one. His analogy: it's "like a wind on a fire. If there's no fire and you pour wind on it, there will be no fire." Some of his products failed even with his brand.
- His brand feeds other channels: directories list his products and people link to him for free, which helps SEO.
- You can't buy a personal brand. Money makes it look corporate.
- With a few hundred followers, focus on a specific problem, not on yourself. His Dev Hunt launch came at about 200 Twitter followers. His message was that Product Hunt mostly serves marketers and developer tools never win there. It was new and public, and the tweet reached about 100k views.
- Get reach first, without promoting yourself. Repeated exposure makes people recognize your name. Stay consistent on one topic (his are bootstrapping, AI and vibe coding).
- Aim for one viral post. Algorithms now push content from small accounts, you have unlimited attempts, and the only cost is embarrassment. After one big post, the algorithm promotes you more. On LinkedIn he sees posts at about 50 likes and then one at 800 to 1,000.
- Build in public in 2025-2026: share good and bad.
- His social posts are written by him with no AI. Everything else is automated.

## Nova: the AI project manager on Discord

- Nova is an AI project and product manager that lives in Discord, where his co-makers, partners and some customers are. It acts on his behalf and many people think it's a human.
- Tasks:
  - Pulls product mentions from across the internet daily and posts them in the co-maker channel with proposed solutions.
  - Watches support chats for customer and prospect questions, complaints and bug requests.
  - Replies to customers and solves problems where it can, or sends bugs to the developer.
  - Knows the roadmap; when John shares an idea, it aligns the co-maker.
  - Coordinates cross-product integrations between two co-makers.
- He corrects it in the chat when it errs, visible to everyone. He says it's his biggest time-saver, with vibe coding next.
- He hasn't launched it publicly because he is liable for what it does. He plans to launch next year, probably.
- He expects that in about 12 months work will be optional for him, since everything will be automated except his own ideas.
- He also uses Gamma (gamma.app) for presentations.
- His closing message: "don't trade freedom for money."
