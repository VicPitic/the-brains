## Why John believes VC-funded startups are failing and bootstrapped ones are thriving

- John lists well-funded companies that collapsed: Bird (scooters) filed for bankruptcy after raising almost $1B; WeWork filed for bankruptcy after once being valued at $47B; Olive AI (raised $0.9B), Convoy (raised $0.9B) and VeeV (raised $0.6B) shut down; Plastiq (raised $150M) shut down; Hopin (valued at $7B) sold for $15M.
- At the same time, he says profitable bootstrapped companies are thriving.
- His view: the future of SaaS and non-deep/hard-tech startups is "highly efficient bootstrapped small teams or even solo founders and indie makers."
- He has run both VC-funded and bootstrapped startups and says he knows both worlds well.

## Survival data: VC-funded vs bootstrapped founders

- John says 99% of the VC-funded founders he knows went out of business (200+ startups).
- He doesn't know a single bootstrapped founder above $10k MRR who went out of business. Most are growing or selling for a good sum.
- These are his personal observations, not a formal study.

## Why too much money hurts the product

- His analogy: "Having too much money while building your startup is like going to a gym with fake weights. You lift them, and it all looks good, but you gain no muscles."
- To build great stuff for users, the business must be funded by users. Then all focus goes on the users, not on the next round, VCs or journalists.

## Funding path John recommends

- Best startups in today's world: bootstrap first, then use VC money for growth after product-market fit (PMF).
- Alternatives he expects more bootstrapped founders to use: community rounds, cheap loans and pre-sales. He wants to try all three eventually.
- Best option in his view: run a Holdco of simple cash-cow projects that bring in money every month, then act as your own VC when you need capital.
- He does this himself, building 24 projects for busy founders in his Holdco.
