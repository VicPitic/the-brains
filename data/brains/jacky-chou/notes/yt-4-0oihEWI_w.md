## How Jacky structures his businesses and diversifies risk

- Jacky is an electrical engineer by education. He started making money online around 2015-2016 after failing courses and googling "how to make money online" the day before a final exam.
- His early attempts ranged from CPL offers to selling LinkedIn recommendations on Fiverr.
- His current mix, as of 2022:
  - A D2C homeware brand (Far and Away).
  - An SEO agency doing "mid five figures" a month.
  - A portfolio of about 34-35 content sites.
  - Traditional investments such as equities and crypto.
- The agency is "an extremely high margin" business. He puts its profit into content sites, which are revenue-generating assets, instead of spending it on lifestyle.
- The agency also lets him "practice what we preach" by ranking his own portfolio.
- He credits his risk mindset to years of trading crypto.
- Diversification is his main theme, and it applies to businesses, link tactics and investments.

## Dropshipping exit and the D2C brand Far and Away

- His first big online money was dropshipping men's fashion accessories (beads) on WordPress, fulfilled manually from AliExpress. It reached about $15,000 in revenue in a month, with hundreds of orders a day.
- Later he ran a home decor dropshipping store doing about $250k a month at roughly 90% gross margin. It was sold to a PE firm in Detroit.
- He wasn't proud of selling "pretty much garbage from China", so he started a D2C brand "to do it right".
- Far and Away sells heritage products from countries not known for making them, such as Portuguese dinnerware, ceramics and cutlery. French scented candles are next, from the oldest candle manufacturer in the world.
- Sourcing is private label. He finds mom-and-pop manufacturers, negotiates a rate and good payment terms, and builds the brand on their storytelling.
- He picked the niche from data from the dropshipping store, which showed winning categories to zero in on.
- It averages about six figures a month in euros at roughly 65% gross margin.
- It is the biggest time sink of his businesses. He calls it the worst for "time to revenue".

## Delegation and operations

- He learned delegation from Andrew Wilkinson of Tiny.
- When he buys a business, he keeps the seller on as operator with a minority stake. He tells them: "this is your piece of the pie", and offers his team's resources. He asks how they would grow it with infinite resources. This turns the work from "zero to one" into "one to a hundred".
- He has used this with four operators. The other ~30 sites run in-house.
- Content team: 8-12 writers paid a flat rate per word as contractors. Four editors upload and design posts and check readability against SOPs. A director of ops oversees the content sites and the agency.
- Content is prioritized by site health:
  - If a site is hit by a core update, the team pauses new articles and fixes existing ones.
  - If a site is doing well, they double down. One site went from about 20 articles a month to double that.
- His own workload is now 4-8 hours a day. In 2020 he worked 16-18 hour days during COVID.
- His routine: put out fires from overnight, work out, coffee, then knock out "the five things you dreaded" from the day before. He argues that being 1% better each day compounds.

## Buying starter sites and portfolio economics

- Only about $60k a month in max revenue comes from the 34 sites. He says they aren't all generating "crazy revenue".
- His model is to buy many starter sites in different stages, add content and links, then wait. A large portfolio lets him test aggressive tactics on $5k sites before applying them to $100k sites.
- Volume matters because some sites simply won't rank even when best practices are followed.
- What he looks for in a $5-10k starter site:
  - Many indexed keywords in Ahrefs.
  - A trajectory that is "up and to the right".
  - Earnings don't matter at this price; the site can make $0.
- He buys any site ranking for products he sells, such as his Amazon FBA homeware brand. He pushes his own products there, like VPN Mentor did with its own brands.
- Due diligence is fast: only Ahrefs, plus verifying in Search Console. He can make an offer within about five minutes and be in escrow within half an hour. Speed is why sellers choose him.
- Deal sources: Facebook groups, and now mostly private deals. People message him before listing anywhere, especially after podcast appearances.
- He rarely builds from scratch now. He had one six-month-old mental health site.
- Buying saves months of time. "$5k a month to save months of time" is worth it to him because it is a small share of his net worth.

## Payback periods, risk and when to sell

- His main metric is payback period. He buys sites at 2-3 years of payback. A custom Amazon rate card shortens that to just under two years.
- If a site survives the payback period, he is "in the green".
- Example: a large site that did $20k a month fell to about $3k after an update. He had paid about $60k for an ugly non-brandable 301 domain, so he had already made his money back.
- Starter sites are his moonshots. He buys many $5-20k sites hoping to reach $100k+ and a six-to-seven-figure exit. If a site makes $1k a month and then dies, he doesn't worry.
- He dialed back risk in late 2022 because of the economic climate.
- Selling rule: sell at peak euphoria, "when you think you don't need to sell", or when a site is too big a share of your net worth. Always take some chips off the table.
- A partial sale works. He would buy 70% and let the seller keep 30% for upside.
- He worries that some operators have most of their net worth in content sites. Updates can be "life-ruining" if a family depends on one site.
- Advice by situation:
  - With $5k in the bank and a $1k/month site (worth at least $30k), sell it and rebuild.
  - With $100k in the bank, buy starter sites.
  - Don't quit your job to start a site from scratch. Have at least 12 months of runway and a site already earning.
  - He flags survivorship bias in success stories.

## Content cadence and indexing

- Publish regularly but not necessarily often. 4-5 posts a month is enough to show Google the site is alive. The main failure is letting a site "sit and die".
- Post volume on a new site:
  - Strong trajectory: about 30 posts a month.
  - Standard trajectory: 5-10 posts a month.
  - Heavy link building early, then adjust to how the site reacts.
- Process after purchase: keyword research, then scheduled posts.
- For indexing, the Rank Math plugin that uses the Search Console API worked well. Paid indexing tools also "work like crazy".
- He admits he can't explain why some sites went up and some down in the May 2022 core update. A site with poor speed and the same links as hit sites went up.
- He says with "very, very high certainty" that the update was not a link update.
- The sites hit hardest were ones where he had earlier reversed a penalty with a 301.

## Link building: vary tactics per site

- Rule: use a different link-building mix on each site. A single SOP across all sites means a link update hits everything. He says you can't do all niche edits across all domains.
- His mixes by site:
  - Churn-and-burn sites: PBNs, which he says still work great.
  - Others: niche edits and guest posts.
  - Riskier sites: tier 2 links as well.
- He considers PBNs more dangerous than tier 2. He has never seen tier 2 get penalized.
- Tier 2 method: guest posts target the same keyword as the money site. For example, a guest post optimized for "top SEO agency 2022" links to the money site. He then points links at the guest post, like parasite SEO while still linking to his own page.
- He thinks of links in three classes: very good, middle, and very bad. He stays mostly in the middle and goes to the bottom side only when feeling risky.
- He believes in "quantity over hyper quality" because Google now mostly ignores links instead of penalizing them.
- For someone with one or two sites that feed the family, he'd skip PBNs and tier 2. He'd use guest posts on hyper-relevant sites, as Mushfiq of Website Flip does.
- Writers and the team keep a note of which tactic each site gets. He says it sounds complex but isn't.

## Site speed, E-E-A-T and video

- Site speed matters for conversion. Based on his ecommerce experience, no one waits on pages that take more than 2 seconds, especially on mobile 4G. Slow pages mean fewer affiliate clicks.
- Expertise depends on the niche:
  - In home, he has writers write from the point of view that they've used the product, and this helped rankings.
  - In sensitive niches like VPN, cybersecurity expertise matters more.
- Video helps E-E-A-T, but he hasn't implemented it well. For his VPN sites he currently embeds other people's videos.
- He would like to make a how-to video for each article, especially those ranking for "best VPN".
- He thinks a single-site operator has an edge in video because they know the content well. At scale, with many writers, it is harder.
- His brother has about 300k TikTok followers. When he embeds his own recipe video, the post ranks quickly with almost no links and a poor backlink profile. Influencer friends see the same when they talk from a "used it" point of view and embed a video.
- He believes Google's language and video understanding is now strong.
