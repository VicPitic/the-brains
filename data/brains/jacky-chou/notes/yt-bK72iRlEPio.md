## How Jacky buys, sizes and funds small online business deals

- Jacky runs Indexy, a holding company that acquires and builds online businesses. He has built a portfolio of 50+ online businesses over about 10 years.
- His path: studied electrical engineering, hated it, Googled how to make money online, then found Black Hat World, drop shipping and affiliate marketing. Those profits became the capital he now invests.
- Deal sizes are small: from about $500 to $5K up to a largest deal of $800K.
- Deals are fully self-funded: no loans, no partners.
- Structure: asset sales, not share deals. He pays cash upfront with an earnout, or buys 51% controlling interest in the asset and has the owner stay on. If the asset takes off, he buys the owner out. If not, "crisis averted."
- When the existing owner stays on, deals "do really really well" because the owner stays invested. He then plugs in his own distribution engine.
- Sourcing is reactive, not active: marketplaces and DMs. People who know he is in the adult space send him adult-space sites. He only does a deal if it is a "no-brainer" priced under market value. In his industry the spread between price and value is large, and any spread is an opportunity.

## Due diligence and red flags on online business deals

- Diligence takes him a day or two.
- Check where the traffic comes from. If it is organic, check Ahrefs and Google Search Console. Some sellers fake traffic numbers, and he has caught this a few times ("You said it's Google. It's definitely not Google").
- If traffic is not organic, he audits all the campaigns.
- He won't touch businesses whose traffic comes from LLMs such as ChatGPT. No tool like Ahrefs shows what you rank for there, so he can't audit it.
- Small software businesses often run on "duct tape in the back end," which is a problem once an engineer looks at the code.
- Avoid commodity businesses. His web hosting purchase was a dud at about $400/month. He had expected a home run because of hosting valuations, but the market is too commoditized.
- Pattern that works: he only builds or buys in a space where he is deep in the trenches. Before launching local SEO software he posted about local SEO daily for about 9 months, with a daily YouTube video. He knew the pain points and assumed most people share the ones he has. Outsiders can't easily replicate it because they don't know the industry's nuances.

## Platform risk, and what happened to his deals

- He argues that content-site and rollup deals are "priced in" for high yield (e.g. 30% a year). Money can be returned in 1-3 years, but "the platform risk is insane."
- Of the sites bought three years ago, he estimates about 5% max are still alive. Most went to zero.
- Towing directory (success): bought for $10K from a founder in Lithuania who hand-dialed New York callers and lost them on accent. It ranked #1 for "towing near me" but made only about $500/month. The founder stayed on for about 10%. He built it into the largest towing directory in the US and sold it for $195K about 6 months later. He exited early because the East Coast towing contacts seemed linked to organized crime and began making subtle threats during negotiations.
- Affiliate site bundle (mixed): about $300K+ for 10 affiliate sites making about $10K/month total. The real value was a custom Amazon affiliate rate card paying about 4x the normal commission. The sites were spammy and all trended to zero within 12 months. He recovered the money only because of the rate card.
- The rate card's scale: about $40M a year in shipped revenue to Amazon. Roughly four months before this interview (Dec 2025), Amazon removed the rates with no notice, no email and no support.
- He had pitched large affiliate businesses: attach to his rate card, get 4x revenue, he takes 20%.
- He reports monthly revenue publicly. It ranged from about $70K at the low to $560K in a single month (about 50% net) at the peak, and sits around $200K now.

## Quick-win tactics for acquired affiliate and SEO sites

- Fix web design first, especially ugly affiliate buttons. He says this alone can give about a 50% uplift.
- Change publish dates to the current month. Google may treat the article as fresh and boost it.
- Attach a higher affiliate rate card, where available.
- Sell sponsored placements. If you rank for something like "best protein powder," go to the brands and demand a flat fee (e.g. $10K/month) plus a high commission. Most affiliates don't do this because they're poor at sales and accept Amazon's roughly 10%.
- Results: at the $550K/month peak, about half came from these partnerships. At one point he ranked for most supplement-industry keywords. The most from one brand on one page was $50K/month plus about 60% commission, because brands wanted external traffic to lift Amazon sales velocity.
- He follows FTC rules and labels the #1 slot as sponsored.
- Media partnership model: he partners with a dying local newspaper and borrows its SEO authority with guides on its domain (e.g. "best Vancouver realtor"). He then pitches local businesses on being listed #1 (e.g. $500/month). That model makes about $30K/month.

## Why he now builds software instead of buying it

- His thesis has shifted: "There is no product moat at all... in especially software." He thinks most B2B SaaS could be replicated in a couple of weeks at most, so "all you need is distribution."
- He mostly builds rather than buys software now. He'd only buy a small local SEO or SEO tool that is mostly built and doing low numbers.
- Market state: buyers are scarce. Rollups have no money and strategics are scared, so valuations are low and you "can value however you want." Exits to Semrush, Ahrefs or a large agency would be "pennies on the dollar."
- Ecom buyers pick up businesses at inventory value (he cites $30M/year businesses), fire the team and run them with their own people.
- Local SEO agencies are the one acquisition category he'd consider. Many are badly run and resell subcontracted services, and you effectively buy the retainers. Downsides: they sell at 2x on the low end (up to 3-5x if large), and once the founder leaves it gets iffy.
- Local SEO outlook: local ranking relies on directory mentions (e.g. Yelp), reviews and directions clicks, which differs from organic SEO. He doesn't expect Google to add AI summaries to local maps soon.

## Distribution engine and launching into an audience

- His engine is YouTube, X and a newsletter. The audience is small but very loyal, and "would buy everything I sell."
- When a product aligns with his audience it hits hard. His local SEO software did $30K out of the gate. A small $100 service did about $6K in a couple of hours and should settle at $10-20K/month. He sees the ceiling for these small services at about $50K/month.
- Alignment is essential. The hosting product flopped because it did not fit the audience and was a commodity.
- He builds in public and says copying isn't the risk: "execution is like everything now."
- Test widely and decide fast. Cast a wide net, go all in if something has product-market fit, and cut or sell fast if not (pennies is fine).
- Daily routine: record a video every day, check ad campaigns, give product input to his tech co-founder, help with customer service if needed, then work on distribution and ad creatives. He films his Meta ad creatives himself because AI avatars aren't working on Meta.
- Advice for someone starting from zero: short-form content daily, YouTube short and long form, redistributed elsewhere. With all the time in the world, 3 short-form pieces and 1 long-form a day. Do it for 100 days and he believes you'd make at least a dollar online. Posting daily lets you see which videos get more views and do more of those.

## Team, portfolio and risk management

- Team: about 13 full-time, mostly overseas. It includes AI-enabled VAs, video editors, a web developer, a front-end engineer and his tech co-founder. On acquisitions, he usually partners with a founder or operator who runs operations while he handles marketing.
- He let go of about 60% of his team because of AI. Content writing, editing, posting, formatting and graphic design are now done with AI (e.g. "one click is like thousand articles"), along with repetitive tasks.
- About 20 revenue-generating assets exceed roughly $500/month. Smaller ones are "dead residuals" he doesn't count. They include software, media partnerships, an SEO agency (about $50K/month) and a paid community.
- Community: Advice, an SEO / make-money-online community of about 500 members at $169/month (annual plan available), making about $30K/month by his figures. The culture is blunt and "grungy."
- Diversification: he holds a lot of cash (about 20-30%, perhaps 40% counting treasuries). His reasoning is that all his businesses are "risk on," so the rest of his holdings should be risk off.
- His advice to buyers: "Take your chips off the table periodically." Don't keep going all in. If you can exit even a portion, do it. "You wouldn't do that with Tesla stock. Why would you do that with a business?"
