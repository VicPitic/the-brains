## SEO partnerships: the model Jacky calls his biggest earner

- Jacky says partnerships are his main business right now. He approaches a company whose core product is not display ads or affiliate revenue (a local newspaper, a publisher, a brand) and carves out a subdirectory on its site.
- He installs a separate WordPress in that subdirectory and builds an affiliate site inside the partner's domain, borrowing its authority.
- He monetizes the subdirectory three ways: affiliate commissions, display ads, and "retail rank and rent" (companies pay to be the top slot on a "best X" page).
- His example: he targets the same keywords across multiple partners. For "best protein powder" he has several pages on the front page of Google, so he can charge a protein powder company heavily for a slot. "They'll be foaming at the mouth."
- Deals are inbound. He keeps posting about it, e.g. "if you have a DR 70+ we can get you up to 300K a month," backed by a case study site.
- His reasoning for sharing openly: "the pie is so big." The more he talks about it, the more deals come in.

## Partnership economics and contract terms

- One partnership does $300K a month, with $150K a month going to Jacky. He notes this is equivalent to roughly 30 retainer clients at his rates, which he would never want to manage.
- Splits vary. One deal was a 50/50 or 60/40 revenue share (the partner negotiated them down) plus a flat $10K a month retainer. The catch was that the partner owned the IP of the content, so they could cut him off after a year.
- The partner is hands-off when the numbers are good. For his biggest partner, he just sends an invoice-request at month end.
- Incentives are aligned because both sides earn more as revenue grows, unlike retainers.
- His contract is "very aggressive," including a cut if a partner exits. He tells people: "don't let these big companies bully you." He briefly sold the contract template (about 20-something copies in a week) and pulled it so it wouldn't saturate the market.
- His hope is partnerships last through the end of next year, and he plans to keep onboarding partners. The risk he names is Google deciding it dislikes authoritative sites, which he sees as unlikely because it would mean hitting Forbes and similar publishers.

## Amazon affiliate rates and e-commerce

- Jacky ships about $2 million a month of Amazon products. His commission ranges from 3% to 8%, with 8% in home.
- He got the rate card by buying a portfolio of sites in a bundle. The sites went to zero but the rate card made the purchase worthwhile.
- Amazon raises rates by category, and you must prove yourself in each one. He says tech is least likely to go up because margins are thin, while categories like makeup can go high with volume.
- His advice on getting there: ship roughly $3-5 million a month, the level of The Verge and Forbes. His own request for higher rates was met with a Q4 freeze.
- He is "very bullish on Ecom." His DTC homeware brand Far and Away had a Black Friday "well into six figures," and it ships only to Europe, with Nordic-style products.
- His earlier path: he made his first million online dropshipping. After a Facebook feedback-request update led to ad account bans, revenue fell from about $250K a month to about $100K a month at roughly 10% profit, and they sold to private equity for about $150K. Half of it was an earnout that never paid. His lesson: he should have private labeled earlier.
- A cutlery set at $130 was their winning dropshipping product because it looked designer.

## Branded search and local SEO tactics

- **Paid ads feed organic:** On an ecommerce site with an affiliate blog, paid ads drive branded searches. For example, $50K a month on Facebook, Instagram and Pinterest ads for Far and Away makes people Google the brand and read reviews. Jacky says that sends positive signals to the site and let him get away with weak content on the blog.
- **PBNs as local citations:** He uses PBNs as an unlinked mention (a citation), then links from the PBN to other citations like Yelp as a tier 2, boosting NAP (name, address, phone) consistency. He says it works and is cheap.
- **Unlinked mentions:** He argues that if Google can detect citations, it can detect unlinked mentions, so nofollow or unlinked mentions are not useless.
- **Hypothesis (untested):** If a brand appears at number one on every "best SEO company" listicle, it should find it easier to rank for "SEO company." He suggests brand name saturation across titles and profiles (he cites Julian Goldie) helps build knowledge panels.
- **Beginner advice:** Start with local rank-and-rent (a site like roofingaustin.org), drive leads, then find a partner.
- **His local start:** He ran local SEO for six real estate clients, reaching about $10K a month at peak. Citations alone were enough to get number one.

## What he likes, doesn't like, and where he's cautious

- **Client retainers:** He dislikes them. He cancelled a $12K a month client recently, and says the math doesn't work compared with partnerships. He also finds the work unsatisfying (ranking a Vancouver lawyer for 100 searches a month).
- **Niche sites:** He labels them his weakest area. One example: a site bought for $10K, scaled to $20K a month, then fell to about $3K a month. His lesson: exit before the update. His site had guest posts (about 1 guest post to 20 own posts) and went too broad. His content is now all AI-written and human-edited, and he let writers go.
- **Informational content:** He thinks informational niche sites will be hit hardest by SGE ("what dogs can eat" is the easiest thing to replace).
- **Display ads:** He predicts a 70-80% drop in RPMs when third-party cookies are removed, and says ad-based sites face roughly "negative 90%" with SGE and cookie removal combined.
- **Past enterprise SEO:** At peak the agency did about $150K a month in retainers, serving Fortune 500 and public companies including Motley Fool. A big client, Overflow, found him via an interview about his dropshipping success.
- **Directories:** He sold a towing directory after learning many East Coast towing companies were mob-owned.

## SEO opinions: links vs content

- Jacky says "content is king" is wrong. His partnership content is "mediocre at best" and still ranks, because the domains are authoritative.
- He says "links don't matter" is also wrong: "links are everything." He challenges anyone to name a significant-volume keyword where links don't matter, and offered $500 if he can't outrank it in a day. He excludes obscure long-tail examples.
- If you don't have a partnership, he says you need to build links.
- He likes fighting fire with fire: he competes against Forbes-type sites with similar authority, not a niche site "water pistol."

## Personal branding, newsletter and monetization

- Jacky has posted on Twitter for about 2 years and recently began daily YouTube videos. He calls personal branding "a huge dub" because it attracts partnership deals, including household names.
- He thinks most SEO people on Twitter are "heavily under monetizing": everyone should put an offer at the end of a tweet.
- His newsletter, Marketing Letter, earns about $6K a month on about $1K a month in costs. He compares it to a Roth IRA: boring, passive and a fallback. Rhino Rank is one of its largest sponsors.
- His paid community brought in $6.2K that month, helped by annual sales on Black Friday.
- His other lines of business include display ads, directories, retail rank and rent, a link building agency, and short-term rentals in Lisbon.

## Running the team and operating style

- Jacky has 17 people. He wants to stay lean after reading *Profit First*, and cites bloat as why some niche site builders went under.
- His rule: "I don't do anything more than once," so he delegates everything, with a person or two per task.
- His director of ops and his EA are both based in Kenya and are his top earners. Everyone else is VA or entry level. He starts people low, then promotes and raises often, and pays bonuses (sometimes a month's salary) for strong performance.
- His EA sends a morning update on which emails to answer and manages his calendar and Twitter DMs. He calls this "the greatest growth hack ever."
- For email, he suggests the free Missive app so an assistant can read and file without Gmail access.
- He reallocates the team by performance. If a site is struggling, he pauses it and moves everyone to a partnership that's working.
- His process is loose: bulk keyword research a couple of times a month, run in Sheets.
- Next plans: use partnership rankings to close the loop with owned products (e.g. sell protein powder if he ranks for it), and aim to retire within five years.
