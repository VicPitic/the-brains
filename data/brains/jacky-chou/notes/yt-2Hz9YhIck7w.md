## SEO agency: economics, team and how to win clients

- Jacky ran an SEO agency (Index C) and scaled it to about $250k/month at its peak. When his content sites started "popping off" (around $100k/month), he fired most clients and kept a select few.
- Google algorithm updates then hit the content sites, taking them from about $100k to $20k/month overnight. He now runs a blend of agency and own businesses, and the agency funds the swings he takes on his own ventures.
- At the time of this interview (Nov 2023) the agency did about $70k/month in revenue, with about $30k take-home (his ballpark, books not done).
- Retainers: $5k minimum, up to about $20k, so roughly 5-6 clients. Team is 17 full-timers, mostly offshore, and about 30+ including contractors. He acts as account manager.
- Biggest expense is backlinks (a list of blog owners the agency works with), not payroll.
- Pros: cash flow (paid right away) and variety of projects. Cons: margin. Agencies tend to drift to 10-20% margin at scale because of payroll; he keeps his much higher. Also, "you're someone's [ __ ]."
- Client acquisition: build a case study by ranking your own asset, then talk about it publicly. He reveals every dollar he makes, and clients flow in. His own businesses make about 50% more revenue than the agency, so the case studies are a byproduct.
- He argues many SEO agencies have never run or ranked their own business. His test when pitched: ask where the pitcher's own site ranks.
- Advice for starting any agency: pick a service people want now (e.g. cutting TikTok/podcast clips), offer it free if you're young, and get a foot in the door. Good taste in design matters.

## Trading time for money vs. owning assets

- Jacky is "super against" trading time for money, even at $1-2k/hour coaching or consulting rates. He calls hourly billing "the wrong unit of measurement."
- His goal is income while he golfs or is at the gym ("Stripe notifications"). He would rather earn less and not be on calls all day.
- He applies the same logic to stock trading: if you must be actively trading, you are still trading time.
- He says doing work you would do for free is the only way to last in entrepreneurship, and money is "a byproduct." He is up around 7:00 a.m. because he is excited about projects.

## Dropshipping: the $1.7M run and what killed it

- His first dropshipping was before Shopify and Oberlo (learned from Black Hat World): men's bracelets and watches, sold via Facebook ads and fulfilled manually between university classes. One month in 2015 or 2016 made about $30k.
- Home-ware store with partner Albert: each put in $5k, burned about $8.5-9k on Facebook ads before finding a winning product. Scaled from zero to about $1.7M in 7 months, with $2k days becoming $10k days.
- Biggest mistake: not private labeling when Albert suggested it. Facebook began sending feedback forms to buyers who never received (slow-shipped) products, the ad account got banned, and the iOS changes hit too. Revenue fell from $250k/month to $80k/month.
- They sold to a private equity firm for low six figures on an earn-out and were never fully paid (the firm claimed bankruptcy).
- Pros: high margins, strong cash flow, no inventory, fast scaling. Cons: not proud of the products, no moat, and multiples around 1x EBITDA, so it is barely sellable.
- Advice: keep fulfillment times short and keep testing ad creatives. When people say ads aren't working, he looks at click-through rates. If CPCs are normal, it is a conversion problem. Hire a designer for the site. Customers are now more sophisticated and have been burned, so "build a real business" with a great offer, product and site.

## Amazon FBA and D2C: wins, losses and lessons

- Supplements (ginseng, lion's mane, cannabis oil) on Amazon: up to about $100k/month on the cannabis oil, ginseng about $30k/month, lion's mane about $8k/month, about 30% margins. Late profit was about $10k/month. He closed it because Amazon hassles (account dings) weren't worth it alongside better businesses, and says he would like to do it again.
- Vanilla candles (launched Q4 2019): about 2,000 units bought at about $1 each, sold hundreds a day and sold out. They then ordered $20-30k of inventory (about 20k units). COVID delayed delivery about 9 months, Amazon warehouse restrictions capped inbound at 300 units, and storage fees ran about $1k/month. The relaunch did about $10k/month. They liquidated at cost, losing about $20-something k.
- Lesson on Amazon (stated in the conversation): don't raise prices before running out of stock, because sales velocity and history drive re-ranking.
- Far and Away (dinnerware, with Albert): sourced from family-owned Portuguese factories after touring about 10 of them for 2 weeks. Kickstarter raised about $100k US, which funded the launch. COVID and a 12-month factory lead time hurt, and they switched some factories. It now does mid five figures a month, below expectations after the $250k/month dropshipping store. Germany is the biggest market.
- Positioning: he describes the brand as luxury (branding, storytelling, perceived value) rather than premium (extra features). He tells the factory owners' stories to customers. Plates cost about 10x what they would from China.
- D2C economics: shipping heavy, breakable goods is expensive. Albert (finance) insists on calculating every line item, because margin leaks of about 2% each add up, and you should always know your exact margin.

## Content sites

- Content sites are blogs that rank on Google and monetize through affiliate commissions (Amazon about 4%) or programmatic display ads (about $20 per 1,000 users).
- Last month: about $75k from display ads and about $50k from Amazon affiliate. Niches: architecture, furniture and home. He wants to eventually refer traffic to his own home products.
- They trade at about 3-4x EBITDA. He reads low multiples as high risk (a 30%+ yield). His model is rolling up many risky assets to diversify.
- Lesson: running 10 sites with the same tactics is not real diversification. A single algorithm change hit them all.
- A million-dollar content business can be run by one person plus a few writers, which he says is far easier than FBA.

## Newsletter (marketingletter.com)

- Started in January 2023 at about 3k subscribers and is now about 23-24k, with about 45% open rates. He has made "100 something K" year to date.
- It covers marketing and AI news, and he says many readers are there for his revenue and net worth updates (built in public).
- Monetization: sponsorships, plus pushing readers to his own SEO services and private community. Readers include people from LinkedIn, Microsoft and Walmart.
- He likes the model because there is no platform risk. The list is yours, and spam-folder problems are fixable by pausing sends for days or weeks. There is a moat, since lists take time or money to build.
- Growth: paid ads on Facebook and Twitter plus organic. He rolls flight profits into growing the list.

## Personal brand, podcast and building in public

- He started a podcast because "people don't want to talk to you, but for some reason they'll talk to you on a podcast." He invites everyone he wants to talk to, including guests like Brian Dean.
- A YouTube deep-dive episode got only about 170-180 views because his audience is mostly SEOs, while SEO guests get thousands. He still argues for making content you enjoy, since that is what is sustainable.
- If forced to keep one business for 5 years, he would pick the personal brand side (YouTube, Twitter, interviews). He has published a podcast weekly all year and meets people by tweeting from cities he travels to.
- First online dollars: selling LinkedIn recommendations on Fiverr for $5 each, a couple hundred dollars total.
- He publicly documents revenue and net worth monthly, which doubles as his marketing for the agency and newsletter.

## Money management and investments

- Mostly equities and ETFs via dollar cost averaging. He knows lump sum wins historically, but DCA helps his emotions. He has about $500k US in cash being averaged in at about CAD 26k/month, paid weekly.
- He treats his businesses as his high-risk bucket and his investments as low risk, focused on keeping the money already made.
- Portugal building (bought for the golden visa, with a 30-year fixed rate at 2.4%): about 15k euros of bookings in a month, about 10-12k profit, and about 70k euros take-home trailing 12 months. A management company runs it, and he holds a short-term rental license, which is a moat.
- Lessons from past losses: he picked individual stocks (including buying companies he used, e.g. Starbucks, Coca-Cola, Spotify, Square, Tesla) and ended down about 80% on most. He lost about 20k on Square. He made about $60k US in an hour on GameStop options, and took profits periodically, rolling some into Tesla.
- He treats retail mania as a top signal: he bought $10k of Bitcoin in 2017 in a brokerage full of older retail buyers, and prices crashed soon after.
- He concluded stock picking is not for him because he checks too often and has to get both the buy and the sell right.
