- Googled "how to make money online", landed on digital marketing, took an internship in Berlin at a marketing agency.

Later head of marketing at another startup, then consulting and his own businesses.
- Learned SEO purely by Googling and trial and error, with no formal training.
- He is "pretty against courses". His view: courses mainly give accountability, and if you are a self-starter you can learn anything from YouTube, Khan Academy, Harvard's online classes and similar. If you buy a course to get yourself to do the work, that is a mindset problem, not an education problem.
- He learns from podcasts (My First Million, All-In) by picking up terms they mention, Googling them and going down rabbit holes.

## Jacky's business portfolio and which model he prefers

- **Indexsy** is the holding company for his content sites ("Niche sites"), his marketing newsletter and a prioritized SEO service. Together these are his largest revenue source. He also co-owns Far and Away (DTC dinnerware) and has smaller brands, including an online interior design company.
- Preference: niche sites. No clients to deal with, he likes to tinker, margins are very high. He hates client calls, which rules out agencies for him.
- Downside of niche sites: 6 to 12 months to ramp up each site.
- Agency work is the cash-flow layer: "pretty decently high margin" and "extremely low risk". Niche sites and e-commerce can go to zero quickly, which has happened to him, so he is glad he did not go all in and kept the agency.
- E-commerce is "very tough to do right". He got burned earlier on a couple of FBA businesses, mostly from lack of knowledge and platform issues.
- Shopify is the only e-commerce platform he considers viable. He says anyone suggesting otherwise is wrong.

## Drop shipping to private label: the Far and Away path

- He and co-founder Albert ran a drop shipping store on Shopify selling dinnerware from China. It proved product-market fit for luxury dinnerware. It hit 7 figures, scaled to about $300K/month (about $2M a year), then fell to about $100K/month.
- Why it fell: Facebook bans from long shipping times (post-purchase surveys led to negative feedback and low ad ratings) and the iOS update, which broke cross-device tracking and conversion data.
- Exit to private equity at a "crappy" multiple, under 2x, because the business was declining. The earn-out paid only about 70 to 80% because the buyer went bankrupt.
- He recommends e-commerce in 2023 for starting out. Use drop shipping (including onshore suppliers) to test products fast. When a product wins, source it, get it into a US warehouse, use 3PLs to fulfil, then private label and build a real brand.
- With the proceeds, they built Far and Away properly: visited 14 factories in Portugal over two weeks and partnered with a family-owned factory. COVID logistics and shipping costs nearly made them question the business, but it is now solidly profitable and has opened new revenue channels.
- Sourcing from Portugal rather than China makes COGS "absolutely sky high", a trade-off they accepted to build something they are proud to recommend.

## Launching with Kickstarter and validating products

- Far and Away's first customers came from a Kickstarter campaign that raised about $100K all in, used to pay for the first inventory batch and confirm everything worked.
- Why Kickstarter: you do not need much cash and do not have to hit the factory's minimum order quantities. You visit the factory, shoot strong video and imagery, and launch.
- Traffic: Facebook ads plus Kickstarter agencies. Agencies have mature pixels of Kickstarter backers and email lists, so they retarget well, for a "fat fee". They also built their own list with their own ads.
- Kickstarter works "almost like SEO". Ranking on the front page is "a guaranteed six figure" campaign.
- Test product-market fit before launching a new product. A Kickstarter for French artisanal scented candles "flopped so hard". They had spent $20K producing assets, but it saved them the cost of a full launch.

## Facebook ads and conversion diagnostics

- He says his partner runs the ads, but gives these keys: test many creatives, get targeting right, and mature the pixel.
- Maturing the pixel means burning money early to feed Facebook purchase data. After purchases come in, create look-alike audiences.
- His drop shipping launch: he and Albert each put in $5K. They burned $8K total, were about to pull the plug, and then one ad took off and paid for everything. "You just need one."
- Always run conversion-optimized campaigns optimized for purchase, never for events further up the funnel, even with zero purchase data, so Facebook can match look-alikes from other campaigns.
- Diagnostics when ads get traffic but no sales:
  - Cost per click under about 50 cents suggests a good ad.
  - Otherwise, look at the funnel (cost per view product, add to cart, initiated checkout), compare to industry norms and find the drop-off. It is typically add to cart.
  - Most often the problem is the website: no CRO (sticky add to cart, one-click purchase), slow load times (5 seconds and "it's over"). A set percentage of visitors will always convert, depending on the site.

## E-commerce margins and extra revenue streams

- Margin rule: 70 to 80%+ gross margins (a $100 product should cost $15 to $25 to acquire). Shipping and weight count toward gross margin. Lower margins are possible only if the ads are exceptional.
- Dinnerware was a headache because of dimensional weight and breakage, now eased with insurance.
- He argues every e-commerce store should add display ads and affiliate revenue. Most stores run 5 to 10% net margins, and these streams lift that substantially.
- Far and Away's blog is not for selling products. It earns affiliate revenue (about $4.5K/month, the biggest non-e-commerce income there) and was about to turn on display ads, estimated at about $2K/month. His illustration: a $50K/month store at 10% net makes $5K/month free cash flow, and these streams add several thousand more.
- Benefits: raises the valuation, takes growth pressure off the core business, and lets them reinvest faster. He says e-commerce sites are currently favorable in Google because they do not look like niche sites gaming display and affiliate revenue.
- Epic Gardening is his example of a company doing display well.
- He says e-commerce should be run as a cash-flowing business, not VC-funded. He cites Allbirds, Casper and Made.com (whose domain sold for about $2M at a fire sale).

## Niche sites, AI and acquisitions

- AI risk: display-ad content sites answering simple queries ("can dogs eat lettuce") will be hurt because AI answers in one line.
- Still viable: comparison and "best X" sites (for example best dog food), because people still shop by comparing and clicking links.
- Acquisition example: a mid-six-figure portfolio of niche sites that came with a custom-rate Amazon affiliate account paying 8% on home goods, versus the typical 2 to 3%. The previous owner was a large Amazon affiliate site owner who got hit by Google. He bought it mainly for the rate card.
- Moving any Amazon affiliate site onto that account adds immediate value, three to four times by his estimate. Payback on some sites drops to about a year, after which it is "pure profit". He is stacking recurring revenue this way and accepts the risk the rate could go away.
- He is now looking for distressed businesses, particularly VC-funded e-commerce brands that are underwater.

## Link building service

- His agency's productized service sells backlinks. He says it is "not trade secret", just a lot of work requiring many VAs.
- Process: scrape sites and their emails, email offering to buy backlinks, negotiate wholesale rates by promising volume as an agency. Gross margins run about 40 to 80%, and he keeps the spread.
- Side benefit: he never has to worry about links for his own niche sites and builds the best ones for his own businesses.
