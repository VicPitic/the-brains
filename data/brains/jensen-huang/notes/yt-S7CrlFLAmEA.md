## Judging AI doom predictions: track the record and demand root-cause engineering

- Jensen calls the quantified extinction predictions "made up". He says they come from researchers, are not grounded in science, and are "irresponsible" to publish.
- He argues the track record of such predictions is poor, and people should be held to it:
  - Radiology would be fully taken over by AI with no radiologists left. The opposite happened: AI automated scan reading, and the world needs more radiologists than ever.
  - Within 6 to 12 months, 90% of code would be AI-generated. He says this proved wrong.
  - Within 6 to 9 months, 50% of entry-level jobs would be wiped out. He says this proved wrong.
- His line: "We have to take accountability for all of the stupid predictions that were made."
- He says safety and leadership are "false choices". America can innovate, execute fast, lead and be safe at once.
- On whistleblowers, he says they must be taken seriously, and he credits the one in question with courage. He separates that from the scientific forecasting, which he says is not grounded in science.

## Regulation should solve actual problems, and safety is an engineering discipline

- His rule: "regulation should solve actual problems." So the first question is which actual problems have occurred.
- He notes the real incidents so far came from the frontier labs, because they have the most compute and work on the hardest problems. A high school student or a startup lacks the compute to cause a frontier-scale incident.
- He describes the labs as moving from research to engineering while building the company, culture, technology and products at once, so he understands it is "a little bit hair on fire".
- His prescription after any incident:
  - Root-cause it from an engineering perspective.
  - Ask what could have been done differently.
  - Implement and institutionalise the fix in technology, methods and processes.
- He bets the labs now have sandboxes, runtimes and continuous monitors. If a lab truly had no control, other companies should send engineers in, but he doubts that is the case.
- He supports third-party evaluators, comparing them to financial auditors:
  - They need not be as expert as the company, only ask the right questions.
  - He agrees there should be several, so one cannot be captured or influenced.
- On recursive self-improvement (RSI), he calls it a combination of sensible ideas: in-context learning, skills, reflection, reinforcement learning, synthetic data, and LoRA-style weight improvements, with the base model retrained later on that experience.
- He says RSI will not spiral out of control, because a product must still be evaluated and regression-tested before release. Better control comes from methods, tools, verification and evals.

## Open and closed models: use both, and win by exploiting the technology

- He says the world needs both. He uses as many closed frontier models as he can, and used four the weekend before.
- His analogy: closed models are like bottled water. Water is free, and "you use the right water in the right places". It is the same as electricity and other commodities.
- Reasons open models are needed: sovereignty, privacy and proprietary technology.
- He cites that $400 billion of venture funding went into AI-native companies in the last 6 months, and 80% of them use open models. Without open models, they could not build their dream.
- He argues winning the AI race is not about a few tech companies. It is about every company, industry, researcher, teacher, student and startup.
- On Chinese open models, he says much of the world's open-source contribution now comes from China, which has far more engineers. But once you download a model, "it's yours": you fork it, improve it and make it your own, as with Linux and Kubernetes.
- His view of the race: it is about who exploits the technology best. The last industrial revolution's inventors (Maxwell, Volta, Ampère) were not American, yet America took social advantage of it better than anyone.
- He says China's narrative is pragmatic, an economic and social advance, with no groups saying it will end civilisation.
- He advises engineers that coding is "just typing". His favourite key is backspace, because "the best software is the smallest software". He expects engineering to continue and grow after typing.

## Nvidia's strategy: go as high as needed and as low as possible

- His strategy: "go up as far as we need to and as low as possible", then "let a thousand flowers bloom".
- He would rather help everybody succeed than take a slice. That is why Nvidia runs every model: a year and a half earlier it ran essentially only OpenAI, and now it runs Gemini, Grok, Meta's models and others, with Anthropic scaling up on its platform.
- Examples of building the layer others need:
  - Without cuDNN, the frameworks would not exist.
  - Without Megatron-Core, large-scale training would not have happened.
- He builds frontier models in five domains, out of need rather than to disrupt anyone:
  - Alpamayo, the first "thinking" self-driving model. It reasons about novel situations, so it needs less road data. Many car, ag-tech and truck makers cannot build the whole stack, so Nvidia builds it and they adapt the last mile.
  - Biology models: ESM2 protein language model, ESMFold, OpenFold, cuEquivariance, and Proteina-Complexa for synthesising next-generation proteins. He says Lilly and Merck need these and cannot build them yet.
- His test: build it because Nvidia has the skills and customers need it.

## Capital allocation: find ecosystem bottlenecks and fund them early

- He calls AI a new industrial revolution that needs manufacturing: intelligence is produced, so infrastructure must be built.
- He says the industry is mostly about applications and the infrastructure layers (data centres, construction, electricity, power generation), not just models and chips.
- His method: look across the whole ecosystem for bottlenecks and constraints, and back extraordinary companies being built where supply must scale, such as land, power and shell.
- He thinks about the long-term supply chain more than most, because Nvidia's scale means many companies must support it (Corning, Lumentum, TSMC, memory makers). He started working with them long before the growth arrived so the growth could happen.
- He is "surprisingly uncompetitive" in the sense of not wanting to own every layer. He would be happy with five hyperscalers.
- Why he backs regional clouds (NCPs):
  - Their early customers were the hyperscalers, which plan once a year while the market is so volatile that they are almost always wrong.
  - Regional clouds are agile and know their state or country, so they can secure land, power and shell better than someone in Seattle or Palo Alto.
  - Countries now see compute as strategic, with some reserving power for domestic companies. Nvidia helps neoclouds there grow, for example in Australia and Southeast Asia, bringing on gigawatts.

## Company culture: quiet, apolitical and consistent

- He says companies like these "ought to be built the way we used to build companies, which is in silence".
- No one speaks for the whole organisation. He says employees accepted this when they joined, and told them how people behave in the company.
- Nvidia is apolitical and bipartisan. It wants America to succeed and will help whatever government is in place.
- No political discourse inside the company. He tells staff to "take it home", and the same goes for race and religion.
- Core values he says attract people: taking care of families, creating conditions for people to do their life's work, meaningful work done quietly, and contributing to everyone else's success.
- He values consistency and stability, and says employees are very happy.

## Long-term thinking and the pace of the frontier

- He thinks in decades: "I've got to think about what happens next decade and decade after that", so two or three years "is just a click".
- On China's lithography, he expects it to get there by 2030. He says China is very good at high-volume production, so it is a matter of time.
- On Elon Musk's Terafab, he says "if anybody could do it, he can". He notes Nvidia knows process technology and memory well. He says Musk's superpower is that once he decides to do something, it is hard to stop him.
- He says slowing down is "definitely the wrong strategy".
- He says AGI is already here. Superintelligence is here in narrow domains, such as self-driving (he cites one-tenth the accident rate of humans) and protein synthesis and virtual screening.
- He is having fun on the frontier and wants "all of America to come with us". He asks for less drama and says the future is "too good not to be" part of.
