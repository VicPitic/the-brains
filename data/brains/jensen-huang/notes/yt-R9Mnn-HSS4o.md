## Building the first AI supercomputer (DGX) from first principles

- In 2016 Nvidia was getting into AI itself and working on autonomous vehicles. Jensen says the team asked what kind of computer is needed for "this new way of doing software".
- AlexNet was the trigger. It was built for computer vision, shattered records set by decades of computer science, and needed Nvidia GPUs to produce.
- Nvidia reasoned about the implications for everything, from chips to systems, operating systems, data centers, networking, algorithms and applications. It concluded a new type of computer had to be created.
- The result was DGX, a deep learning system and "AI supercomputer". Jensen announced it as something built for Nvidia itself, expecting only some interest. He then delivered the first one outside the company to OpenAI in 2016.
- The rethink went chip by chip:
  - The CPU was no longer the ideal processor, so the GPU evolved into an AI processor. A graphics chip built to simulate the virtual world turned out to be a near-perfect starting point for understanding the world.
  - The PC architecture suits personal computing, not giant AI systems that learn from data. The IO and networking had to change.
  - Nvidia bought Mellanox to change how computers connect, so that millions of GPU cores work together instead of a handful of CPU cores.
- Why it is hard: a phone or PC application fits in a few gigabytes. GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters, fits on no phone or single PC, needs a giant data center to train, and many computers to run. Training takes weeks, so shaving off half the time is measured in weeks.
- One of his illustrations of the scale of chip R&D: the R&D budget for one of Nvidia's chip generations is probably around $5 billion, which he says is more than building a rocket.

## Why AI is an "iPhone moment" and the next industrial revolution

- Jensen's pattern from about 40 years in computing: a platform grows slowly and linearly, then phase-shifts abruptly, like water turning to ice. Examples:
  - The PC existed about 10 years before Windows 95 changed everything.
  - The internet was used by researchers for 5-8 years before Mosaic and Yahoo.
  - Cloud and the iPhone followed the same pattern.
- Each transition makes computers easier to program. Mainframe applications are few, while iPhone applications number over five million.
- AI is the first computer anyone can program with human language, with no Python, C or Java needed. He says the number of people who can program went from tens of millions to several billion, closing the technology divide.
- Industrial revolution framing: the last one produced steam and electricity, and mass-produced physical goods. This one produces information and intelligence. "The production of intelligence is going to be what all companies do in the future."
- Nvidia runs AI factories. Data goes in, improved software comes out every day, and the software helps design chips, run self-driving cars, do computer-vision quality inspection and build better software.
- Companies hold deep domain knowledge, and AI puts that knowledge in every employee's hands.
- On timing and innovation: he says the insight behind ChatGPT was seeing that the technology was close to useful. The jump from GPT-2 to GPT-3 turned a marginally useful product into an incredibly useful one. The iPhone, PC, internet and Google search followed the same pattern: "the innovators are the ones that realize the timing is now" and industrialize it.
- He calls ChatGPT "unquestionably the single best software product the world's ever made". His reasons are that it does surprisingly amazing things, is the easiest product on the planet to use, has no manual, and had been used by over 100 million people.

## Productivity gains: chips, climate and software engineering

- **Chip design:** Nvidia's chips are the largest and most complex in the world, now impossible to build without AI. He compares it to organising a city 1,000 times bigger than New York, placing every building and routing traffic and infrastructure. The chips are a couple of inches per side, about two-thirds the area of a coaster.
- **Weather and climate:** AI that simulates weather multi-physics is already 10,000 to 50,000 times faster than numerical methods. His point is that you can't act well on a problem you can't simulate, and a simulator helps decide where to put resources. It also helps with infrastructure, flooding and dams, insurance, agriculture and regional impact, and it uses less energy.
- **Software engineering:** software engineers are Nvidia's single greatest expense. Microsoft estimated that some 40-50% of code on GitHub is produced by AI (Copilot-style program completion). Jensen says Nvidia's estimate is that it will improve its engineers' productivity by a factor of 10.
- He declines to put a number on society-wide productivity gains.
- He sees a particular opportunity for countries without many computer scientists, and names India, Southeast Asia and Africa. Developed countries gain from lower cost and acceleration.
- On jobs: "it's less likely that in the near term that AI is going to displace our jobs, it's more likely that someone uses AI is going to displace our jobs." Some jobs change, some are created (such as AI data scientists), and some are displaced. Society should understand and adopt the technology fast.

## Where AI goes next: digital biology, world models and assistants

- **Digital biology and drug discovery:**
  - Having learned the language of humans, AI has now learned the language of proteins and chemicals.
  - A protein's 3D shape determines its function, as a plier differs from a hammer.
  - From a desired function, AI can synthesize candidate proteins with better solubility, or proteins that capture energy from light or break down plastics and oil spills.
  - Drug discovery is expensive, slow and low in success rate, and this improves the odds.
- **Intelligence as perception, reasoning and planning:**
  - Progress on perception is strong. The goal is a model of the world, both static and dynamic ("if I did this, what would happen to that").
  - There are many worlds at different scales (human, molecular, atomic, galactic), sometimes needing different physics.
  - Reasoning must achieve objectives within core values and principles, keep people safe, and be explainable, interpretable and transparent.
  - Planning must be efficient and cost-effective.
  - AI is progressing along all three, with robotics and autonomous vehicles as examples.
- **Multimodality:** GPT-4 learns from language and images together. His example is that if you know horses and are told a zebra is a horse with black and white stripes, you can imagine a zebra you've never seen.
- **Digital assistants:** he expects personal assistants and group or company assistants for HR, IT and programming. Assistants could also model a company's business or the effectiveness of a new product.

## Regulation, openness and the societal view

- Jensen says AI is powerful and can be used for harm, so regulation is necessary: "We regulate salmon for God's sake, we should regulate AI." He says governments have no choice but to step in, as with food, drugs, transportation, chemicals, electricity and broadcasting.
- Regulators should engage with the technology and put guardrails in place so it advances helpfully. He says there were none in place at the time.
- He notes that regulating free expression is hard, but what generative AI produces can be regulated, since there are things it should not generate.
- He wants the technology democratized as best as possible, and says it is less good if it lands in the hands of one company.
- Whether OpenAI stays open is "a company choice", and he says they have the right to do that. He adds that plenty of large language models are open, so access is not what holds anyone back. What matters is "the willpower" and the insight on timing.
- He sees fake news as a real harm, since AI can generate it and also detect it.
- He is cautious on geopolitics and says it is hard to say how AI will change US-China relations.

## Building a company that lasts: character, resilience and "how hard can it be"

- Nvidia was started in 1993 when Jensen was 30. He says nobody in the company knew how to build a company, he didn't know how to be a CEO, and he had never used a PC.
- Skills like raising money, organising the company and recruiting were learned along the way. He says skills are learnable.
- The entrepreneur's attitude is "how hard can it be?" He applied it to learning the PC, building a company, hiring, and later to AI, scientific computing and autonomous vehicles. "It turns out all of those things were super hard", but he says you want to go in with that attitude. He pairs it with reasoning from first principles and learning as fast as possible.
- What made Nvidia successful, in his view:
  - A perspective on the future that spans a long arc and is directionally right. He says Nvidia's was.
  - Above all, the character of the company: how resilient it is, how it handles adversity and new assumptions, and how agile it is when the world changes.
- Culture is people: "It's not made of the document that describes the culture", nor the core values inscribed on the building. It is how people overcame existential crises, through their agility, cleverness, ingenuity and will.
- The values of learning and agility are installed by talking about them, teaching them and living them.
- Nvidia suffered greatly in its first 15 years, with one adversity after another. Five, six or seven times it was existential. He credits that suffering for the company's strong chance of long-term success.
- He describes corporate character as "the utter incredible ability to suffer", doing something even in extraordinary pain.
- On his own persistence, he quotes his 2003 Stanford line: "my will to survive exceeds almost everybody else's will to kill me". He says that once he believes in something he keeps doing it until the company is great at it, and he is hard to discourage or distract.

## Jensen's work habits and advice to young people

- **Work habits:**
  - He describes himself as focused, curious and a perfectionist.
  - He distinguishes hard work from "insanely hard work", and puts himself in the second category.
  - He wakes at 5am and starts working immediately, works every day, and is asleep by around 9:30pm. He values sleep.
  - He relaxes through work (solving problems and achieving things), reading and family time.
  - He recently read *Chip War* and skims many AI papers, trying to understand all of them.
- **Advice to young people:**
  - Be a learner.
  - Above all, "think from first principles". He says most advice he received over the years was irrelevant because it was an opinion, a perspective of the time, or based on wrong assumptions.
  - He is sceptical of "find something you love". He says he loved every job he had (dishwasher, busboy, paper delivery, waiter), and every day at Nvidia.
  - His alternative is to fall in love with what you're doing. Once you desperately want to do a good job at it, doing it well and doing it hard comes more easily.
