## Why Jensen says he would not build Nvidia again

- Asked if he would do it again, he says "absolutely not", and thinks most people who say yes are being dishonest.
- His reasoning: the question is not whether he loves the outcome (the contribution to the world, the joy for employees, the benefits). It is whether he would start if he knew how hard it was and did not know the result.
- What he would be signing up for: how hard it is to build Nvidia and CUDA, how long it takes to get the world to use CUDA, plus the pain, suffering, embarrassment, humiliation and setbacks.
- His warning to his 30-year-old self: it will take much longer than you think, you will deliver horrific financial news, activists will come after the company, you may have to lay people off, and you may go out of business.
- His conclusion: "the pain and suffering necessary, the endurance necessary to do something great" are routinely forgotten.
- On the 2007-era CUDA spending while the stock was down, he calls it "embarrassing" and "humiliating". He describes being "the only face that everybody hates", with employees probably embarrassed for him.

## Forgetting yesterday: how he handles pain and long timelines

- He spends "all of my life trying to forget yesterday". Revisiting past setbacks triggers anxiety, which is why he dislikes talking about his past.
- He compares it to athletes: "Forget the last point."
- He frames high tech as a "marathon sprint": full out every day, and Nvidia has taken 34 years and is still at it.
- His method: look forward with promise, enthusiasm and deep belief. Learn from the setbacks, codify the learning, make sure it goes into "every fiber in your body", then let go of the pain.
- He calls this healthy but says it takes a lot of discipline.
- On conviction: he was never worried CUDA would fail. "It would have been harder to give up." The idea was already fully real in his head, so the only questions were when it would succeed and which application would come first.

## Founding Nvidia and the near-death of NV1 (1993-1997)

- Chris and Curtis kept asking him to join their startup. He had a good job at LSI Logic, two kids, a wife at home and "$30,000 in the bank".
- The seed idea: the PC was the first general-purpose computer. Bring Silicon Graphics-style 3D graphics (like Jurassic Park) to a personal computer at an affordable price. A Jurassic Park-class machine cost about $1 million; the target was about $300.
- The NV1 sold 250,000 units, but the algorithm (forward texture mapping) was wrong. The right one was inverse texture mapping, which DirectX in Windows 95 used. All 250,000 came back. He calls it a technology disaster.
- Sega had contracted Nvidia for $12 million (1994-95) to build the NV2 and NV3 for its console. Continuing meant two more years on a flawed architecture, while 30 competitors did it the right way.
- What he did:
  - Flew to Japan and told Sega's CEO the architecture would not serve them, and recommended Sega find another partner.
  - Asked him to convert the remaining contract money into an investment, saying his first job as CEO was to keep the company alive. Sega turned the remaining $5 million into equity.
  - Cut the company from about 250 people, laying off two-thirds.
- At 32, he had around six months of cash and could not iterate on silicon. He bought a leftover emulator from a failed company (IKOS) for about half the company's remaining money, so the chip could be verified in software before tape-out. It worked well enough to save the company (the Riva 128).
- On the team: it was existential, and everyone was "super calm, super focused", reasoning step by step that this was the only chance.

## Disruption, focus and software-first strategy

- Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* shaped him. His takeaway: the moment technology becomes "good enough" and overserves the market, it is ready for disruption.
- He says Nvidia was the disruptor at every step. Riva 128 and the first HPC GPU were imperfect but good enough, and initially looked "toyish".
- Nvidia was the first graphics company built to focus fully on one application industry: video games.
- From the start he treated applications as what creates industries, so the company worried about software and downstream developers, not only the chip.
- His stance on markets: he does not want Nvidia fighting for a point of share in someone else's market. He wants something really hard that makes sense to him first, in a direction nobody has gone.

## CUDA: carrying a platform on GeForce for years

- Origin: he wanted physics (fluids, particles, explosions) in virtual worlds, which pushed the GPU toward general-purpose parallel programming. He was constantly looking for algorithms that only ran on supercomputers and could reach many more people on a PC.
- Early uses (seismic processing, CT reconstruction at Mass General, molecular dynamics) came from universities and did not pay the bills. Only gaming did.
- CUDA launched in late 2006. From roughly 2006 to 2012 the stock was weak, and he describes the period as years of pain.
- His reasoning: "We believed on first principles this should be quite useful." The problem was chicken-and-egg:
  - A new architecture needs a large installed base to attract developers.
  - Developers are needed for the killer app.
  - The killer app is needed to sell the hardware.
- His answer: put CUDA on GeForce so it reached every PC, at the cost of gross margins going "from bad to horrible". CUDA was given away free. GeForce has carried it for 25 years.
- Tactics:
  - A "CUDA everywhere" initiative.
  - Evangelising to universities, researchers and companies, with him pitching personally.
  - Technology demos, and asking developers (for example imaging software) to port to CUDA.
- He admits he thought about being too early and running out of money "all the time", and says there were "900 other smart strategies" to keep the company alive.
- Researchers such as Ian Buck and later the Toronto deep-learning group bought GeForce cards from retail.
- When AlexNet won ImageNet, the leap over prior algorithms caught his eye. He asked what it was, why it worked, where it could go and what it meant for chips and computers.

## Becoming an AI company: first principles and bringing people along

- He says his method is to break things down to first principles, then ask what the foundations are, how far it can go and what it means for the industry. He calls himself good at connecting dots and systems thinking.
- Before sending the 2013-era email declaring the company an AI company, he had already held tens of engagements with groups across the company. By the time the email went out, "everybody's already been brought along". He then sets the direction.
- On people: he deeply believes in the idea, helps others believe it, and the company follows.

## Leadership style, resilience and luck

- On being tough: "I don't think people care about style." They care about values and what you care about. Be tough on the same things every time, and once the moment is over, it is over. People must know they are safe, that you love them and want them to succeed.
- He says he works harder than ever because he wants everything he has to reach his people and partners. Being "pure and high integrity" and fully there for them lets him be quite challenging while they know he is on their side.
- Decisions: he has taken "several hundred forks in the road", mostly the right one, through judgment, values, friends, informed decisions, critical thinking and some luck.
- The company built a system for self-reflection: don't take yourselves too seriously, don't be so hard on bad decisions, adjust quickly and never let a bad decision be fatal.
- The people who help with the next fork will likely differ from those who helped with the last, so you must find them.
- He felt safe taking risks because of Lori, the kids and the employees behind him. His summary: "It's always personal."
- Humility and being grounded help: "Not doing this job from a yacht is helpful."
- Supply chain: build resilience through diversity and redundancy where possible, and do your best where you cannot.

## AI fears, jobs and advice for students

- He did not sign the 2023 extinction-risk letter. He is worried about safety but wants worry channelled into building safer technology, as the auto and airline industries do, rather than repeating doom narratives.
- He argues for speeding up AI innovation, because innovation makes it safer. Today's AI hallucinates and lies less.
- His greatest fear: the US scares people out of using AI. He says Western sentiment is "really out of control" and that China is not buying the doom narrative.
- Radiology example: a decade ago experts predicted radiology would vanish. AI now does scans faster and more accurately, yet demand for radiologists has gone up because scans are cheaper and hospitals see more patients. His point: "a job has a purpose and a task". The task is reading scans, the purpose is diagnosing disease. The harm was young people avoiding the field.
- He says the opportunity for a new graduate in computer science, software or chip design today is far greater than when he graduated, with tools "a billion times more capable".
- On Nvidia headcount, he says AI makes little difference to the number of employees. Each will have hundreds or thousands of AI assistants, and expectations rise: "Things that takes 10 years, I think it'll take one year."
- On Taiwan, he is less worried about hot conflict than most. He urges balanced, nuanced policy and not pushing adversaries to the wall.
