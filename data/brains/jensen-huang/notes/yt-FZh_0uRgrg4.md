## Early struggle at Nvidia: Sega contract and the lesson on being CEO

- Jensen started Nvidia at 30 with Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem, wanting "a new kind of computer, one that could solve problems ordinary computers could not."
- They had "absolutely no idea how to build a company, raise money, or run Nvidia." His attitude was "How hard could it be?" and the answer was "super hard."
- The first technology didn't work and the company nearly ran out of money.
- He flew to Japan to tell Sega's CEO that the technology Sega had contracted Nvidia to build would not work. He asked to be released from the contract, and then asked Sega to still pay Nvidia. Without that money, "Nvidia would vaporize."
- He calls it embarrassing, humiliating and one of the hardest things he has done. Sega's CEO, Hayao Nakayama, said yes.
- Lessons he drew:
  - Being CEO "is not about power, but the responsibility that comes with keeping the company alive."
  - Honesty and humility can be met with generosity and kindness, even in business.
- Nvidia used the money to reset the company. Out of desperation, it invented new ways of designing chips and computers that it still uses today.

## Resilience: failure as a learning moment

- Over 33 years Nvidia has reinvented itself repeatedly, each time asking "How hard can it be?" and each time learning "It's harder than we thought."
- Jensen says never to see failure as the opposite of success. Each failure is "another learning moment, a humility moment, a character-strengthening moment."
- "The resilience forged through setbacks is what gives you the strength to go again."
- He is one of the longest-serving CEOs in technology. Nvidia, shared with 45,000 colleagues, is his "life's work."

## Origins: immigrant background and work ethic

- Jensen is a first-generation immigrant. At 9, his father sent him and his older brother to the US, to a Baptist boarding school in Oneida, Kentucky (coal country, a town of a few hundred people).
- Two years later his parents left everything behind and joined them with "little to nothing." His father was a chemical engineer; his mother worked as a maid at a Catholic school.
- His mother woke him at 4:00 a.m. to deliver newspapers. His brother got him a dishwasher job at Denny's, which "felt like a major career advancement."
- His view of America: "Not easy, but full of opportunities. Not a guarantee, but a chance."
- He studied at Oregon State University and met his wife, Lori, as sophomore lab partners. They have been married 40 years, and both of their children work at Nvidia.

## AI as a complete reset of computing

- Jensen has lived through every major platform shift: mainframes, PCs, the internet, mobile and cloud. Each built on the one before. He says what is happening now "is bigger than anything before."
- For 60 years, humans wrote software and computers executed instructions. He says "That paradigm is over."
- The shifts he describes:
  - From human coding to machine learning.
  - From software on CPUs to neural networks on GPUs.
  - From following instructions to understanding, reasoning, planning and using tools.
- A new industry has emerged to "manufacture intelligence at scale." Because intelligence is foundational to every industry, every industry will change.
- Timing for new graduates: his career began at the start of the PC revolution, theirs begins at the start of the AI revolution.
- He credits Carnegie Mellon as a birthplace of AI and robotics: the Logic Theorist in the 1950s, widely considered the first AI program, and the Robotics Institute founded in 1979.

## Engaging AI with optimism, responsibility and safety

- Every major technological revolution created fear alongside opportunity. When society engages technology "openly, responsibly, and optimistically," it expands human potential far more than it diminishes it.
- He calls AI one of the most powerful technologies humanity has created, with both great promise and real risks. The task is "to advance it wisely."
- Responsibilities:
  - Scientists and engineers must advance AI capabilities and AI safety together.
  - Policy makers must create thoughtful guardrails that protect society while still allowing innovation.
- "Societies that retreat from technology do not stop progress. They only surrender the opportunity to shape it and to benefit from it."
- His four things to do at once: advance safely, create thoughtful policies, make AI broadly accessible, and encourage everyone to engage.
- "Everyone should have AI. Opportunity should not belong only to the people who can code."

## AI and jobs: tasks versus purpose

- Jensen says AI will change every job, but "the task and the purpose of a job are not the same." Many tasks will be automated and some jobs will disappear, but many new jobs and industries will be created.
- Examples:
  - Software engineers use AI to expand the search for solutions and tackle more ambitious challenges.
  - Radiologists are elevated to better diagnose disease and care for patients.
  - Demand for both software engineers and radiologists continues to grow.
- "AI does not replace human purpose. It amplifies human capability."
- "AI is not likely to replace you. But someone using AI better than you might."
- Everyone becomes a programmer: a shopkeeper can create a website, and a carpenter can design a kitchen and offer new services. He says this can close the technology divide for the first time.

## The AI infrastructure buildout and advice to graduates

- He says AI, like electricity and the internet, will need trillions of dollars of infrastructure investment: "the largest technology infrastructure buildout in human history."
- It is a chance to reindustrialize America and restore its capacity to build: chip factories, computer factories, data centers and advanced manufacturing. He addresses electricians, plumbers, ironworkers, technicians and builders directly: "this is your time."
- It also drives major energy investment: modernizing the grid, expanding power generation and accelerating sustainable energy.
- Advice to the Class of 2026:
  - "We are all standing at the same starting line."
  - "Run, don't walk."
  - Take up Carnegie Mellon's motto, "My heart is in the work," and "build something worthy of your education, your potential, and the people who believed in you long before the world did."
