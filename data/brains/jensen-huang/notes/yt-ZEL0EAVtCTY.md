## Founding Nvidia: two ideas and the chicken-and-egg problem

- Jensen and co-founder Chris Malachowsky (the transcript renders the name inconsistently, so it is not named here) started Nvidia at the beginning of the PC and CPU era, when "everything was about general purpose computing" and Moore's law.
- **Idea one:** a CPU cannot be the right format for every interesting problem, such as real-time computer graphics (one of the hardest problems in computer science at the time) or simulation. They imagined a second tool that would offload work unsuited to general-purpose computing and turn the computer into a supercomputer. His analogy: there is a right tool for the right job, and a house, garage or company with only one tool is badly equipped.
- **Idea two:** how to get the company started at all. General-purpose computing is about 64 years old, and a positive cycle of install base, sales and applications reinforces it. Getting developers to consider a new architecture is a chicken-or-egg problem that "has never been solved" apart from Nvidia.
- His answer was to find a first application that needed the new architecture and also had enough volume to spread it. Computer graphics was a tiny market at the time (essentially Silicon Graphics), so they chose 3D graphics for video games.
- He calls the business plan "impossible to fund", with multiple chicken-and-egg problems. Sequoia Capital and Sutter Hill still invested, and the company attracted top computer scientists.
- Conviction rested on first principles: "the general-purpose computer cannot possibly be the only computing platform."

## Finding new markets for accelerated computing, from graphics to deep learning

- Jensen frames computer graphics as a simulation of the world, and AI as a simulation of the mind. Simulation happens concurrently and in parallel, so it suits a different architecture from step-by-step "recipe" execution.
- The first application was computer graphics. The second was seismic processing, or inverse physics, along with CT reconstruction and ultrasound. The third was molecular dynamics (Newtonian physics). "We just kept finding one problem after another."
- The GeForce 3 had a 57 million transistor GPU, and Jensen says it was the first time the graphics processor was as programmable as the CPU, with an instruction set game programmers could target.
- Deep learning arrived when researchers including Andrew Ng (Stanford), Geoff Hinton (Toronto) and Yann LeCun (NYU) reached out to Nvidia. Jensen says he was alert enough to see a problem Nvidia could contribute to.
- The resulting computer vision capability was something "no one had ever imagined". It prompted questions about why it worked, what else it could do, how far it could go and what it meant for computer science and the industry.
- His method: "step by step by step, we broke everything down to its first principles", then rebuilt where Nvidia should be in that world.
- Nvidia built for about a decade before anyone paid attention.

## Conviction, grit and keeping a team motivated without feedback

- Jensen says Nvidia "suffered our way here" because nobody believed in it. He describes the work as endeavoring something with "no positive feedback, no external motivation."
- His traits for it: reasoning, vision, strategy, discipline, patience and belief, "at the core of grit."
- Leadership recipe for keeping people going:
  1. Go back to core values and core beliefs, and ask why you believe the future will happen.
  2. Show you are determined to pursue it.
  3. See the future in your mind's eye, even though nobody else can.
  4. Tell the story so everyone else can see it too.
  5. Believe it yourself.
- He applies the same logic to America, Stanford and Nvidia: each has core foundations that explain its purpose.
- He says an entrepreneur must be "desperate to succeed": "If you don't work hard every single day, you will perish." He compares this to his immigrant parents, who had "nothing to fall back to", and says his constant desperation to do better at Nvidia is the same feeling.

## First principles as the response to uncertainty

- When he is uncertain about the future or things are moving too fast, "you always go back to first principles."
- The questions he asks: What are my core values? What makes me great today? What do I aspire to?
- He says it is possible to feel three things at once: grateful for what you have, unsatisfied with where things are, and aspiring to greatness.
- He argues that "during technology change, during world change is the only opportunity for greatness. Status quo, it's really hard to make a difference."
- The same first-principles approach was behind Nvidia's founding thesis that general-purpose computing could not be the only platform.

## Advice to students worried about AI and jobs

- Dive deeply into AI and use it in every possible way. The goal is mastery of using AI in your own field, not only mastery of the science.
- Separate the tasks of a job from its purpose. Examples:
  - A doctor's purpose is to care for people, which is different from reading a radiology scan.
  - An engineer's purpose is to solve a known problem, or discover problems that have never been solved and are worth solving.
- His rule: "every job's purpose consists of tasks but not defined by the tasks."
- He tells young people that AI is a moment of opportunity rather than a reason for fear.

## Cautious optimism and the five-layer AI cake

- Jensen calls himself a "cautious optimist". Intelligence is the foundational ingredient of almost everything in society and industry.
- "Cautious" means advancing the technology quickly so that it works as promised, is functional, and doesn't produce something that sounds intelligent but is flawed. His point: "Functional things are safer", and AI needs to function as promised like a car does.
- He defines AI as a five-layer cake, and says the US has to win every layer:
  1. Energy
  2. Chips (his layer)
  3. Infrastructure (cloud services)
  4. Models (where "everybody talks", but not the only layer)
  5. Applications: healthcare, military and defense, cybersecurity, transportation, manufacturing
- The application layer matters most to the nation, and whoever advances it most will exploit the industrial revolution the most.
- Leadership can change during a technology inflection, even for a leader. Policy should not hinder the application layer.

## Why America enabled Nvidia

- Jensen calls his path "a chain of extremely low probability events" and says America "provides tailwind, not headwind."
- What entrepreneurs rely on:
  - Understandable, dependable laws and rules.
  - A business environment where everyone plays by the rules, so you can find underserved market segments.
  - Confidence that a market you serve "won't be foreclosed on you randomly, arbitrarily, unknowingly, unpredictably."
  - Risk-taking and a willingness to fail, with the chance of a second chance.
- Immigrants arrive by choice with little expectation and great hopes, and appreciate everything. He says the immigrant and entrepreneurial spirits are "rather similar".
- He calls Nvidia "genuinely an only in America story", achieved in one generation, and says he is "the embodiment of the American dream."

## Designing with simulation, and the Nvidia campus as an example

- The new Nvidia building was fully designed in simulation first, using a supercomputer.
- They simulated the building for every hour of the day across an entire year and kept adjusting until it was "perfect."
- Design choices from the simulation:
  - Light wells in the roof let in sunlight only where needed, since extra sunlight means extra heat to remove.
  - An undulating roof tracks the sun.
  - Sloped walls (the "baseball cap") move offices further back from the low afternoon sun.
- The goal was maximum use of California sunlight with high energy efficiency.
