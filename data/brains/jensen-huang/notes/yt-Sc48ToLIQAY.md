## Why accelerated computing became necessary

- Jensen traces modern computing to the IBM System 360, which he learned from. He says its architecture and strategy still govern the industry.
- In the 1980s he was in the first generation of VLSI engineers, trained on Mead and Conway's textbook from Carver Mead's work at Caltech.
- CPUs and Moore's Law drove the mass production of software, which became a "$3 trillion industry". When he was a student, "the concept that you could make money selling software was a fantasy."
- Transistor scaling and instruction-level parallelism have slowed CPU gains while demand keeps growing exponentially. He says the widening gap, left unaddressed, means computing energy use and cost inflation "would eventually stifle every industry."
- After two decades of CUDA, accelerated computing is the path forward. Offloading time-consuming algorithms to a GPU built for parallel processing routinely gives 10x, 100x, sometimes 1,000x speedups, saving money, cost and energy.
- Domains accelerated include:
  - ray tracing and computer graphics
  - gene sequencing
  - scientific computing and astronomy
  - quantum circuit simulation
  - SQL data processing and pandas
- He calls it NVIDIA's "first great contribution" to the industry and to society. Costs can keep falling while computing requirements grow.
- His reasoning: a 100-fold saving in time, cost or energy will trigger a new development somewhere else. The industry did not know what until deep learning arrived.

## Betting on deep learning after AlexNet

- In 2012, Hinton, Krizhevsky and Sutskever trained AlexNet on NVIDIA CUDA GPUs and won the ImageNet Challenge. Jensen calls it the "big bang of deep learning."
- His point is that NVIDIA's decisions after AlexNet transformed the company. They were based on principled thinking and the company's own analysis of how deep learning scales.
- The belief: deep learning might be a "universal function learner" for problems that are hard or impossible to express from first principles. Its limits might be set only by model and data scale.
- The dilemma:
  - NVIDIA was small then, and massive GPU clusters could cost hundreds of millions of dollars.
  - There was no assurance scaling would work, and no one knew how far it could go.
  - His logic: "if we don't build it, they can't come."
- His rule: "when the company believes something, we should go act on it."
- Over the next decade NVIDIA reinvented every computing layer: the modern GPU, interconnects, systems, networking and software.
- It invested billions "into the unknown," with thousands of engineers working for a decade without knowing how far the technology would go.
- In 2016 NVIDIA announced DGX-1, its first AI supercomputer. Jensen delivered the first one to OpenAI, then a startup nobody knew.
- In 2022, 10 years after AlexNet and about a million-fold more computing later, OpenAI launched ChatGPT.

## The new computing model and AI factories

- NVIDIA went from a graphics company building GPUs to an AI company building data-center-scale supercomputers.
- The stack changed: GPUs process large language models trained on supercomputers, instead of CPUs executing instructions written by programmers.
- Computers are now "intention-driven rather than instruction-driven": tell a computer what you want and it figures out how.
- Future AI applications will understand the mission, reason, plan and orchestrate teams of large language models. They will assemble teams of experts and use tools, much as people do.
- AI's inputs and outputs are tokens, floating-point numbers that embed intelligence.
- Companies are building a new kind of data center that produces tokens: "AI factories." He compares AI token generators to Tesla's AC generators as the factories of a new industrial revolution.
- He says this model and industry emerged because "we reasoned from first principles, formed our belief about the future, and we acted on them."
- On AI's pace: it is the only technology he knows that is advancing on multiple exponentials at once. He told students to "run, don't walk" to engage with it.

## Setbacks that led to robotics

Jensen contrasts the AI factory strategy, which came from reasoning and deliberate action, with robotics, which came from a series of setbacks.

- NVIDIA invented the GPU and programmable shading in 2000, and wanted GPUs in every computer.
- A sequence of markets was built, then lost:
  1. **Chipsets for AMD CPUs:** went from zero to about a billion dollars almost overnight. AMD bought ATI and no longer needed NVIDIA.
  2. **Intel connection license:** led to work with Apple on what became the first MacBook Air. Intel then terminated the agreement.
  3. **Mobile SoC built on an ARM license:** the world's first SoC that was a full computer. It excited Google and led to work on Android devices. Qualcomm then refused modem connections. There were no other LTE modem companies, so NVIDIA had to exit mobile.
- This repeated roughly yearly: build something successful, then be pushed out a year later.
- With no markets left, he chose one with no customers, reasoning that "where there are no customers, there are also no competitors, and nobody cares about you." That was a "$0 billion market": robotics.
- NVIDIA built the world's first robotics computer, running deep learning, over 10 years ago. Today's robotics computer does not even need graphics.
- The next wave of AI is robotics, where AI has a physical world model as well as a language model. NVIDIA works with hundreds of companies on robots, robotic vehicles, pick-and-place arms, humanoids and robotic warehouses.
- The lasting gain was agility and a culture of resilience: "Our company is really hard to distract and really hard to discourage, and no setback that comes our way doesn't look like an opportunity these days."
- His advice: "Swiftly, shake it off." There is another opportunity out there, "or create one."

## Resilience as a superpower

- Of his abilities, he says intelligence is not top of the list. What he values most:
  - enduring pain and suffering
  - working on something for a very long time
  - handling setbacks and seeing the opportunity around the corner
- He calls these his "superpowers" and says pain and suffering strengthen character, resilience and agility.
- Setbacks strengthen a company's character as well as a person's: "Each time, we gained skills and strengthened our character."
- He says sacrifice, enduring pain and suffering are qualities graduates will need.
- He also cites a strategic retreat as one of NVIDIA's best strategies. He credits intellectual honesty and humility with saving the company during the canceled Sega game console project.
- He says his own longevity comes from not going out of business, not getting bored and not getting fired. He has run NVIDIA for 31 years and is the longest-running tech CEO.

## Craft, priorities and having time

- At the Silver Temple in Kyoto, he met a gardener picking dead moss with a bamboo tweezer in a garden about the size of a courtyard. The gardener said, "I have cared for my garden for 25 years. I have plenty of time."
- Jensen's lesson: when you dedicate yourself to a craft and your life's work, you have plenty of time.
- His morning routine, identical every day:
  - He keeps a clear priority list and does the highest-priority work first.
  - Before he gets to work, his day is already a success, and he can spend the rest of it helping others.
  - When people apologize for interrupting, he says "I have plenty of time", and he means it.
- He hopes graduates find a craft to spend a lifetime perfecting. It need not be decided on day one or soon.
- He says to prioritize your life and you will have time for the important things.

## Advice to graduates

- Believe in something unconventional or unexplored, but let it be "informed" and "reasoned." Then dedicate yourself to making it happen.
- You may find "your GPU, your CUDA, your generative AI, your NVIDIA."
- See setbacks as new opportunities.
- Engage with AI now: use it, play with it and enjoy it, because it is changing so quickly.
- Understand that computing is being rebuilt "from studs on up," because computers are "the single most important instrument of knowledge" and will transform every industry.
- Jensen says he does not love giving advice, especially to other people's children, so he frames it as stories from his own experience.
