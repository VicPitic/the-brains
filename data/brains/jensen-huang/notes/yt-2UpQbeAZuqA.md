## From retrieval to generation: why AI computing needs "factories"

- Jensen says modern computing dates to the IBM System/360 in 1964. IBM was then the most valuable company in the world. The model stayed largely the same for about 60 years: pre-recorded computing.
- Under that model you write a story, program, photo, song or video, save it to a file, and retrieve it later. Recommender systems only make the retrieval smarter.
- That is why the buildings are called data centers. Jensen notes they are not called "computer centers", because they mostly store data.
- AI inverts this. Each time you use it, you supply context and a query, and the output is generated fresh for that circumstance.
- He argues that in future every pixel, sound, video, ad and story will be generated for the individual's context, not retrieved. That means far more "generators", which is what Nvidia builds.
- His first-principles lens: ask what happened to the computer that made this possible, not only what AI can do.

## What AI can do, and why agentic AI is the commercial turning point

- Two years earlier, generative AI translated "anything to anything" (text to text, text to image, image to text). Jensen calls it interesting and "a little cute".
- Generation enabled internal thinking, step-by-step reasoning and problem-solving. It also enabled generating control commands to use tools such as a browser, spreadsheet, Photoshop, PowerPoint or AutoCAD.
- Generating commands for a mechanical system is robotics. Generating commands for a vehicle is self-driving cars.
- Value comes from work, not knowledge: "We love people who are know-it-alls, but we don't pay them for it. We pay for people who do work."
- Agentic systems (he names ChatGPT, Codex and Claude Code) now do useful work, and people pay for AI by the hour, for example "$30 an hour" or "$20 an hour". He calls it the fastest-growing software business in history.
- Nvidia runs hundreds of thousands of agents internally, all guard-railed and sandboxed, talking to each other and solving problems.
- He expects an internet used today by about a billion people to become mostly agents, possibly a hundred billion, working around the clock. Companies, employees, cars, robots, manufacturing systems and buildings would all be agentic.

## The AI factory as the new dynamo

- Jensen compares three layers that "cocoon the planet": energy (the grid), communications (the internet) and now intelligence.
- The dynamo turns atoms (motion from water, wind, fire, steam) into electrons. An Nvidia AI factory turns electrons into numbers (tokens). Tokens can be recombined into language, math, proteins, human biology, physics, climate, 3D and robotics. "That's why I call it a factory."
- He expects intelligence to become a commodity used everywhere.
- Hardware figures he gave:
  - One rack holds 72 chips, weighs 2 tons, costs $4 million and has 1.5 million parts.
  - He calls it the most expensive piece of equipment in the world.
  - Nvidia manufactures about 8 million chips this year (his wording: "call it 8 million").
- Economics: each gigawatt of AI factory is about $50 billion. He says one such factory generates $300-400 billion in intelligence, so the return on investment is "extremely fast".

## The five-layer cake: where to invest in AI

Jensen offers this as the industrial map for investing.

1. **Energy.** He calls it the greatest opportunity in generations for energy industries and the first time in roughly 100 years that grids in many countries can be invested in. Any form that produces energy will get funded: nuclear, wind, solar, hydrogen. He cites Siemens, Mitsubishi and GE Vernova as doing well.
2. **Chips, computers and networking.** This includes switches and silicon photonics.
3. **Infrastructure.** Land, power, shell, money and data center operations, all in scarce supply today.
4. **Models.** OpenAI and Anthropic are the two names people hear, but he says not to overlook everything else.
5. **Applications.** These are startups in financial services, legal, accounting, transportation and logistics. About $100 billion of venture capital went into this layer last year, which he calls the largest VC year in history.

Scale claims:
- About $1 trillion goes into the whole stack this year.
- He guesses the AI ecosystem could reach about $20 trillion a year. He frames this as a guess.
- He says it was zero two years ago.

## Models beyond language: anything with structure can be learned

- Jensen says the model layer is more than the two language-model companies. AI can learn the meaning of anything with predictable structure.
- Examples: proteins, genes, cells, physics, 3D worlds and human anatomy. He asks what a gene or cell means, not only how to sequence or edit it.
- Learning the meaning of a cell is analogous to learning the meaning of a word. Combining two words creates a new meaning, and combining two cells does something similar.
- To the computer it is all tokens. Computer scientists must work out how to represent each domain's information so the machine can understand it, reason, plan and act. He calls this the "intelligence loop".
- Unpredictable things are hard to learn. He gives a quantum-like example: if people floated, had body parts in 17 places and could be seen through, there would be nothing to learn.
- He puts the physical-world industries at about $80 trillion and calls them the most important frontier, and one not much discussed.

## Jobs, task versus purpose, and the radiology example

- Jensen's advice on fear: "You may or may not lose a job to an AI. But you will absolutely lose a job to someone who uses AI." He says he is certain of that for himself, so the first step is to make sure he uses AI.
- He calls the Terminator, singularity and "20% chance of ending humanity" narratives "complete nonsense". His argument that builders understand the technology is that it improves every year: "If you don't know how something works, how do you make it better?"
- He says the scare narrative can serve people who want to benefit from it themselves.
- Safety evidence he gives: hallucination has dropped to almost nothing compared with two years ago. Models now research, reflect on and question their own answers. He compares it to cars being both better and safer than 100 years ago.
- His two responsibilities:
  - The technology industry must build AI safely.
  - Everyone else must engage with AI and tell family, employers and countries to do the same.
- Task versus purpose:
  - A job is related to its tasks but not the same thing. His own CEO job involves typing and talking, which AI does at superhuman level, and he is busier than ever.
  - Radiology: a leading computer scientist warned about 10 years ago that it would be wiped out, and advised against entering the field. Computer vision did permeate every radiology stack, yet demand and headcount for radiologists rose.
  - The reason is that the purpose of a radiologist is to work with doctors to diagnose disease. Automation made departments more productive and profitable, so hospitals admitted more patients and hired more radiologists.
  - He says careless predictions did harm, because fewer people trained as radiologists.
  - Software engineering: he is hiring more engineers than ever. "Typing is not the job of a software engineer. Coding is not their job. Solving problems is their job."
- Elevating crafts: a plumber could become a designer, a carpenter a home designer, and a furniture salesperson an interior designer.
- Jobs created: he says the trillion-dollar buildout is creating more jobs than ever at every layer.

## Closing the technology divide

- Jensen has spent about 40-odd years in computer design, during which computers grew more complex and the share of people who could program them shrank.
- He estimates that about 2% of society knows C++. Now anyone can program a computer in human language.
- He calls AI "the greatest force for eliminating the technology divide in my entire career" and says "We got to bring everybody with us."
- He says a country or company that does not invest in AI misses both the jobs boom and the elevation of its people.
- He tailored the talk to a global, mostly non-technical audience. The goal was to give them enough intuition to reason about their next investment, such as recognising when something that looks like a $100 billion market is actually far bigger.
