## AI as a new industry that manufactures intelligence

- Jensen says computer science has been reinvented: software used to be hand-coded (he started with Fortran, then Pascal, C and C++) and ran on CPUs. Now machines observe data, learn the patterns and relationships, and generate neural networks that run on GPUs.
- He breaks problem solving into three steps: perception, reasoning and planning. A self-driving car is a "digital chauffeur" and an AI reading a CT scan is a "digital radiologist".
- Many digital agents consuming a data center make it look like it is producing "tokens", meaning digital intelligence.
- His analogy is the AC generator. General Electric and Westinghouse built the generator and also invented its consumption (light bulbs, toasters). Copilot and ChatGPT are the light bulbs and toasters of AI, connected to a digital intelligence factory.
- He frames this as a new industry that takes energy in and puts digital intelligence out. It never existed before, just as AC power generation did not before it.
- He describes generative AI as a "universal translator": text to image, protein to text, text to chemicals. Being able to understand the meaning of difficult data helps a digital biologist find a needle in a haystack, a chip designer, or someone searching for new materials.

## Scaling laws and the million-times drop in the cost of computing

- Moore's law rested on VLSI scaling (Carver Mead and Conway) and Dennard scaling. Doubling every 1.5 years gives about 10x every 5 years and 100x every 10 years.
- The AI scaling law is empirical: the bigger the neural network and the more data it is trained on, the more intelligent it seems. He says it appears to be continuing.
- He names three phases where more computation gives better answers:
  - **Pre-training:** learning from all the data. He compares it to graduating from college, a milestone that is "not enough".
  - **Post-training:** going deep into one domain, using reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback, synthetic data generation and multi-path learning. It is like choosing a career.
  - **Thinking (test-time scaling):** breaking a problem into first-principles steps, iterating and simulating outcomes. The longer it thinks, the higher the quality may be.
- He wants AI answers people "largely trust" rather than having to judge whether they are hallucinated. He says that is several years away, so computation must keep increasing.
- His view of NVIDIA's biggest contribution: over 10 years it cut the marginal cost of computing by a million times. When the cost of something falls that far, habits change. Researchers no longer think twice about having a machine learn exhaustively from enormous data, and that is why machine learning took off.

## Leading for decades: learning, confidence, vulnerability and serving others

- He started NVIDIA without ever taking a business course and says he has never written a business plan.
- He treats NVIDIA as his "life's work", not a job. He says this mindset matters whether you are a carpenter, an artist or a neurosurgeon.
- A CEO must reinvent themselves constantly. What you know today will be useful but "not even nearly enough". He learns every day, for example by using an AI as a tutor. He asks it why it gave an answer, for step-by-step explanations and for analogies, and says "I torture my AI to teach me."
- **Confidence is not certainty.** A leader can be fully confident in a direction and still leave room for uncertainty, which lets them keep learning. "Uncertainty is your friend not your enemy."
- **Strength is not the absence of vulnerability.** People feed off a leader's strength, but a leader who needs help should ask for it. He says he asks for help constantly.
- **Decide for others, not yourself.** Leaders are trusted when every decision serves the mission and other people's success: colleagues, partners, the ecosystem and the supply chain. When asked what to do in a discussion with a key partner, he asks what is in the partner's best interest and decides from there.

## Flat organisation: 60 direct reports and reasoning in the open

- He has about 60 direct reports, and he corrected Harry's figure of 50.
- He reasons through strategy in front of everyone, so the whole team hears the same thing at the same time. It is not "I went into a forest and came back out with an answer."
- He usually speaks last, to state the direction and priorities and remove any ambiguity.
- Once aligned, he counts on everyone being an adult. They should say when they don't know something and ask for help. "Nobody loses alone, nobody fails alone."
- When the CEO shows it is fine to be vulnerable, uncertain or wrong, everyone else can do the same. He calls his staff the best in the world, people who "don't need my help" beyond that.

## AI for science, emulation and building a medical school

- He introduced AI at the supercomputing conference in 2018 and met great skepticism because AI was a black box. He says it is more explainable now: you can probe an AI the way a professor probes a student, asking it to reason step by step.
- AI emulates the physics from observed data and does not solve from first principles. He argues emulation is invaluable, because we know equations like Schrödinger's and Maxwell's but cannot simulate large systems with them.
- Human biology spans nanometers to years, and first-principles solvers cannot handle that. His hope is a digital twin of human biology, so digital biologists and climate scientists can understand large multiscale systems for the first time.
- For HKUST's new medical school he sees a rare chance. Most hospitals started as hospitals and then tried to insert technology, which met skepticism and distrust. HKUST can build from the ground up with technology and AI as the founding expertise, by people who understand its limits and potential.

## Universities need shared AI supercomputers

- He says AI supercomputers are the instruments of science today, like radio telescopes or particle accelerators. Advancing science at scale is impossible without machine learning, and that needs machines.
- The structural problem is that each principal investigator raises their own funding and then won't share it. Machine learning needs a gigantic machine for a short time, and nobody needs all of it forever.
- Computer science researchers at Stanford or Harvard can raise a lot. Researchers in climate or ocean science struggle.
- His fix is to pool funding and build infrastructure for the whole school. Researchers also intern at companies like NVIDIA, Google and Microsoft to use their infrastructure, and professors do part-time visiting research.
- His advice: universities have to rethink how they do funding.

## Energy use: the goal of AI is inference

- He argues that if the world uses more energy to power AI factories, "we are a better world when that happens."
- Three points:
  - The goal of AI is to use the model (inference), not to train it. Inference is very efficient and could discover ways to store carbon dioxide, new wind turbine designs, and better materials for energy storage and solar panels.
  - AI doesn't care where it goes to school. Supercomputers can sit slightly off the grid and use sustainable energy, away from where people live.
  - AI can make a heavily over-provisioned power grid smarter and cut waste. He hopes it saves 20-30%.
- His conclusion: using energy for intelligence is the best use of energy.

## Robotics, physical AI and the Greater Bay Area

- Today's language models understand cognitive knowledge but not physical intelligence, such as a cup not passing through a table. AI must be taught the physical world.
- His reasoning: if generative AI can produce a video of him picking up a coffee cup from a prompt, it can generate the tokens that make a robot arm do it. He says the leap to general robotics is "very very close".
- Robots scale only with a technology flywheel: high volume funds high R&D, which brings breakthroughs and better products, which raises volume further.
- He says only three robot types can reach high volume, because they can be deployed into the world as it is today (brownfield deployment): cars, drones and humanoid robots. Humanoids will be the highest volume, because we built the world for ourselves.
- The Greater Bay Area is the only region where mechatronics and AI technology reside together. Japan and Germany are strong in mechatronics but "quite far behind on AI". He urges the region to lean in.

## Advice for students and founders

- He started NVIDIA after promising his future wife Lori that he would be a CEO by 30, which he calls his pickup line, and he says he had no idea what he was talking about at the time. He tells students that dating won't hurt their studies.
- Keep learning constantly, and treat your work as your life's work.
- Be confident without needing certainty, and be strong without hiding vulnerability.
- Ask for help early, and make decisions in the interest of the mission and other people's success.
