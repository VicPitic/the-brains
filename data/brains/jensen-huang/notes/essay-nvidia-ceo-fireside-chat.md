## NVIDIA as a platform company and the five-layer AI stack

Jensen defines a platform as "something that you build other things upon." He calls NVIDIA the largest pure-play technology company the world has known: "We create technology out of nothing. Our final product is pure technology." Social media, e-commerce and search companies are technology companies whose business models are something else. NVIDIA's business model is the technology itself.

He describes AI as a five-layer cake:
1. **Energy** at the bottom.
2. **Chips and systems**, where NVIDIA sits.
3. **Infrastructure**: software (CUDA plus hundreds of other libraries), but also land, power, shell and financial services, because the capital required is enormous.
4. **Models**: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok are four of roughly 1.5 million AI models. Others understand genes, proteins, chemicals, physics, quantum, robotics, long time-series (finance) and multimodal healthcare data.
5. **Applications**: healthcare, entertainment, manufacturing, self-driving cars, transportation.

He says this is why NVIDIA works with every AI company in the world. "We don't build self-driving cars, but we work with every single self-driving car company in the world. We don't discover drugs, but we work with every single drug discovery company." He treats CUDA, an architecture invented 25 years ago, plus its libraries and algorithms, as the platform. He is wary of the word "moat" and sees it more as the language the whole ecosystem speaks.

He also notes that the first industry NVIDIA created was modern gaming. He says NVIDIA is the world's largest gaming platform, with 300 million active users, 100 million of them on Nintendo Switch.

## Handicapping the US-China AI race layer by layer

Jensen says his "China is winning" headline dropped its caveats. He argues the race should be judged per layer, not as "ChatGPT versus DeepSeek."

- **Energy:** China has twice the energy the US has, with a smaller economy. The US is flat while China is growing "straight up." He says US energy costs are higher to begin with, and China discounts energy for chip companies by 50%, so US costs are "probably call it four to eight times."
- **Chips:** the US is "generations ahead," but he warns against complacency. "Semiconductors is a manufacturing process. Anybody who thinks China can't manufacture is missing a big idea."
- **Infrastructure:** in the US, breaking ground to a standing AI supercomputer takes about three years. "They can build a hospital on a weekend."
- **Models:** US frontier models are about six months ahead. But most of the 1.4 million models are open source, and China is "way ahead on open source." Startups, university researchers, teachers and scientists all depend on open source. He points to Linux, Kubernetes and PyTorch as examples.
- **Applications:** he cites a poll claim that about 80% of Chinese people expect AI to do more good than harm, and says the US would be the reverse. He wants AI described practically, as automation, not through science-fiction framing. "Whoever applies the technology first and most wins that industrial revolution." His analogy: electricity was invented in the UK, but the US applied it faster and more broadly.

## Why conceding China is a strategic mistake

Jensen says NVIDIA is "the first company in history that has been banned on both sides," by the US and by China. He admits he is being "a little cute" and that the situation is more nuanced. His points:

- The US has conceded the second-largest AI and technology market in the world.
- "Growing somewhere else" will not replace China, just as no market replaces the US. The US should compete there, while respecting Huawei as one of the most formidable technology companies ever: agile and fast-moving.
- The export controls did not set China's AI industry back. Its semiconductor industry has "double, double, double" while the West grows 20-30% a year. Compounding at those rates, "doesn't take long to catch up."
- China has nine of the top 10 science and technology schools (his estimate), about 50% of the world's AI researchers, and 70% of last year's AI patents.
- With the US out, China will build its own complete stack and export it quickly. The danger he names is a world where the US ends up "buyers, not sellers."

## Ecosystem and first-mover stickiness

Jensen frames technology proliferation as an ecosystem play. A platform that arrives first and builds applications and relationships on top becomes "sticky" and an essential, dependent part of the ecosystem.

- NVIDIA was 25 years early with CUDA. "For a long time, nobody paid me any nevermind," which gave him years to build ecosystems, applications and relationships across industries.
- Huawei did the same with 5G. A policy-protected home market of about a billion phone users gave it scale. It then exported through Belt and Road, and he says there is now an "AI Belt and Road."
- His conclusion: China will diffuse its technology as fast as possible, because getting there first builds the ecosystem and makes the platform hard to dislodge.

## Industrial policy, reindustrialisation and national security

Jensen agrees industrial policy should intervene "when a dramatic action needs to be taken." He names three actions:

1. **Energy growth.** "There are no new industries you can grow without energy." Services only need calories, but manufacturing needs electricity. He praises the pro-energy turn and says energy was vilified too long.
2. **Reindustrialising America.** Manufacturing is the largest segment of the economy and was offshored for 20 years. To fix social issues, prosperity has to reach every segment, not just people with PhDs and college degrees. NVIDIA is building three kinds of factories in the US at once: chip factories, supercomputer factories and AI factories (data centers). He committed to building half a trillion dollars of AI supercomputers within the Trump term. He credits Taiwan (TSMC in Arizona, Foxconn, Wistron, Amkor, SPIL) and South Korea (memory) for helping stand up the supply chain.
3. **Technology leadership as national security.** US technology used worldwide is a strength that helps keep the US safe.

His three-part export framework:
- Safeguard sensitive technology from adversaries.
- Make sure American companies get "the best and first."
- Then proliferate American technology standards and compete globally, to fuel the R&D flywheel that funds the strongest military.

He says economic security and national security "go hand in hand." He says he comes to Washington to explain what AI is and to warn that some policies could have "unintended consequences" that are "quite dire."

## Energy as the binding constraint and the pace of compute

Jensen calls the energy constraint "deeply serious." His prescription:
- Use every form of energy.
- Don't rely on the grid. "Build behind the meter."
- Accelerate nuclear, and get energy growth "very, very shortly."

On NVIDIA's pace and scale:
- No company of its scale has introduced new generations every year.
- A GPU for AI data centers weighs two tons, has 1.5 million parts, consumes 200,000 watts and costs $3 million. He uses this to dismiss smuggling claims: you would need enough of them to fill a football field.
- Annual generations improve performance at about the same power by roughly 5x to 10x, and so energy efficiency improves by the same factor.
- Demand is growing faster, because AI is more compute-intensive and adoption is rising. His phrase is demand rising by a factor of "10,000, a million times each year." He says NVIDIA will keep chasing it, "but the bottom line is we need energy."

## Robotics: why it is around the corner

Jensen's reasoning:
- Video AI can generate an arm picking up a cup pixel by pixel, token by token. "The AI can't tell the difference between it manipulating pixels, versus it's manipulating a bunch of motors."
- What remains is embodying cloud AI in a physical mechanical system.
- China has demand (a coming severe worker shortage), AI technology, and strength in mechatronics (electronics-mechanical integration). It has "the harmony of demand and supply side capability."
- Japan and Germany have demand and mechatronics but need better AI.
- The US, if it reindustrialises, will have strong demand and software, but must improve its mechanical and electronics capability.

## Jobs, tasks and how to approach AI

Jensen says every job will be affected, some will become obsolete, new ones will be created, and every job will change. His key distinction is **task versus job**:
- A prominent AI scientist predicted radiologists would lose their jobs within five years. Years later every radiology platform is AI-transformed, yet the number of radiologists has increased. Studying scans is the task, and diagnosing disease is the job.
- A financial analyst's task is spreadsheets. The job is giving financial advice, helping customers and analyzing markets.
- 100% of NVIDIA's software, hardware and other engineers have AI assistants and AI coders, and "they're busier than ever."

His advice: before deciding AI is something to fear, "go engage it, go use it." He uses AI to speed up first drafts of speeches, and says the original thought and voice must remain his. "Original thought, original writing, your taste made with human hands are still always going to be valuable."

He is "1,000%" optimistic and says he won't miss the next two decades. He expects them to do more for science, industry and the nation than all prior periods combined.
