## Accelerated computing and driving marginal cost toward zero

- Jensen says Nvidia has spent about 31 years on accelerated computing. General-purpose computing is "ill equipped" for some problems, so Nvidia built a new way of computing for algorithmic work that can be parallelised.
- In that domain, the marginal cost of computing went to approximately zero. Over the last 10 years Nvidia cut the cost of deep learning by 1 million times.
- People told him that cutting cost a million times would mean customers buy less. He says the opposite happened. Cheap compute made something "insanely amazing" thinkable: scraping the internet's digital human knowledge into one computer and letting it find the program.
- Software moved from being written by humans to being written by computers grinding on data.
- His analogy: if flights took a month, he would never go to New York. When the marginal cost of something falls to zero, people do things they would never have considered.
- He expects another million-times gain in deep learning compute over the next 10 years.
- He calls AI, enabled by accelerated computing, probably the single greatest invention of the technology industry and the most important thing of the 21st century. He leaves the transistor comparison to history.

## The H100 and the future of one continuous learning loop

- Jensen describes the H100 as a 70 lb system of 35,000 parts, of which 8 came from TSMC. It costs about a quarter of a million dollars per chip.
- One H100 replaces a data center of old CPUs. The cables alone in the old system cost more than the chip.
- It works as a team across chip, algorithm and data-center level, with miles of cables. The next generation is liquid cooled.
- Today the process is "learn then apply" (train, then inference). He expects continuous learning, where training and deployment merge into one loop, as humans learn and apply all the time.
- That loop would combine synthetic data generation, reinforcement learning and grounding in real-world data. The computer imagines, tests against reality, and is grounded by it.
- Nvidia is training models to be multimodal and grounded in physics, building a "world model" from video. It is also working on "system 2" long-thinking: reasoning and planning that cycles through options before answering.
- Future prompts might be "work on this for a day" or "spend up to $1,000 and bring back the best answer".

## Competing on inference, install base and total cost of operations

- Jensen says inference is 100% on Nvidia GPUs today, because every prompt to ChatGPT, Copilot, Midjourney or Firefly runs on them.
- Inference is hard, but not mainly as computer science. It is an install-base problem, like writing an app for the iPhone. A model deployed on Nvidia's architecture "runs everywhere", and that takes years of patience and architectural compatibility.
- Nvidia faces competition from competitors and from its customers. He openly shows customers who are designing replacement chips his current chip, his next chip and the one after. His reasoning: if you don't explain why you're good, they never get a chance to buy.
- Chips built for one algorithm lose to a programmable architecture, because software engineers keep inventing new things, including new Transformer variants. Nvidia also accelerates SQL, quantum physics, Schrödinger equations, fluids and particles. It is in every cloud and every computer company.
- Chip buyers think about chip price. Data-center operators think about total cost of operations (TCO): time to deployment, performance, utilisation and flexibility. His goal is for TCO to be so good that "even when the competitor's chips are free it's not cheap enough".
- He adds: "we don't take anything for granted."
- Customisation is possible only if it builds on the existing platform, for example a proprietary security system or new numerical processing. Each generation is five chips (GPU, CPU, networking processor, two switch types), and each tape-out costs several hundred million dollars. A fully different design would "reset" the roughly $100 billion it took to get here.

## Resilience, low expectations and "pain and suffering"

- Advice to Stanford entrepreneurs: Jensen says one of his great advantages is "very low expectations". Graduates of elite schools have high expectations, and "people with very high expectations have very low resilience".
- He doesn't know how to teach resilience, except to hope suffering happens to you. He wishes "ample doses of pain and suffering" on the students.
- Inside Nvidia he uses "pain and suffering" with glee, meaning it happily. He wants to refine the company's character.
- His reasoning: greatness is not intelligence, it comes from character. Character is formed by people who suffered, not by smart people.

## Running a flat company: 55 direct reports, no secrets

- Jensen has 55 direct reports and writes no formal reviews. He gives constant feedback and receives the same.
- Compensation for executives is the bottom-right corner of Excel dragged down. Many are paid exactly the same to the dollar.
- He holds no one-on-ones unless someone needs him, and then he drops everything. He has no meetings alone with a report.
- He never tells an executive anything that the rest of the company doesn't hear. No secret information.
- The design goal is agility and fast information flow, with people "empowered by what they are able to do, not what they know."
- On keeping people motivated after they've become rich: the answer is his behaviour. Every day he looks for chances to instil culture, in how he celebrates success and failure, talks about setbacks and results, and defines what "good" is.

## First-principles forecasting and how much compute is needed

- When asked how many fabs are needed in 5 to 10 years, Jensen says he's "horrible at forecasting" but good at first-principles reasoning about the size of the opportunity. He doesn't know the number of fabs.
- His reasoning: today, essentially all content is pre-recorded and retrieval-based. In the future AI will understand context and generate the right content for you, so most computing will be generative. He asks how that changes the shape of computing: how much more networking, memory and so on.
- His answer is that more fabs will be needed, but algorithms and processing keep improving. Compute efficiency improves about a million times per 10 years, while demand rises about a trillion times, and the two offset.
- He says one day 100% of the trillion dollars of general-purpose data-center infrastructure will be replaced, and new infrastructure built on top.

## AI and drug discovery, AGI, and who gets to program

- Biology: Jensen says the first job of AI is to understand the meaning of digital biological information (genes, amino acids, proteins, cells). AlphaFold gives protein structure, but the next step is function.
- He imagines putting a protein into ChatGPT like a PDF and asking what it is for. He calls understanding the meaning of biology probably the single most profound thing AI can do.
- AGI: it depends on the definition. An engineer needs a specification and tests. If AGI means passing a list of human tests (math, reasoning, history, biology, medical exams, bar exams, SATs, MCATs), he expects the industry to do well on all of them in 5 years. If it means "human intelligence", nobody knows how to specify that yet.
- Coding: he says he was misquoted. If you want to code, code, because it is a reasoning process. But coding won't guarantee a job, and in future you tell the computer what you want in plain English. "Human is the best programming language of the future."
- Prompting is an artistry. For example, you iterate "cuter, cuter" on an image prompt until it fits.
- About 10 million people are paid to program today, which leaves about 8 billion behind. AI closes the technology divide because everyone can program a computer by prompting.

## Geopolitics and sovereign AI

- Jensen calls Nvidia "almost a poster child of geopolitical risk". He says the US has every right to limit who gets this instrument and has exercised it.
- His response: understand the policies and stay agile so Nvidia can comply.
- Export limits close some opportunities, but they open others. In the last 6 to 9 months, he says, every country has awakened to sovereign AI: India shouldn't outsource its data so another country turns it into intelligence it re-imports.
- Countries he names as investing in their own AI: India, Canada, the UK, France, Japan, Singapore and Malaysia. The goal is protecting language, culture and national industries.
