## Inventing technology, products, strategies and markets (NVIDIA's founding approach)

- Jensen says NVIDIA is built to invent technology, then products to carry it to market, then strategies, then ecosystems and markets. He notes many inventors never had product inventors alongside them.
- In 1993 NVIDIA wanted to reinvent computing. The view was unpopular and controversial for a long time, in the era of CPUs, Moore's law and the PC revolution. Early customers were PC chipset startups such as Cirrus Logic, S3, Western Digital and Trident.
- He says it took 33 years to happen, and for the world it "happened very suddenly", with a change in November 2023.
- "In order to create the future you have to live in the future long before it happens."
- The "CUDA everywhere" strategy: he took CUDA to universities, startups, established companies, researchers, labs and conferences, sometimes to an audience of three people, pulling out his laptop to present. "You tell the story long enough, you kind of feel like it's happened."
- He says NVIDIA is the smallest GPU company by volume, even though it invented the GPU. So volume is not what makes it work.

## First-principles reasoning and spotting phase changes

- Belief should not rest on anecdote ("somebody said something"). Break reasoning down into sound first principles, anchored in physics or ground truth, and check them regularly. If they change, re-evaluate and move on.
- "If you believe in something, you owe it to yourself to do something about it."
- He constantly re-reasons through the past: which assumptions were right, which were wrong, how the company readjusted. He says this teaches you how to reason forward.
- Phase-change idea: make something a thousand times faster, larger or smaller and a phase change happens, with a surprising result.
- Why the company pivoted behind deep learning: AlexNet couldn't be the end of it, the architecture scales, and data is abundant. The expected obstacle was needing human labels, because humans would be the bottleneck. When self-supervised learning arrived, "I knew we were off to the races", and the scaling laws were unleashed.
- He says the first-principles foundations are fundamental computer science, not hunches or taste, so what is happening now is "in a lot of ways inevitable".
- Computers are learning the languages of proteins, cells and quantum systems, and representations such as 3D Gaussian splats. He compares it to the movie Arrival.

## Staying an "employee", the empty chair and 60 direct reports

- Jensen's line to Jodi Shelton in the 1990s: NVIDIA is "neither a church nor a prison". You don't have to come and you don't have to stay.
- He says the CEO reports to the board and can be let go, so it is an employment in an institution. He feels like an employee of the company, which keeps him grounded and humble. "I don't love my job every day, but I do it to my mightiest every day." He has to earn being the best person for the job every day.
- He was "raised by the company". On day one he couldn't form a company strategy, talk to shareholders, or build a culture.
- He has almost 60 direct reports, whom he describes as potential world-class CEOs elsewhere. He reasons in front of them about every decision, including successes, setbacks and adversity, so "NVIDIA has 61 CEOs".
- Hiring: "An empty chair is better than a chair filled with the wrong person." He is never in a hurry because the company keeps moving and people swarm the mission. Worst case he does it himself.
- On Colette Kress, the CFO (the host says he interviewed 22 CFOs; he doesn't give a number): in her first week she asked how long he wanted her as CFO and he answered "for as long as we shall live". The end date is only when she decides NVIDIA is no longer right for her.
- He doesn't have a formula for a great employee or leader. Everyone is smart and competent. What makes the difference is chemistry and corporate character.

## Character, pain and suffering, and a safe place to drop passes

- Getting Grace Blackwell into production "almost broke our company's back, but we wouldn't let it." He calls that 100% character, not intelligence or hard work, and says character can't be interviewed into existence.
- He believes you can bring almost anyone in and NVIDIA will instill character. The company "tortured greatness out of us" and forged character into everyone, and none of the 60 are the same people as when they started.
- Most companies lose someone after a hard stretch through blame or firing. NVIDIA keeps the team together.
- "It's always somebody's fault": the team loses together but everyone knows who dropped the pass. Nobody is fired for dropping passes, and he says he has dropped plenty. The culture is tolerance, forgiveness and learning from mistakes, as long as teammates gave everything.
- What he looks for: not a jerk, not self-serving, can answer simple questions, wants to be on the team, is transparent, vulnerable and willing to learn. "They don't have to know it all, they just have to learn it all."
- Pain and suffering is described half-jokingly as NVIDIA's "secret sauce". Fear and anxiety are real when you hold the lives of tens of thousands of people. A leader who feels none of it is a bad leader.
- His pet peeve is someone who won't answer the question asked in a hard moment, when the team needs facts fast.

## Wisdom, ignorance and having no endgame

- On whether you must sacrifice your 20s: he thinks it is a mistake to assume so, citing Morris Chang working into his 80s. In his 20s he thought faster and concentrated harder. Now he is slower but reaches right answers faster because of wisdom, patterns and strategic thinking. He will "go toe-to-toe with a 20-year-old all day long".
- Wisdom and grit about pain can be partly learned through imitation (reading, watching), but the emotional toll can't be learned without going through it.
- "Ignorance is a superpower." If he had known everything he knows now, he would never have built NVIDIA. His attitude was "how hard can it be?" He says it is impossible to build NVIDIA, but no one convinced him because he didn't know better.
- He worries about a generation made cynical by seeing too much. People need to build an internal reserve of optimism and goodness.
- NVIDIA has no endgame: "Staying in business is our plan." He has future dreams of the world but no life goals beyond staying employed and doing good work with amazing people.

## Childhood lessons on expectations and will

- His mother kept telling him he was incredibly smart, which put a burden on him to live up to it. He thinks setting expectations "beyond reason" on people or a company can make them rise to it.
- His mother, who did not speak English, taught the children English every day with a Webster's dictionary, writing each word and its Chinese translation on folded paper. His lesson: not knowing how shouldn't stop you.
- In 1973 he was the first Chinese kid in Kentucky and the youngest at Oneida Baptist Institute. At age 9 he crossed a hanging bridge with missing planks every day, rougher kids waiting on the other side, and cleaned bathrooms as his school job.
- He describes himself as not an outlier in intelligence, with intensity and care that are hard to hire into.

## How AI changes the next five years

- Computers will shift from being programmed to programming themselves under guidance: you tell them to go learn Japanese rather than teaching it.
- Faster tools make problems look smaller, like Mach 10 planes shrinking the world. Once all of the internet's data looked "tiny", researchers could feed it to a computer. Problem sizes could be a billion times larger. Many solvable problems are limited by our imagination in formulating them.
- He expects effects across digital biology, physical sciences, material sciences, traffic and smart grids (less over-provisioned energy). Companies will be more productive and profitable. NVIDIA still releases once a year but can try many more design iterations.
- Jobs: "100% of the world's jobs will change" is more likely than half being lost. More solvable problems means more work. Thought experiment: if answers come back in a second, he becomes the critical path and is busier than ever.
- He is surrounded by 60 people better than him in their fields, and uses AIs that are already smarter than him in ways, with no trouble working with either.
- AI closes the technology divide, e.g. vibe coding. He cites Cursor and Lovable, and says Lovable's CEO told him users built small businesses making $23 million a year.
- He hopes for household robots (the Disney robots he brings on stage at GTC) and thinks they could help lonely, aging people. In five years the computer is still a computer and applications are just smarter.

## Leadership as vulnerability, and what "smart" means

- CEOs are surprisingly vulnerable: "I'm the only person in the company who can't get anything done without other people's help." He often opens conversations with "I need your help" and people have been generous.
- The job is lonely "in our heads": he has talked to himself for thousands of hours through every phase shift and reinvention.
- He wants others to succeed for their own sake, and says that is part of why he gives people undivided attention. He also describes respect for craft, such as watching people cook.
- His definition of smart: technical skill combined with human empathy and the ability to infer the unspoken and see around corners. He calls pure problem-solving intelligence a commodity, noting software programming is the first thing AI is solving.
- Public speaking scares him. He says company meetings and GTC keynotes stress him more than earnings week. He won't regurgitate old talks, so each has to be fresh, genuine and useful. He calls himself a "highly reluctant CEO" but an enthusiastic NVIDIA builder.
