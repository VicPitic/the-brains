## Founding NVIDIA: the mission and the first killer app

- Jensen left LSI Logic in 1992-93 because Chris and Curtis (then at Sun) wanted to start a company and insisted he work out with them how to build it. They met at Denny's.
- The mission was to build computers that solve problems normal, general-purpose computers can't. Jensen says that is still the focus.
- The founding bet was accelerated computing, with 3D graphics as the technology and video games as the application. At the time 3D graphics meant million-dollar image generators from Silicon Graphics, and the video game market was "$0 billion".
- Don Valentine of Sequoia warned that "startups don't invest in startups". NVIDIA needed another startup, Electronic Arts, to succeed too.
- Jensen skipped writing a business plan (Gordon Bell's book was about 450 pages, and he and Lori had about six months of money in the bank). Wilf Corrigan of LSI Logic phoned Valentine on his behalf.
- His lesson: "you can't run away from your past, and so have a good past." He says he was Denny's best dishwasher and busboy: organized, "mise en place", never left the station empty-handed.

## Create the technology and the market together

- NVIDIA had to invent a new way of doing computer graphics that took a $1 million system down to a $300-$500 card that fits in a PC. It also had to create the market that would buy it.
- Jensen says this defines NVIDIA: "we create technology, we create markets." People call it a stack or an ecosystem.
- This is why NVIDIA was early in autonomous driving, deep learning and computational drug design.
- For roughly a decade (10-15 years), the markets that fuel NVIDIA today did not exist. Graphics was the only market.
- The hard part was bringing management, engineers, shareholders, the board and partners along with no evidence of a market.
- Technology that solves interesting problems is not enough. The question is whether those problems can also be interesting markets, because otherwise the company is not sustainable.

## Surviving the Direct3D reset: "how hard can it be?"

- Microsoft introduced Direct3D, which spawned hundreds of companies. NVIDIA found itself with 89 competitors, and its own architecture turned out to be incompatible with Direct3D.
- The company had to reset or go out of business, and nobody knew how to build it the Microsoft way.
- Jensen bought three copies of the OpenGL manual at Fry's Electronics for about $68 each. He handed them to the founding team, and they implemented the OpenGL pipeline "like nobody had ever implemented" it.
- The lesson was that you can succeed at inventing a future even when you start uninformed. His reflex on anything new is "how hard can it be?" He thinks it is probably "a textbook away" or "one archive paper away".
- Learning how something is done is not the same as copying it. He then returns to first principles and asks how he would reinvent it today, given current tools and conditions.

## First principles, core beliefs and early indicators of future success

- Jensen says to go back to first principles constantly, for software, computers and company structure. Software used to be monolithic and built for supercomputers, and is now disaggregated.
- NVIDIA's moves rest on core beliefs: CPUs and general-purpose computing have limits, and there are important problems a different computer can solve. Unless something changes his mind, he keeps believing and looks for evidence.
- He rejects KPIs like gross margin as results, not signals. NVIDIA instead uses "early indicators of future success" (EIOFS), which are the earliest signs of being on the right road. He used them to give the company hope while the markets did not exist.
- Examples of early indicators:
  - Programmable shaders, then Cg (about 2003, roughly three years before CUDA), written up with textbooks by Mark Kilgard.
  - Doctors at Mass General using Cg for CT reconstruction, and a computational quantum chemist using it for his algorithms.
  - Deep learning researchers needing a domain-specific language, which became cuDNN, "the SQL" of neural network computing.
  - Andrew Ng's cat detection and AlexNet, from which NVIDIA reasoned that deep learning "could change everything".
- Project selection: NVIDIA is inspired "not from the size of a market, but from the importance of the work", and the importance of the work is the early indicator of a future market. Nobody needs a business case or a P&L.
- The test is whether the work is important and whether it would happen without NVIDIA. If someone else will do it, he is happy to let them ("ultimate laziness"). NVIDIA picks things that would not happen without it, because that gives a sense of purpose.

## Staying steady through crisis

- When the share price dropped 80%, he says his pulse was the same as any other week. It was embarrassing, and he admits not wanting to get out of bed.
- His routine is to wake up at the same time, prioritize the day the same way, and "gut-check back to the core": what do I believe, and did anything actually change?
- The stock price changed, but physics and gravity did not. If the assumptions behind a decision haven't changed, "you change nothing, keep on going." If they have, you change everything.
- Leaders must be seen. Jensen says he is shy and dislikes public speaking, but he had to face employees at a company meeting after the drop, explain it without knowing how long or how bad it would be, and know some were thinking "we're doomed" or "you're an idiot".
- He credits NVIDIA's leadership team for staying, and calls it the deepest technology management team the world has seen.

## Flat organizations and management

- Jensen has about 50 direct reports. He argues a CEO should have the most direct reports, because people at that level need the least management.
- He rejects the idea that a CEO's information is so valuable it can be shared with only two or three people. He doesn't believe in a culture where information is the source of power.
- Management exists in service of everyone else. His stated job: "to create the conditions by which you could do your life's work".
- Empowerment requires context, so he keeps as few layers of "information mutilation" as possible. He reasons out loud in front of people: the facts, the data, the assumptions, the knowns and unknowns. NVIDIA has 30,000 people, which he calls "the smallest large company in the world".
- No task is beneath him (he was a dishwasher and cleaned toilets). When people send him something for review, he shows how he reasons through ambiguity, forecasting and breaking down problems. It costs him emotional and intellectual energy to get into their headspace, but he learns from it.
- He is transparent and trusts people with hard information: "you're adults here, you can handle this."
- Design the organization from first principles, not from other companies' org charts:
  - Old hierarchies existed to keep soldiers short on information. He wants employees to "question everything".
  - Organizational machinery should fit what the company builds, so computers and health care services shouldn't be organized identically.
  - Ask what the inputs and outputs are, and whether the environment is stable or constantly attacked.
  - The CEO's job is to architect the company. Culture is the operating system, set by which behavior you encourage and which you discourage. He did this reset at age 29.

## Generative AI, working backwards, and advice

- Jensen's reasoning on generative AI: everything was digitized (words, sound, images, video, genes, amino acids), and models now learn what the data means. Because they learn modalities in the same context, they can translate between them (text to image, video to text, text to text), and translation is generation of information.
- He then asks what this means for every layer of computing. Computing will shift from retrieval of pre-recorded information to generation from a seed prompt. This changes chips, systems, software and applications. He tells entrepreneurs to ask which industries get disrupted, for example networking and storage.
- Next markets he is excited about:
  - Computer-aided drug design, as EDA was for chips. He says we are close to representing and understanding what a cell means, like a paragraph.
  - Humanoid robotics, by tokenizing manipulation the way speech is tokenized.
- On regulation: he says we need technology for guardrails, fine-tuning, alignment, safety and cybersecurity to advance much faster, and he gives the aviation example of diversity and redundancy. He favors existing product and service regulators (FAA, FDA, NHTSA) adapting to AI rather than a single "super regulation", and says he has no great answer for the social implications.
- How he thinks about the future: he goes forward in time and looks backwards, working back from the end result. He wants NVIDIA remembered for one insanely hard thing it is great at and loves: advancing computing, which he calls "the single most important instrument of all humanity". He says the company is 31 years in and "still just beginning".
- Advice: "Have a core belief, gut-check it every day. Pursue it with all your might, pursue it for a very long time. Surround yourself with people you love, and take them on that ride."
- On purpose: make a unique contribution, so that people say the world was better because you were here.
