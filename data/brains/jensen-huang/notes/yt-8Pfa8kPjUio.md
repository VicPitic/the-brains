## Why pain and suffering are part of building something great

- Jensen argues that "choose your career by your passion" is incomplete. People equate passion with happiness, but great things are not easy, so you will not enjoy every day.
- He says he doesn't love every day of his job and isn't happy about the company every year, but he loves the company "every single second."
- Joy is not the definition of a good day. You have to suffer, struggle and work through hard things to appreciate what you've done. Nothing great was easy to do.
- His line to students: "I wish upon you greatness," which in his words means "plenty of pain and suffering."
- His upbringing: he immigrated in 1973 at age 9, his family wasn't wealthy and his parents worked hard. At Oneida Baptist Institute in Kentucky, as the youngest kid, he cleaned the bathrooms. He worked as a dishwasher, busboy and waiter at Denny's.
- His attitude to every job was to do the best he could. He jokes he was the best bathroom cleaner the world has seen.

## Flat organisation: 60 direct reports, no one-on-ones, feedback in public

- Nvidia's leadership team is 60+ people, all reporting directly to Jensen. He calls it not conventional but "certain" it's the best practice.
- Reason 1, information: layers of hierarchy matter. Contribution should not depend on privileged access to information. Almost everything he says, he says to everyone at once: the challenges, the direction, what is and isn't working.
- Reason 2, empowerment: equal access to information empowers people. Everyone works off the same song sheet and contributes to solving the problem. 60 direct reports removes roughly seven layers.
- Staff meetings are every other week and issue-driven. Everyone hears the reasoning of the problem and of the solution.
- This is not meant to scale downward. Below the executive-staff level, people need more supervision, so 60 reports per manager would not work.
- He discourages one-on-ones. Feedback is given in front of everyone because feedback is learning, and others should learn from it too. Reasoning through a disagreement in public teaches everyone how to reason.
- "Learning from other people's mistakes is the best way to learn," the same logic as case studies.
- He dislikes people saying "Jensen wants us to do this" or "the staff said that." Everyone should already know.

## Not giving up on people: "torture them into greatness"

- Jensen rarely fires people. He would rather improve someone than give up on them, believing people can learn if given the opportunity (he went from cleaning bathrooms to CEO).
- He learns constantly from the 60 smart people around him.
- Half-joking, he says he would "rather torture them into greatness." Coaches who believe in a team push them, because people are often close to a breakthrough and it comes suddenly, so giving up right before is the worst outcome.

## How Jensen spends his time

- He has been Nvidia CEO for 31 years (as of April 2024).
- He works from waking to bed, seven days a week, and is thinking about work even when not working. Much of that is imagining what the company can be.
- He gets his own work done before going to the office, then spends the day in meetings he deliberately selects.
- He avoids regular operational and reporting meetings, since others run those. A CEO should work on what nobody else can or is doing: stuck or off-track projects, new ideas, problem, brainstorming and creation meetings.
- "I try to live a life of purpose" and not let Outlook manage his life.

## Zero-billion-dollar markets and reasoning over spreadsheets

- Jensen prefers to be a market maker rather than a market taker, and dislikes the concept of share. He favours things never done before that are insanely hard and could make a real contribution. Such a market is "zero billion dollars" because it doesn't exist yet.
- Hard things take a long time, which filters out less committed competitors. They need resilient, dedicated people, and you can flounder for years without anyone noticing.
- Decisions start as a gut call (an intuition and starting thesis) but must be reasoned through. He hates spreadsheets because "you can make spreadsheets do whatever you want." He loves words: "words are reasoning." He asks why you believe it, why it matters and why it's hard.
- He frames this as growing the pie (new transactions, new businesses), which he shares with Patrick Collison's idea of growing the GDP of the internet. Most of the value over the coming decades isn't limited by physical things.

## CUDA: believing through a decade of pain

- Accelerated computing came from CUDA, which originated as "unified driver architecture" in 1993. He describes the path as accelerated computing, then Cg (C for graphics) to make GPUs programmable, then CUDA when Cg's programming model wasn't right.
- CUDA was "an incredible disaster overnight." It added a lot of cost to chips while there were no applications and customers wouldn't pay a premium, so gross margins were crushed. It hurt the existing $1B-ish market, and Nvidia's market cap fell to about a billion dollars.
- He did not cancel it. He deeply believed accelerated computing could solve problems normal computers couldn't, and that the sacrifice was needed to make the architecture general purpose. He opened every board conversation with what he believed and gave reasoning, not a spreadsheet.
- It took about 10 years to work. He says he "barely remembered" the suffering.
- Conviction vs stubbornness: the line is fuzzy. He gut-checks daily against first principles:
  - Distill a short list of first principles, and don't just "want" something.
  - Ask whether the principles changed, whether external conditions changed, or whether someone else solved the problem.
  - Be clever and monetize in small ways to buy time. For CUDA he sought applications in CT reconstruction, seismic processing and molecular dynamics. They weren't home runs, but they "sustained us just enough."
- The accelerated computing idea: a small part of a program's code consumes 99.999% of its runtime, and that kernel can be accelerated heavily. He says Nvidia could not be as successful in AI without CUDA.

## Accelerated computing, AI factories and the data centre buildout

- Moore's law has run its course and CPU scaling has basically stopped, while the computation the world does keeps doubling every year. Without accelerating software, the cost of computing would rise exponentially ("computation inflation").
- Arithmetic he gave: about $1 trillion of installed data centres run general-purpose computing. With industry growth of roughly 20%, about $2 trillion of computers would be replaced with accelerated computing over some years (he said pick the number of years).
- The "new industrial revolution": for the first time, high-volume production of tokens (floating point numbers) using a new instrument, the GPU. Tokens can be reformulated into English, French, proteins, chemicals, graphics, video and robotic articulation.
- He calls the new data centres "AI factories": electrons in, tokens out, as electricity once became valuable and sold per kilowatt-hour. He expects "tokens per dollar" to become normal within 10 years.
- He says these tokens will support about $100 trillion of industries on top.
- Model progress: AlexNet-era image recognition error halved yearly for about seven years until superhuman. He expects language accuracy to double similarly. Future systems will be planning models that iterate, use tools, look up proprietary data and call other agents or humans, not one-shot.
- AGI: define it as a list of measurable tests and he expects excellent results within about 5 years. Error rates on current tests are halving every six months. An undefined AGI can't be timed.

## Using AI inside a company, domain models and craft

- "You're not going to lose your job to AI, you're going to lose your job to somebody who uses AI." Companies fail because another company used AI. Engage aggressively, so you do things you thought cost too much (like asking questions freely) as the marginal cost of intelligence nears zero. Productivity leads to earnings, employment and growth.
- At Nvidia, AI explores chip design spaces overnight that would be too costly to explore manually. Software can't be written without AI because compiler optimisation spaces are too large, and AI files bugs and identifies who should fix them. He wants to turn Nvidia into "one giant AI."
- Language models learn any sequence (sound, SQL, robot articulation) once tokenised, so a copilot can sit on top of every tool.
- Domain-specific companies should fine-tune their own models. For Nvidia, the difference between 99% and 99.3% is "life and death," as with fraud detection for Stripe.
- Open source: ChatGPT democratised computing, and Llama 2 democratised generative AI by opening access to every industry and activating research.
- Craft and tenure: operational excellence comes from process, but extraordinary work comes from craft, which takes "loving care" that you can't put in a spec or process. He talks openly about love, care, craft and beauty.
- Elegance and simplicity are hard ("simplicity is not simple"). Customers can't articulate it but know it when they experience it.
- He values tenure because the team's accumulated pain, joy and knowledge is encoded in people, and he doesn't want to reset or work with new people. Small teams do great things, and Nvidia has 28,000 people.
