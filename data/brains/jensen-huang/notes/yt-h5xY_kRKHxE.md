## Staying technically current to lead a technology company

- Jensen says he is "surrounded by amazing people" who are generous teachers, and he dedicates himself to "being a good student". He loves teaching great students, and he treats learning as an effort he has to make.
- NVIDIA works across many domains, from self-driving cars to climate research to digital biology. Jensen says that breadth is why he has to keep learning.
- In a tech-driven industry, he says leaders must understand the underpinnings of the technology. That gives an intuition for how the industry will change, and for which technology is "a little bit of a left turn" and which is fundamental.
- Example: generative adversarial models, variational autoencoders and diffusion models are "cousins of each other". Seeing the impact of one can lead to a breakthrough in another, as with today's diffusion models.
- Intuition lets you extrapolate and "see down the road". This matters because solutions take several years to build, on top of technology that changes "by a factor of a thousand every few years".

## Designing the company from first principles

- Jensen starts company design from first principles: what machine are we creating, what is the input and output, what are the conditions, and is the industry fast-moving, bureaucratic or regulated.
- Goal one: a company that naturally attracts amazing people. NVIDIA's mission is to solve computing problems that are "barely possible". If normal computers can solve a problem, NVIDIA doesn't do it.
- Goal two: a company as small as possible, "as large as necessary to do the job well" but no larger.
- To empower people, he makes the organisation as flat as possible so information travels quickly. A pyramid is for command and control, "all the way back to the Roman Empire".
- There are no business units and no divisions. "Everybody works as one." The company is shaped to build accelerated computing fast. He jokes it would struggle with fried chicken, and have "no chance" with Swedish meatballs.

## Flat organisation: many direct reports, no one-on-ones

- Jensen has no one-on-ones. The host puts it at about 40 direct reports, and he agrees with "something like that".
- Reasoning: the first layer is the most senior people, who need the least management. None of his management team comes for career advice. They are experts who are "fabulously happy" and know what they are doing.
- Strategic direction goes to everyone at once. After strategising, he tells everybody at the same time, takes feedback and refines it. He asks why he would tell one person.
- Flatness, wide access to information and empowered staff make the company agile. He calls the "algorithm" well conceived and the architecture well implemented.
- The practical difficulty is getting all direct reports in one room. The odds of everybody being in the office at once are "approximately 0%".

## Direct communication, reasoning out loud, and ground truth in the room

- Jensen says he has no leadership style: "It's just me." If he dislikes a direction or has a different opinion, he says so openly. He does not take people aside or do one-on-one coaching. He admits this can be "a little too direct".
- He reasons through decisions in every meeting: why he decided something, and how the ideas compare and contrast. This teaches employees how leaders think through problems, which he sees as empowering.
- His meetings are not limited to vice presidents or directors. New college graduates and people from every organisation sit in.
- He wants the person who is most informed, best skilled or most experienced, the one who "actually made the mess" or confronted the situation. "You want ground truth."

## Top Five Things instead of status reports

- Jensen reads no status reports. By the time one reaches him it is meta information: distilled, biased and given a perspective, so he is no longer looking at ground truth.
- Instead, anyone can send an email called "Top Five Things": whatever they observed, did or learned. It can include a great restaurant or a new baby.
- Each person sends their own version, from the edge inward. It is not one shared thread. He reads about a hundred every morning.
- He keeps his own top five private. He compares it to IoT: sending his out "from the center" would contaminate the system.
- He uses the emails to check strategy. "Strategy is not words, strategy is action." If people's top five things don't reflect the strategy, they are not executing it, and what they do is the real strategy.
- He doesn't read them all every week. He samples "sporadically and stochastically" to sense whether the company is heading where everyone agreed.

## Continuous planning and changing your mind

- NVIDIA has no periodic planning system, no five-year plan and no one-year plan. "There's just what we're doing." The world is "a living, breathing thing", so planning is continuous.
- He calls five-year plans "horrible for technology" and "ridiculous".
- Big bets should be reasoned from first principles, starting with the key assumptions behind believing that the computer, chip architecture, software development or data center must change.
- Example: every company will have data centers that are not centers for data but factories that produce intelligence. Data comes in, is refined by the computer, and "intelligence" comes out. He formed this view by working backwards from first principles.
- Then he pursues it with dedication and conviction, even when it is hard. If he is wrong, he says so ("That was goofy") and changes his mind.
- Because the company replans constantly, people may not notice that he "adapted 17 times in the last year" or changed his mind "maybe 35 times". He thinks this makes leadership easier.

## Empowering people to do their life's work

- Jensen says it is a leader's mission to create the environment for others to do their life's work.
- Most important: don't make people do commodity work. NVIDIA never talks about market share. Market share implies many others doing the same thing, so he asks why he would squander talented people on work already done. He prefers to do things never done before.
- He shows this by walking away from commoditised businesses. NVIDIA has done so many times, by its own initiative or otherwise, and employees see that it won't do commodity work.
- He encourages transparency and says NVIDIA has few secrets, because siloed information disempowers people.
- He says that a sense of hierarchy is not empowering, but anyone, including a new college grad, being able to contribute in a meeting is.
