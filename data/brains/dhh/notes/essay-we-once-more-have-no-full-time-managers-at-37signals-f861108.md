## Why 37signals runs with no full-time managers

DHH says 37signals is back to its original configuration: no full-time managers whose sole function is to organize or direct the work of others. After experimenting with several management roles over a few years, the company reverted. Everyone who manages does so on the side, next to their primary work as an individual contributor, including DHH and Jason Fried.

- Managerial work still exists: yearly performance reviews, onboarding new colleagues, scheduling on-call coverage, and supervising junior work.
- Instead of concentrating these duties in a few full-time managers, the load is distributed among senior staff.
- He says the arrangement works, and that "the result is astounding" when it does.
- After "twenty-plus years of running a company like this," he says he no longer has any interest in working any other way. The couple of years with a more traditional structure were "a wonderful experience because it cemented this fact."
- His stated resolve: "We're going to make this company work without full-time managers or die trying."

## How management is minimized: automation and one mentee each

Distributing management duties incentivizes a minimalist approach to it.

- Instead of a recurring schedule of weekly one-on-ones, 37signals drives status updates and check-ins with automated questions (Basecamp's automatic check-ins feature).
- Instead of assigning five, six or seven reports to a single person, every lead and principal programmer is responsible for one mentee.
- Managers are primarily senior individual contributors, not a separate career track.

## Parkinson's Law and the zero-hour management week

DHH argues a part-time, distributed model avoids Parkinson's Law (work expands to fill the time available).

- With no fixed forty hours for managerial duties to expand into, management stays small.
- Everyone doing a small share of the management "would usually rather get back to their own work as quickly as possible."
- Some weeks the managerial overhead is literally zero, because everyone is busy getting their work done and nobody needs anything.
- He says this is impossible in a traditional setup: a full-time manager can't scale their interventions down to zero hours a week and still feel like they're doing their job.

## Managers of one, and why the boss should also be the better doer

The model depends on hiring, mentoring and promoting "managers of one": people with the competence and drive to set their own agenda and follow it autonomously.

- They don't need weekly one-on-ones with a manager to stay on track.
- They thrive on long stretches of uninterrupted time.
- This is the kind of person 37signals seeks to hire and develop.
- DHH finds it easier to steer that development when someone's primary manager is also their professional superior. You learn faster and "frankly respect that person more."
- He references Steve Jobs's quip about professional managers who "knew how to manage, but they didn't know how to do anything." He wouldn't go that far, and acknowledges many professional managers have solid experience doing things, but he thinks the principle holds when managers are not better at doing things than the people they manage.

## Where the model strains: conflict and poor performance

DHH calls it a trade-off. The hardest managerial burden to distribute broadly is dealing with conflict and poor performance.

- Plenty of senior staff are willing and able to mentor and manage juniors and peers on a solid trajectory who act as managers of one.
- A much smaller group welcomes dealing with reports who can't keep up or repeatedly get lost.
- So the arrangement has to acknowledge its limits: someone else in the organization must be able to direct, or even take over, when an employee needs strong, repeated "redirecting feedback" (he calls it a euphemism).
- Those cases "will have to bubble up to the top."

## Poor performance as a root-cause signal

DHH argues that cases bubbling up is not really a cost, because it exposes upstream problems.

- If poor performance keeps bubbling up, there is a fundamental problem somewhere in the process, which can usually only be addressed definitively by the top.
- Questions he asks: Are we not hiring the right people? Are we unclear in our expectations? Are we too diffident in our feedback?
- These are "root cause problems that require root cause reparations."

## Scale limits and the payoff

DHH is explicit that this would be difficult to maintain at grand scale.

- With hundreds or thousands of employees, you naturally yearn for more structure and hierarchy.
- That isn't the case at around 60 people, which is where 37signals is.
- The payoff: small teams of highly competent managers of one "can progress at an unbelievable pace."
- Left to just do their job, they get it done and are rewarded with job satisfaction from making a direct, personal difference. In his words: "There's nothing better than shipping quickly alongside peers you respect highly."
