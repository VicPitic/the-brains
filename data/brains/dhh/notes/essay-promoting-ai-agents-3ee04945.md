## DHH promotes AI coding agents from helpers to real contributors

- At the end of 2025, AI agents "really came alive" for him.
- Part of the reason is better models. The bigger reason is that agents were given tools that go beyond pure reasoning.
- Coding agents now control the terminal, run tests to validate their own work, search the web for documentation, and use web services through skills taught to them in plain English.
- He argues the code from this generation is "leagues ahead" of what the predecessors produced at the start of 2025.
- He says he is ready to give the current agents "a promotion". They are no longer just there to help him learn, answer questions, or check his work. They can produce production-grade contributions to real-life code bases.

## Models and harness he uses: OpenCode with Opus 4.5, Codex 5, Gemini 3 and open-weight models

- Models he names as new and strong: Claude Opus 4.5, Codex 5, Gemini 3, and the Chinese open-weight models MiniMax M2.1 and GLM-4.7.
- He says the gains show up when these models run in a modern terminal harness that gives them autonomous powers (terminal, tests, web search, skills).
- His harness of choice is OpenCode, a terminal interface for coding agents. He likes that it:
  - switches seamlessly between all of the models
  - captures sessions for sharing
  - looks "astounding" when theme-matched with the rest of Omarchy
- OpenCode becomes a default in the next version of Omarchy.
- His advice: download OpenCode, throw real work at Opus or the others, and see where things stand for yourself.

## Why agents work for him and autocomplete did not

- He never cared for in-editor AI autocomplete, the format pioneered by GitHub Copilot and Cursor. It left him cold.
- His reason: "When I code, I want to finish my own thoughts and sentences." He compares autocomplete to an overly zealous pair programmer who keeps stealing the keyboard to finish the code he was in the middle of writing. He expressed this on the Lex Fridman podcast in summer 2025.
- Autonomous agents feel different. It is more like working on a team than pairing:
  - the agents do their work autonomously
  - he reviews the final outcome
  - he offers guidance when asked

## Supervised collaboration is real today; pure vibe coding is not

- He says supervised collaboration is here now. He has worked alongside agents to:
  - fix small bugs
  - finish substantial features
  - produce several drafts of major new initiatives
- Pure vibe coding remains "an aspirational dream" for professional work, for now.
- The paradigm shift "finally feels real" to him, though results depend on what you work on and what you expect.

## Pushing back on the hype: 90%+ AI-written code and the end of programmers

- He rejects the claim that agents will put professional programmers out of work in five minutes. If you bought that pitch, he says, you will be disappointed.
- He is "nowhere close" to the claims, made by some online, of agents writing 90%+ of the code.
- He does not know what code those people are producing to hit such numbers. It is far from what he can achieve if he "holds the line on quality and cohesion".
- He forgives the excitement anyway. The leaps agents made in 2025 are "simply incredible", and he calls this the most exciting thing computers have been made to do since they were connected to the internet in the '90s.
- He understands why people extrapolate to 2026 or 2027 and get dizzy.

## Why programmers should not tune it out

- He acknowledges why some programmers want to ignore AI:
  - the hype drones on relentlessly
  - the most fantastical claims are still unsubstantiated
  - there is real uncertainty about where this leaves the profession
- He says none of that is reason enough to miss the moment.
- His message: "You gotta get in there." Try it yourself and relish "the privilege of being alive during the days we taught the machines how to think."
