## Core principle: restate hard problems as easy ones

- DHH argues that most hard problems in business, design, or programming don't have to be solved. Almost all can be restated as easy problems if you "dare question the assumptions, reweigh the trade-offs, and stop diving after sunk cost."
- He ranks this above every other principle at 37signals: it is the key to keeping pace, making big things happen with tiny teams, and avoiding "motivation-killing slogs."
- The rule is a stubborn refusal to solve hard problems of any kind unless there truly is no other choice. He says there almost always is another choice.

## Cultivate an allergy to hard problems

- He says to build a higher sensitivity to the cumbersome and to park the part of the ego that treats doing hard things as a point of pride.
- He notes this is deeply unnatural to ambitious, creative, diligent people, which makes it a rare trait.
- It usually appears natively only in people whose constraints forced it on them, such as bootstrapped founders.
- Practical reframing moves he names:
  - Question the assumptions behind the problem.
  - Reweigh the trade-offs.
  - Stop diving after sunk cost.

## Teach the habit through artificial constraints

- DHH says the aversion can be taught by adopting constraints tight enough to require it.
- Example 1: the 6-week cycles in Shape Up, the project-shaping method used at 37signals (Basecamp and HEY).
- Example 2: deliberately small teams, smaller than you might think is needed. Virtually everything at 37signals is built by two people: one designer and one programmer.
- Constraints only work if backed by a mandate from the top: when push comes to shove, the right answer is not to throw more people or effort at the problem, but to do the opposite.

## Less is the answer

- His stance on scope pressure is "Less is the answer, less is the way."
- Instead of adding resources to a struggling problem, cut the problem down.
- He frames this as the way a tiny team makes big things happen.

## Where to find purpose

- DHH advises finding purpose "in pace and progress," not in "heroic tales of climbing impossible mountains."
- This is aimed at the ego trap of equating hard-won effort with value, which he says drives teams into slogs that kill motivation.
