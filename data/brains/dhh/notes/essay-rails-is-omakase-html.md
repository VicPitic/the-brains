## Omakase: a framework as a curated menu, not à la carte

- DHH contrasts à la carte environments (pick your own ORM, template language, routing library) with Rails, which is "omakase".
- In Rails, "a team of chefs picked out the ingredients, designed the APIs, and arranged the order of consumption" for a full-stack framework.
- The menu is "personal and quirky". It "isn't designed to appeal to the taste of everyone, everywhere."
- He admits à la carte is a popular way to consume software. It also demands that you already know what you want, and he notes you rarely get your horizons expanded by always ordering the same thing.

## Substitutions are allowed, within reason

- Omakase does not mean no exceptions. Patrons can swap parts out.
- Examples he gives: don't like test/unit, bring your own rspec. Don't like CoffeeScript, delete a single line from your Gemfile.
- Defaults are a starting point. Opting out of a default should be cheap, so disagreeing costs little.
- The menu is also open to suggestions: "Good ideas are good ideas regardless of where they come from."

## Friendly suggestion versus belligerent diner

- The line between the two shows up when a suggestion is declined. The right reply is "I'm sorry, but hotdogs don't really fit our sushi menu... But thanks for the suggestions!"
- Many arguments carry on after that point. He mocks the shouting version: "BUT I REALLY DON'T LIKE UNAGI!!! ... TAKE IT OOOOOOFFFFF!!"
- His answer to that is to sit down and "have a sake". The default course does not get changed to appease it.
- If you disagree about a feature, the conclusion is likely to be 'then don't eat that'.

## Why the head chef's taste decides the menu

- When a dish is included, "it's usually based on our distilled tastes and preferences."
- He cites his own experience: a decade working on Rails and "well in the excess of ten thousand hours" poured into it.
- He claims this doesn't make his tastes right for you, but "it certainly means that they're well formed."
- He rejects "my opinion is as valid as yours" as applied to the menu: "Your opinion is valid _for you_, but most certainly not for the menu I've designed in this restaurant."
- The weight of an opinion scales with time spent in the establishment. First-time patrons, and people far from the kitchen, carry less, especially when the opinion is loud and obnoxious.

## Vote with your feet

- Your standing power is to leave. "If most things on the menu disgust you, what on earth are you still doing at the table? The door is right over there."
- To shape the menu over the long term, you have to work your way up to it.
- He gives a concrete example: three more people were added to the Rails core group "this week" who did exactly that.
- They arrived in one of two ways: with a "what can I do to help?" directive, or with "here's a dish I've designed, have a taste and see if you like it."
- His claim: "Nobody ever got to have a meaningful impact on the direction of Rails by contributing no code and just showing up in a Github comment thread and yelling about the evils of CoffeeScript."

## Why people argue about defaults anyway

- His "pet theory" is that yelling feels like meaningful activism in the moment. People haven't followed day-to-day project work, but it's a chance to "paint some signs and get on the barricades."
- He mocks the shifting target: "Turbolinks? Or was it CoffeeScript we're protesting this week? Or Bundler?"
- He treats this as futile behavior.

## Evaluating a framework by the bigger picture

- His test for a prospective user is two questions: "Do I generally like what's being served in this establishment?" and "Can I express my differences as exceptions or substitutions to the few things I don't care for?"
- If both answers are yes, give the maintainers "the benefit of assuming that they are working on what they truly think is the best way to go."
- You don't have to like everything. You can still drop the idea that a disliked default is "a conspiracy to make the world love unagi as a sick joke."
