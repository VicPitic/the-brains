## From fidget spinners to a viral TikTok leggings brand: Oliver's path, 14 to 18

- At 14, Oliver saw fidget spinners take over his school and borrowed $500 from his grandpa as seed money. He bought a 3D printer, a rubber mallet and skateboard bearings.
- He printed frames after school and soaked the bearings in acetone to strip the grease, which gave longer spin times. He sold to classmates and built a commission-based team of friends who sold for him.
- When spinners got banned at school, he sold door-to-door to business owners (20 or 50 at a time). A party planner then had him set up booths at bar mitzvahs and sweet 16s where kids printed and customized their own spinners. That made over $2,500 a night until he saturated the in-person market.
- He moved to eBay, ran keyword research and internal ads, and started an Instagram page to push external traffic to his listings. He sold over $50,000 of spinners by age 14. As he puts it, "as quick as the fidget spinner trend came they left."

## The social media agency lesson: no attribution means no impact

- After spinners, Oliver started a social media agency called Star Social. His first clients were his barbers, who gave him free monthly haircuts in exchange for running their accounts, plus a room in the back of the shop.
- That led to referrals to the fish market, a clothing store and other local businesses. To deliver, he built a team: a photographer, a content strategist, a copywriter and a Philippines-based virtual assistant.
- Oliver's problem with pure social media management was attribution. There is no tracking of what the work actually does, so impact is impossible to prove.
- He concluded that paid ads (PPC) fix this because every event is measured. If he could drive real revenue for a store or brand owner, he "would be untouchable." That is why he set out to learn paid ads at 16.

## Failed experiments: dropshipping and the Etsy chargeback collapse

- To learn paid ads, Oliver ran eight or nine dropshipping stores (charging cables, gadgets, kitchen supplies). He found no success. He says it is "certainly not a scam," but it wasn't for him.
- He went back to eBay and Etsy selling phone cases and wallets with no inventory and no costs, and became obsessed with growth-hacking listings to the top page and using each platform's native ad tools. He hit his first $1,000, $3,000 and $5,000 days.
- Then chargebacks hit: 30 or 40 at once. His supplier said they were shipping, but they were lying. He refunded close to 1,000 customers and lost every dollar he had made.
- The lesson he draws is implicit: a business that depends on a supplier you do not control can wipe out your gains overnight.

## Turning a $400/day Blazer store into $3,000/day

- At 17, a friend (Jesse) asked Oliver to help his mom Andrea's fashion e-commerce business. Her wholesale and retail business was solid, but her website was broken English, had incomprehensible emails and was a mess. It still sold about one blazer a day, roughly $400 a day.
- Oliver says he knew he could turn it around. In the first month sales went from $400 a day to $3,000 a day. He says the changes were "super basic optimizations" plus a revamp, not anything exotic.
- When a reality TV star wore one of her blazers on TV, the next day did over $28,000 in sales.
- Andrea then let him go because she wanted a "professional agency," and told him she couldn't pay him more because "you're a kid." Her store manager earned more than he did. A year later she called to say she had been through countless agencies and was still using the flows he set up.
- Oliver says the skills from that job are a big part of why he is where he is today.

## Early TikTok influencer arbitrage at the "It's Me" app

- At 17, a mentor (a few years older, heading up growth at the "It's Me" app, a 3D-avatar chat app backed by $15 million from the Reddit co-founder) brought Oliver in to pioneer TikTok influencer collaborations.
- Oliver explains why early TikTok was ripe for advertisers:
  - Influencers had never monetized on TikTok, so brand deals cost "pennies on the dollar."
  - Viewers had not seen ads on the platform, so they were more impressionable and more likely to convert. Today they scroll past ads, so there is more friction.
- He built a team of five Philippines-based virtual assistants. Two did list building: scraping TikTok for US-based creators with 50,000 to 500,000 followers.
- His logic on creator size: creators in that range don't charge big-name rates, so you pay for distribution rather than brand. Also, TikTok had democratized virality, so content mattered more than follower count.
- The app reached number four on the social networking chart, behind Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp.
- He was paid about $3,500 a month. The company, pressured to show astronomical growth before a fundraise, replaced him with an influencer agency costing $45,000 a month. "Money wasn't a problem for them... the only thing they cared about was showing growth."

## Scaling a viral TikTok leggings brand and deciding to start his own

- At 18 (end of senior year, accepted to the University of Michigan Ross School of Business), Oliver was introduced to Jake, a 17-year-old dropshipping whiz, and Nico, an investor who had backed him. Jake and Nico hired Oliver to run their whole influencer collaboration process and he came on as an operator, effectively CEO.
- Together they sold over $1 million of viral TikTok leggings in about a year. Jake and Nico treated him as a partner, and he and Jake would talk for five hours at a time.
- The brand sold for mid six figures.
- Oliver says that by then he had the experience and intuition to know "exactly what I need to do to go from zero to five million," and so started his own brand, Types Chocolate.
- The frame he uses for the whole story: each venture, successful or not, built a skill that fed the next. Those skills included product sourcing, marketplace growth, paid ads, influencer sourcing, building teams of virtual assistants, and attribution thinking.
