## Oliver's path from DTC brand to B2B SaaS (Bustem)

- Oliver started Taps Chocolate, scaled it from $30,000 of starting capital to an eight-figure-a-year business, and exited about two years before this conversation.
- He deliberately avoided another ecom brand. He was burnt out on "shilling" products and wanted something more product-focused and value-driven, where he could still use his strengths.
- He chose ecom SaaS because he was familiar with it (in ecom since age 14), knew the operators, and had a distribution edge. He compares the move to Peter Rahal building David after RXBAR: "that's compounding."
- He picked software over a course ("that would ruin my reputation") or an agency ("I don't know a single agency guy that likes what he does"). Software is infinitely scalable and carries the highest valuations.
- He built an audience of about 50,000 ecom entrepreneurs by building Taps in public. When he tweeted about an agency or software vendor, they got about 15 DMs overnight.
- Through affiliate partnerships with the vendors he used, his UTM links drove over $2 million in tracked deal flow. His conclusion: stop being a "street promoter" for other people's offers and build his own offer on his own distribution.

## What Bustem sells and how it prices

- The product scans the internet for counterfeits, copycats, scammers and content thieves ripping off brands. Enforcement agents, mostly in South Africa, handle takedowns.
- Takedowns go through DMCA, cease and desists, reports to domains, registrars, hosting providers and payment processors, and payment blocks, avoiding court that "would take years and hundreds of thousands of dollars."
- He estimates brands bleed 5 to 15% of revenue to bad actors. He buckets it all together: counterfeits, competitors stealing content or ads and inflating CPMs, fake listings, duplicated sites, ads on branded keywords.
- Two pricing models:
  - Performance: $300/month platform fee plus $50 to $200 per successful takedown, billed only after the takedown lands.
  - Unlimited retainer: $2,500 to $5,000/month.
- The performance tier works as a natural upsell. Clients who see many bad actors do the math and ask to move to unlimited. One brand, Kill Crew, racked up 30 to 40 kills in nine days, then signed a 12-month contract at $3K/month about 10 days in.
- His analogy: "$20 a chicken finger or $30 for the whole buffet."
- Longer term, he wants to automate identification and enforcement and drop monitoring toward free as a lead magnet, then charge only per successful removal, with unlimited retainers for high-volume brands. He admits the tech is hard because it requires scrapers for platforms that don't want to be scraped.
- Small brands outnumber big ones roughly 10 to 1 at the door, so he splits focus between both.

## Making ROI the center of the product and sales

- Founders get angry about copycats ("[ __ ] that guy"), but he says the emotion gets them on a call and the job is then to tie it to the P&L: "if you're not close to the P&L, you're [ __ ]."
- He frames everything as dollars: revenue lost per bad actor, revenue "recovered" per takedown. He admits the recovery number is a stretch, since takedown doesn't literally return the money.
- He wants to guarantee a 5x ROI, saying it improves retention and customer satisfaction.
- Credibility tactic: show the formula. Inputs are traffic times conversion rate times AOV, with sliders the brand can adjust, because "anybody could say that you're getting a 10x ROI."
- Put the dollar figure everywhere in the product:
  - Each bad-actor review card shows the money lost, with approve or deny buttons.
  - A running total across all cards sits at the top.
  - Per-client Slack channels get daily automations with takedown counts and estimated revenue.
  - A Slack alert fires at 15 bad actors pending review, for example "30 bad actors... potentially $30,000 a month."
  - Weekly and monthly reports lead with the dollar amount.
- He says ROI alone isn't enough; the product also has to be hard to replace. His competition is a VA with no technology, or a lawyer who bills what Bustem charges for a half-hour call.
- On putting prices on a website, he reported split testing it: "conversion rate took a massive dump when I put the pricing on the website."

## Distribution and growth tactics for Bustem

- His main weapon is the audience from Taps. The launch tweet for Bustem got over 250,000 impressions and over 400 signups on day one.
- He posts daily and books three to five calls off his X account.
- He runs cold email framed around specific, hard-to-ignore proof:
  - "We found over 200 copycats and counterfeits that are ripping you off. Do you want to see the list?"
  - "I almost got scammed by a product pretending to be you guys... do you want to see the link?"
  - His reasoning: no one replies "no, I don't want to see the link."
- Referrals and word of mouth are the largest driver of clients; in SaaS, he argues, you're rewarded for building a good product.
- He notes distribution lessons from Taps still apply: understanding the Twitter algorithm, getting attention, and being scrappy. But the audience and playbooks are different, so in many ways he's "starting over."
- A SEO mistake: he bought bust.co to save about 5K, later bought bust.com, and a migration issue between the two domains means bust.com doesn't show up when searching "bust" on Google. He's working with an SEO agency and running Google ads on branded terms to catch warm intent.

## DTC versus B2B SaaS: how the games differ

- DTC marketing is one-to-many and aimed at a huge audience. B2B is "hand-to-hand combat," with every deal involving decision makers, buy-in and calls where prospects "fall off a cliff."
- B2B inputs map to outputs more directly. He can calculate emails sent, reply rate, booked calls and close rate, so the outcome feels predictable and rewarding. Ecom felt less in his control, with big months coming from decisions made months earlier, platform timing (he was early on organic TikTok), or the right tailwind.
- Consumer, to him, is "all marketing," almost a content agency. He says it's about acquiring customers with a CAC lower than LTV. SaaS rewards focusing on product and retention, and he thinks focusing on product is better long term.
- In SaaS you play two games at once: the product game and the marketing and distribution game. In ecom, the product is fixed and you only market it, because iteration is slow (sell out inventory, change the formula, three-month lead time, two months shipping).
- SaaS is slow for a non-technical founder. He has a one-person dev shop, is hiring a second full-time engineer, and says everything "takes three or four times longer" than estimated, leaving him feeling two or three months behind the product in his head.
- He sees continuous iteration as an upside: churn, onboarding and retention are levers to pull, so improving the product can drive growth without acquiring more customers.

## Retention and upgrades via product design

- The review-card UI makes the value of each takedown visible, which pushes customers up the pricing ladder from per-kill to unlimited.
- Customers on the performance model with no minimum rarely pause, since $300/month is cheap "insurance" for 24/7 monitoring, daily scans and manual enforcement sweeps.
- He currently runs a hybrid of software and agency, with human enforcement agents, and plans to phase them out as automation improves.
