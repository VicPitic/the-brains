## Starting a chocolate-format brand: invent the product's format, not the formula

- Tabs Chocolate sells "sex chocolate": three chocolate squares per box, each with a clean diagonal break so it splits into two triangles, one for him and one for his partner.
- Oliver says he did not reinvent the wheel or create a "crazy recipe". He took the most popular and effective natural supplements (normally pills and powders) and put them in a new format.
- The format is the differentiation. Pills and powders feel pharmaceutical and taboo, so competitors like Blue Chew and BYD advertise discreet packaging so "your girl won't even know".
- Tabs does the opposite: "embrace it with your partner, enjoy it with your partner, break it in half". Oliver says this also plays well on camera because the product explains itself visually.
- He and his partner picked the idea after a partner sent him a TikTok of a sex-chocolate product with about 8 million views and 2 million likes. The brand behind it had weak product and packaging, no social presence, no digital footprint and no ads. His reasoning was that a better-executed version could blow up.
- He designed the product to go viral on TikTok, and calls this "easy mode" compared with a random product.
- Results he cites for year one: over 250 million views across social, about $2.5 million in revenue, roughly 45% net profit margin, all organic. A recent month hit $500K in revenue, a record.

## Building the supply chain on a tiny budget

- The first year went to building the product: supply chain, formulation, branding and packaging, before launching about a year before this interview.
- Tabs started with $30,000 total and was fully bootstrapped.
- Manufacturers would not talk to them. Minimum order quantities were typically 100,000 to 300,000 units.
- Oliver cold-called hundreds of companies until one agreed. That co-manufacturer works in micro runs, mostly by hand, and takes about three months per production run. Tabs later outgrew him.
- He lists the moving pieces: sourcing four or five supplements from separate manufacturers, the chocolate maker, the mold manufacturer, the boxes, and chrome polymer packaging that traps heat so the chocolate does not melt in transit.

## The TikTok organic system: one creator per brand page, volume as the strategy

- His first approach was mass influencer outreach, and it was a headache at scale:
  - About 90% never respond.
  - Half of those who do charge exorbitant rates.
  - Of those who agree and get product, many ghost and keep it.
  - Outreach through VAs firing DMs hit Instagram action blocks, so he set up multiple accounts. He also used cold emails, texts and TikTok Creator Marketplace.
- His alternative was to hire creators to run brand pages, the way businesses hire a creator. He started with one creator on one page, then kept adding.
- Today he has 30 to 40 creators on retainer, posting one to three TikToks a day each.
- His reasoning on virality: TikTok's algorithm is a black box. He cannot predict which video will hit, but "if we put up 50 videos in a day, one will hit". Instead of finding the needle in the haystack, "grab the whole haystack".
- One creator per account, scaled horizontally across "subsidiary accounts". TikTok analyzes a page's creator style and content ethos to decide which audience to push to. Stacking several creators on one page confuses the algorithm ("too many cooks in the kitchen").
- Lesson: you do not need followers to hit the For You page; good content from a small account can still blow up.

## Affiliate Discord: 1,800 teens reposting viral content

- Tabs runs a Discord with about 1,800 members, roughly 16 to 20 years old. Each gets a custom promo code that tracks the sales they drive.
- Each member makes their own subsidiary Instagram account and reposts Tabs' most viral videos to Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and Facebook Reels. Oliver says this collective has driven close to $500K.
- Oliver describes an affiliate as a "mercenary": an independent person with no relationship to the brand who captures a percentage of each sale they generate.
- Retention and growth tactics:
  - Invite competitions and daily bonuses.
  - Daily wheel spins where his VA enters names and the winner gets about $10, spun several times a day.
  - A community feel, including a gym chat where members post their physiques.
- He cites one 13-year-old in the Discord who made roughly $10-15K reposting videos, about 2 minutes a day.
- He is scaling the group and sees affiliates as a major lever.

## Paid ads limits and next growth levers for a restricted category

- Because Tabs sits in the sex and supplement space, Facebook and TikTok restrict it. TikTok shadow-bans or deletes brand accounts, so he keeps coming back with new pages and new creators.
- He says this organic model probably cannot take Tabs to eight or nine figures. Brands without his restrictions, such as Mini Katana, can compound brand accounts into tens or hundreds of millions of views a month.
- He tried Trunk-style networks (unclear in the transcript) without success, and says he may not have given it a real shot.
- He says Tabs must crack paid media acquisition to move from a couple of million to tens of millions. Options he is exploring: creative advertising, landing pages, advertorials and cloaking.
- He notes comparisons with venture-backed brands are unfair: they can write off spend as brand awareness, while he needs a return on every dollar.
- Planned expansion:
  - Amazon, wholesale and retail (currently only small shops, sex shops and similar).
  - International, since over 40% of incoming traffic is international but they cannot sell there. Each country has its own supplement labeling and nutrition-facts rules.
- Ideas offered to him and one he is acting on: a lead-magnet product or ebook that can be advertised compliantly, such as a date idea book similar to The Adventure Challenge. He is building an ebook (a how-to guide) for email capture.
- He does not want expansion too fast: year one was a roller coaster.

## The entrepreneur ladder: dropshipping, private label, proprietary product

- Oliver argues drop shippers are underappreciated and that dropshipping is arguably harder than running a brand. A brand banks on LTV and repeat customers and can be unprofitable on the front end. A drop shipper has to be profitable on the first purchase.
- His three-step ladder:
  - Dropshipping: no barriers to entry, costs $0, teaches skills.
  - Private label: find a product that works and put your logo on it (the leggings brand was his example).
  - A proprietary product: fewer competitors, higher barriers, a moat.
- He does not look down on the earlier rungs and sees them as fundamental steps.
- He criticizes brand builders who raise money without experience, burn cash, know nothing about performance marketing and outsource marketing, which he calls the hardest part of ecommerce. His line: "I'm a marketing company, I'm a media company."

## Career path: from fidget spinners to Tabs

- At 14 he got a $500 loan from his grandfather, bought a 3D printer, a rubber mallet and skateboard bearings, and made fidget spinners in his basement. He sold to classmates, then recruited friends as sales reps, then went door to door to local shops, then moved to eBay and Etsy.
- He also grew a fidget-spinner Instagram account from zero to 10,000 followers using giveaways, hashtags, bots and follow/unfollow engagement tactics.
- When the trend died he tried dropshipping and failed, then started a social media management agency around age 15. His first client was his barber, who paid with a free haircut a month.
- He found Instagram management hard to attribute, so he moved into paid ads, where every dollar is measurable.
- In his senior year of high school he ran influencer collaborations for an app, "It's Me", spending $1,000 to $3,000 a day on micro influencers new to brand deals. The app reached number four in the social networking charts.
- He then ran TikTok and influencer marketing for a leggings brand that sold about $700K to $1M in a year. He says these were foundational even though they were not his own companies.
- Before Tabs he considered selling hot cocoa bombs, then switched to the sex-chocolate idea.

## Setbacks, mindset and where he points his energy

- During the first summer, revenue fell about 60%. A co-founder disappeared for roughly three months, a summer intern tried to extort him for equity, and a roommate scammed him out of money. He was broke, moved back home with his parents and says his self-esteem collapsed.
- Hiring lesson: bring in specialists with defined roles and autonomy who know more than you in their domain, who should be teaching you, not generalists.
- Mindset points he states:
  - Success takes a long time, and Tabs would not exist without everything he did from age 14.
  - Early wins and losses are building blocks.
  - He had tied his identity to Tabs, then zoomed out. He loves the game and Tabs is a vehicle for it, but he does not plan to run it for 5-10 years.
- He runs a small social media agency on the side (six to eight brands at the time) applying the same systems: TikTok organic, Instagram, meme pages and Twitter memes.
- On Twitter, he uses his account as an unfiltered outlet and says it led him to investors, VCs, entrepreneurs and both of his roommates.
