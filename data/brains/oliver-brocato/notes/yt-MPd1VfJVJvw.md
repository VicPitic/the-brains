## Oliver's path into entrepreneurship and why he thinks ownership beats salary

- Oliver started at 14 as a high-school freshman. He saw the fidget spinner trend explode in class, borrowed $500 from his grandpa, bought a 3D printer, manufactured the frames, hammered in skateboard bearings, and sold to friends, then local stores, then neighboring towns, then online on eBay and Etsy.
- He calls that first online sale his first taste of internet money: "people that didn't know me had zero connection to me... and they're giving me their money."
- From 14 to 18 he ran many projects: a social media management company that became a marketing agency, and running e-commerce operations for a friend's mom's fashion brand, taking it from about $300/day to $2,000/day in sales.
- His motivation: he grew up around much wealthier families (a friend's dad was a hedge fund manager) and saw his own dad, "a brilliant guy," earn through a corporate job. He concluded "the only way you really get rich is having ownership and equity, not getting paid per hour or per salary."
- Money was tight in his household and tied to constant pressure about SATs and the "right" school. Making money became validity nobody could argue with. He decided to "stop playing other people's games" and create his own path.
- Confidence compounds through a positive reinforcement cycle: a small business gets small wins, which leads to the next business, which attracts people who want to work with you.

## The rule against hustle porn: focus on the first customer, not the lifestyle

- Oliver says the dream-and-consume trap is common. His point: talking about or watching content on building a business can feel like progress without producing any.
- He says to think "how do I get my first customer," not about the Lamborghini, the watch or the travel.
- The sequence he describes: get the first five customers, then the first 50, then the first 500, and let momentum "do the work for you."
- He says only people who actually do things build real confidence, while most people "only consume and consume."
- Surviving the early days takes an intrinsic drive or a "chip on your shoulder." He says "99% of people wash out" because early entrepreneurship is "eating [expletive] constantly."
- He says not to believe 99% of what you see online. His example: a Miami guy flexing a G-Wagon and a watch who, per a friend, couldn't use his apartment building's elevator because he had scammed other residents.

## Using TikTok early: influencer deals and the booty-lifting leggings

- At 17 he was brought on by a friend (Alma man) to run influencer collaborations for an app on TikTok, when TikTok was still new.
- Why it worked: influencers had never monetized on the platform, so deals came "for pennies on the dollar," and users weren't yet bombarded with ads because other companies hadn't arrived.
- Result: a team of six took the app to number four on the social networking charts, right behind Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp.
- At 18 he teamed with a 17-year-old to push the viral TikTok booty-lifting leggings. They didn't create the product but were among the first to put it on TikTok.
- Path: started with dropshipping, then ordered inventory into the US, set up a 3PL, and private-labeled with their logo on the leggings.
- Outcome: zero to seven figures in about seven months.

## How Tabs was built: start with something already viral

- Tabs is a sexually enhancing chocolate: popular natural supplements that usually come as pills or powder, put into a chocolate.
- His approach was the reverse of the usual order. Instead of making a product and figuring out marketing, he took something already viral with an "it factor" and built a business around a product that "lended itself well to TikTok." He says he created it for TikTok.
- Results he states: zero to eight figures, about $11 million in revenue this year, scaled from zero to $11M a year in two years.
- Tabs is being acquired. It cleared due diligence and the buyers sent the purchase agreement; he says it could still fall through.

## Learning by doing, pattern recognition, and the long game

- He learns by experience, "trial and error and failing," not courses. Success is "the accumulation of tens of thousands of micro experiences" that build intuition through pattern recognition. He says there is no finish line, only the next level.
- Shiny object syndrome is "one of the biggest reasons" beginners fail online. His example: a friend who went crypto, then NFTs, then day trading, then dropshipping, then a TikTok content agency, switching every four months.
- He cites the "trough of sorrow" graph: initial honeymoon spike, then a drop as novelty wears off, then "false wiggles of hope," then eventually the promised land. He says his own sales chart "follows the trough of sorrow almost exactly."
- His rule: there are many ways to make money, so pick one, stick to it and see it through.
- On timing, he cites a tweet from the founder who sold Pura Vida for $120M: "it takes 10 years to do anything good." Skill compounds, and a few percent improvement every six months is a 3 to 10x difference over time.
- On juggling projects, he agrees multiple projects only work if they have synergies and feed each other.
- There's no single route to success: some people do 5am cold showers and journaling, while a "fat guy" who doesn't may hit the jackpot with a software business.

## Internet money, anonymous operators, and the founder's edge

- He says skepticism of internet money is understandable because gurus flashing lifestyle and selling courses give it a bad rap.
- The biggest operators of e-commerce brands are often anonymous and look like normal people. Examples he says he knows personally: a friend selling slime in cups on track for over $20M, a friend selling mini katanas organically on TikTok on track for over $15M this year, and a 14-year-old making real money.
- Common thread among these people: they didn't fit the mainstream path, were "a little bit different," had hunger, and could take risk. "You kind of have to be a little bit crazy" to sell swords or slime online.
- His own edge, in his words: he's a marketer fascinated by consumer psychology. He looks at everyday businesses (an ice cream shop's table turnover, staffing) and thinks about optimization.
- Second edge: obsession. He thinks about his business 24/7, which he says can be a detriment but is his "competitive advantage," combined with being a quick learner who iterates constantly.

## What he does next: pick the vehicle carefully, build a personal brand

- Current side projects: Study Buddy, a Chrome extension that runs online quiz or homework questions through AI and shows the answer plus an explanation. He launched it a few months ago and grew it from zero to $15,000 MRR despite summer, with no ads running. He plans to double down at the start of the school year.
- Personal brand: he is investing in content on Twitter, Instagram and short-form, with an editor, a content strategist and possibly a full-time camera person. He says he wants to be a business person first, so content comes from the business: "you don't want to create content, you want to be the content." He criticizes business YouTubers who have to brainstorm video ideas on a whiteboard.
- Why a personal brand: it gives "infinite reach," access to mentors and peers he could never meet otherwise, and luck "increases a hundredfold." Example: a three-hour lunch with an Israeli entrepreneur who sold a cybersecurity company to Microsoft for $200M. He says his brand makes him more money than his businesses.
- Next big venture: he wants to build a real team and infrastructure, maybe raise VC money, and "swing for the fences."
- Key lesson from Tabs: "the vehicle or the business that you choose is actually more important than your ability as an entrepreneur." An amazing entrepreneur with a bad product only goes so far, while an average one with the right product gets a rocket ship.
- He wants something he could do for 5 to 15 years or a lifetime, because that is what separates unicorns from companies worth millions or tens of millions.
- With his basic needs covered, he says he can "play the long game" and think in decades, which is hard when worried about the next lunch.
