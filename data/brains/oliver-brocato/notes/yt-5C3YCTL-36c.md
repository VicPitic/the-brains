## Selling one product: Tabs Chocolate and the $11M/year model

- Oliver sells "sex chocolate" online (Tabs Chocolate). It is an edible for sex that turns the most popular and effective natural supplements, which normally come as a pill or powder, into a high-end, luxurious experience.
- He expects about $11M in revenue for the year, with no outside investment. He reinvests every dollar into growth.
- He started it as a college freshman during a two-month winter break, and was a millionaire two years later at age 21.
- In the first year alone, Tabs was out of stock for more months than it had stock and still did about $4M.
- In the first three weeks he sold out of all inventory. In the first month he moved to pre-order and did over $280,000 in revenue at roughly 50% net margins.

## How he picked the product: spotting a viral loophole

- A friend sent him a TikTok of a teenage girl reviewing a sex chocolate. It had 8 million views and 2 million likes, and the product had no website, Instagram or TikTok page.
- He took that as proof the concept could go viral, and decided his prior experience in this space could turn it into a multi-million dollar brand.
- Criteria he used: pick a product that "intrinsically goes viral". Tabs is novel, so people who have never seen it react.
- Key principle: virality runs on emotional arousal. Make the viewer feel a super intense emotion (the type doesn't matter). That makes people watch to the end and share with friends.
- He then spent a year building it "the right way", agonizing over every detail of the customer experience.

## Startup costs and finding a manufacturer

- He started Tabs with $30,000, drained from his bank account, money earned from earlier ventures from when he was younger.
- He thinks almost any e-commerce brand can launch an MVP for about $10K. The biggest cost is R&D, then developing the first sample.
- Many manufacturers won't give a tiny brand the time of day. The goal is a small manufacturer willing to take you under its wing, which can get you under $10K.
- How he found one: he typed "chocolate contract manufacturer" into Google and went through the first 20 to 25 pages of results. He built a spreadsheet of each company's name, website and contact info, then phoned and emailed them.
- Supply chain: he designed the raw box files himself in Canva and Figma, then had a professional designer clean them up and get 3D renders made. The boxes are made in China. The chocolate, and everything that touches it, is made in the US, which he says is "super super important" to him.
- Each supplement has its own independent manufacturer because he sources the best of the best. Each chocolate also has its own wrapper manufacturer, and the chocolate is stamped with the logo and has a clean break line so it can be split.
- It took a year from idea to reality. He admits there were weeks he wasn't working on it because he was waiting on someone.

## Biggest operational mistake: inventory management

- Inventory forecasting is his perpetual problem. After selling out, he shipped the next order of about 60,000 to 70,000 boxes by boat instead of air because it's much cheaper, but it takes four times as long.
- The container was delayed. He moved to pre-orders, sales declined to almost zero, and he calls it a disaster.
- His fix is to order as much inventory as he can, which is why he reinvests every dollar into boxes and chocolate.

## Why a brand beats dropshipping

- He argues dropshipping "sucks" for most people. You sell a trendy product in a saturated market with no competitive advantage, so there's no reason for a buyer to choose you over Amazon or 150 other sellers. In his view the people making money from it are those selling courses.
- The power of a brand: you drop a product and it sells out in 30 seconds. You build a community and an identity where customers "live and breathe" your product.
- A cult-like following gives you evergreen customers who come back for new drops and new SKUs, so you can keep making money from the same cohort.
- Brand equity matters for the exit. He says real money is made on the exit, not on cash flow or yearly sales. Dropshipping has no equity, because you keep shutting down and opening new shops every couple of months.
- Takeaway: people thinking long term and building brands always win.

## The marketing engine: a UGC content machine

- Most of his business comes from short-form social: TikTok, Instagram, YouTube and any other short-form platform. He says he has pushed out hundreds of millions of views on TikTok.
- He pays 60+ creators, and thousands of people repost the content across a web of accounts. Hundreds of videos go out every day, reaching millions of people and driving tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars in sales a day.
- Videos are made to look as native as possible. "Consumers are smart. If they think it's an ad, they're not going to watch it." Creators follow whatever is trending: the popping sound, the current movement or format. They also push some validated concepts that perform hard.
- It is mostly organic, with little paid advertising. He does "double down" by taking his best-performing creatives and paying meme pages with tens of millions of followers to post them.
- Other channels: a little Google Ads, plus email and text message marketing, which he calls bottom of the funnel.
- He credits this organic system, which he says he pioneered, for the $11M+ year.

## Advice for starting a brand

- "I'm not smarter than you. I don't have a higher IQ." There is no gatekept secret sauce: go make it happen and never look back.
- Progress compounds daily, and in 3, 5 or 10 years people won't recognize how far you've come.
