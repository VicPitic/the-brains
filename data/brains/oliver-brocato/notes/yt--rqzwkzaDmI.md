## Marketing a product built for virality (TABS Chocolate)

- Oliver describes TABS as an edible for sex: a high-end luxury chocolate, three squares per box, each square breaks in half to share with a partner. It packages popular natural supplements (usually pills or powders) into a less taboo, less pharmaceutical experience.
- He says he did not pick it out of love for the category: "it was a vehicle to go viral."
- His skill set was viral marketing on TikTok, so he built a product that could go viral by itself instead of marketing an existing one.
- Prior experience: at 18 he headed growth and partnerships for an app, took it to number four on the social networking charts behind Facebook and Instagram with a team of six, and was one of the people behind the viral TikTok Ripple woven booty-lifting leggings (no ownership in that company).
- Why it spreads: the product is visual and understandable in a 3-second clip or sound bite. A creator told him "even a monkey can make Tabs go viral."
- Influencers often get more views on sponsored TABS posts than on their own organic content. Oliver treats this as proof that product-first design for virality works.
- He argues short-form gives reach that would normally cost millions in paid advertising, and that TikTok has democratized virality: follower count matters little ("a thousand followers or a million").

## Why he moved from micro influencers to dedicated content creators

- Starting plan: many micro influencers, because they give the best value per dollar. Bigger names cost more for brand name, managers and agencies charging 3x. The idea was to find undervalued creators, pay little, send free product.
- What failed: most influencers ghost; of those who reply, half charge ridiculous rates; some take free product and never post. Scaling was a logistics nightmare.
- He built a team of 8 scraping influencers, sent mass emails across 12 domains, and automated everything with Zapier (including an email triggered on chocolate delivery). He still could not crack it.
- Result: sales were a roller coaster, a good day after an influencer posted then back to zero. "That's not how you build a sustainable brand."
- Fix: hire one content creator to run a brand page called "tabs chocolate," be the face of it, and post 1-3 videos every day. In their first month the first creator generated about $70-80K in sales.
- Scaling: rather than adding creators to one page, he expanded horizontally, giving each creator an independent page. Hundreds of pages exist; many are inactive or dead because creators are hired and fired and accounts get shadowbanned, so new accounts are constantly created.
- He compares it to the Andrew Tate model: many people posting every day. He can't predict what hits, but with enough consistent volume from different people and pages, things go viral and drive revenue. He calls it a black box.

## Creator compensation: retainer plus upside

- Pure affiliate sounds ideal but is hard to get people to accept at the start. Nobody wants to work for free, especially before a creator's content starts popping, which can take a couple of months.
- His model evolved: first creators on a retainer with renegotiation, then a hybrid. The hybrid is a very small retainer, "enough where they're happy if nothing happens", plus a percentage of all the upside.
- Result: creators making tens of thousands of dollars a month while driving hundreds of thousands a month for him.

## Diversify channels for longevity

- Oliver treats TikTok dependence as a real risk: pages get banned often and US government bans and hearings were in the news. His question: if TikTok goes away, does the business die?
- His answer: be "an octopus" with tentacles on 12 channels. Each channel growing incrementally (he cites 5%, 10%, 20%) compounds into a multi-million dollar brand.
- Use the 80/20 rule to find what drives most revenue and focus there, but build a durable company by doing everything well, not one thing.
- His current mix: email and text message ("crushing" them), a Google ads media buyer, Facebook ads he is starting to run himself, plus Instagram, Twitter and other creator work.

## Building a moat: lessons from the leggings business

- The leggings brand was private label: they stuck their logo on someone else's product, ordered inventory and sold online. When it blew up, around 300 other people sold the same pair, the market saturated, and the business died as soon as it took off.
- Lesson: whatever he built next had to have high barriers to entry, be an amazing product that can't be ripped off, "own the industry, own the category," and be viral. He reverse-engineered the business from there.
- On execution, he says it is not genius: call the first 300 results when searching for chocolate co-manufacturers, find a box manufacturer on Alibaba. "It's not about IQ. Do you want it, are you hungry, and do you have enough experience and intuition."

## Packaging, product and repeat purchase

- Packaging drives the first purchase. Customers can't touch or taste the chocolate online, so they judge by looks, like a first impression of a person.
- His test: put your product on a shelf against competitors; do eyes gravitate to it, or does it blend in with the herd?
- The product itself drives repeat purchase. If it isn't good, customers won't reorder just because the box was nice.
- A great product creates a flywheel: repeat orders, customers telling friends, friends talking about it.

## Personal brand as leverage

- Oliver says he is a business guy first and a personal brand second. He compares content to sawdust: his business building is the saw, so he always has things to tweet and post about. He doesn't need to brainstorm ideas.
- Hiring: almost every hire on his team of 11+ people came from Twitter or a connection made through Twitter.
- Networking leverage: meeting that many people would take decades of networking events and flying around; content does it daily ("sitting on the toilet").
- He has no fixed goal for the personal brand, just the belief it is an asset that will likely be one of his most valuable and a lifelong one. Kids also message him that his content changed their perspective on college and business.

## Advice on building online

- Do what you love instead of following conventional wisdom from parents and school. He says he knows "14-year-old degenerates" pulling half a million dollars a month online.
- He sees no barriers to entry: free education on YouTube, Twitter and social media if you follow the right people. Options he lists: create content, start a YouTube channel or TikTok page, build a brand or software.
- Expect slow results (maybe 5-10 years). If you stick with it and don't give up, "it's inevitable" you succeed.
- Context he gives on himself: he dropped out of the University of Michigan (Ross) last year to work on the business full time, and has been building brands and selling online since age 14.
