## Why Oliver moved from e-commerce into SaaS

- Oliver sold Tabs (his chocolate e-commerce brand, scaled to $1M a month at age 21) and moved to software because he found it removed e-commerce's operational pain points.
- Inventory planning: he relied on viral traffic with month-over-month swings that were impossible to predict. In Tabs' first year the brand was out of stock and on pre-order for more time than it had inventory.
- Logistics: overseas and domestic manufacturers added moving parts, and "chocolate melts" over the summer.
- Iteration speed: improving a physical product means sample rounds with manufacturers, roughly a month each. A V2 or V3 can't ship until V1 inventory sells out, so a product change can take six months to a year.
- Software offers a shorter feedback loop, zero cost of goods, huge margins and "the only limit is your imagination."
- He says every part of a software business is measurable and can be tuned like a dial (onboarding, paywall, customer experience, accuracy, efficiency). Improving 3%, then 5%, then 8% ties directly to the P&L through lower churn, better retention and more revenue.
- He admits "the grass is always greener" and that each model trades one set of problems for another.

## How Oliver picks what to build

- He leads as a marketer: "what has potential and opportunity to be pushed on TikTok or short form or paid ads," because that is his core competency and edge. He wouldn't start a construction company "because what the [ __ ] do I know about that."
- He lets others do the validation. If a mediocre product does $40,000 a month from a few YouTube videos, that is a signal that he can come in and make a dent. Seeing a competitor with your idea is "more signal," not a reason to quit.
- He frames innovation as micro-improvements: a buggy with a slightly better wheel, then a steam engine, and so on. Competition also creates an efficient market (a competitor can undercut on price and force others down).
- He doesn't believe in one-to-one copying. He takes inspiration piecemeal: one brand's website, another's email funnels, another's product packaging, and he studies the best constantly.
- Why pure copycats lose: the visible ad or page isn't what makes the money. The back end does: pricing split tests, email and SMS flows, and the ascension from subscriber to high-ticket customer. You can run the same ad as a competitor and lose money on it while they profit. He also warns that what a brand does today (for example Gymshark's flagship pop-up) isn't what got it there.
- His rule for a worrying competitor: a copycat that also does something a little better.

## Winning at paid ads: the machine behind the ad account

- Oliver says he is not the best media buyer. His view is that winning ads is mostly about what sits behind the account, not only the media buying or creative.
- He cites a friend's mushroom gummy brand (name withheld). It has the same CAC as every competitor, but a fully optimized landing page and a layered upsell journey (cart upsells, a checkout popup offering two more bags for the price of one, more upsells in checkout) turn a $40 customer into a $100 customer. A higher LTV lets them afford a higher CAC than competitors.
- The product has to be really good too. If visitors churn after a week or a month, "you're kind of cooked from the start," regardless of ad quality.
- His LTV levers on his own SaaS:
  - Price testing: monthly billing amounts, trial offers, a free trial billed after a week or three days, and a $0.99 trial period then rebilling, which he found worked better than a free trial.
  - Paywall placement: paywall right away on the landing page versus after the user has used the product a bit.
  - Offer testing: "buy this and get a mystery gift," or "get this and also access to that." He calls these price tests that go beyond the dollar amount.
- He notes these elements all need to be dialed together: website, email and SMS, client ascension and ads.

## Creative testing and which metrics he watches

- He ran Facebook ads himself, starting with help from Alex, a 17-year-old ecom and affiliate marketer who now does roughly $4M a month on his own stuff. Oliver later outsourced to a friend who runs a media buying agency, then switched to paying for a weekly audit call (about $250 for the call, roughly $1,000 a month, versus about $3,000 a month for the agency) to learn the skill himself.
- Lessons from those operators:
  - Scaling from six to seven figures a month "all comes down to creative testing and volume testing," plus split tests and A/B tests to find the winning angle.
  - Once there is a winning angle, remake it in variations: 10 different creators (a Black creator, a white creator, an older, a younger, a gay creator) remaking the same winning creative, or the same creative with different hooks.
  - Run each batch as a test, take the conclusions, then run a new batch and keep refining.
  - Pump spend into winning creatives; on Meta they can last a long time.
  - Give Meta time to spend and collect data, and don't touch the account constantly (his advisor recommended roughly once a week).
- Metrics: the only ones he really cares about are CAC and LTV. Hook rate (share who watch the first three seconds) and hold rate (how far they watch through) are correlated with performance, but he has had a creative with his best-ever hook and hold rates and a worse CAC than another creative. He doesn't think getting deep into the weeds is productive; if CAC and LTV balance, "you got a license to print money."
- On attribution, he relies on intuition trained by pattern recognition and looks at many data sources rather than any single tool or platform. At Tabs he could check sales at 10 a.m. and predict the day. He doesn't tell people to ignore data, and he doesn't rely on Hyros.
- He ran an incrementality-style test on one micro-SaaS: he scaled Facebook spend from $20k to $25k, $50k, $70k and $90k a month while Facebook still reported a steady CAC, yet overall revenue barely grew. When he later cut spend aggressively, MRR and revenue stayed the same. His takeaway: a lot of ad spend can be wasted, and platforms (whose incentive is for you to spend more) can't be fully trusted. Play "if I do this, what happens" games with spend.
- He hit about $180K MRR on one B2C micro-SaaS running ads (per Jaen's recollection of what Oliver said; Oliver didn't state the figure himself in this talk).

## Multi-creator organic strategy and channel testing

- Oliver describes himself as the pioneer of the multi-creator strategy. The TikTok algorithm is "a bit of a lottery," so you want as many high-quality lottery tickets as possible.
- Setup: bring on many creators, each posting on their own independent account created from scratch by the creator, not a brand login and not tied to one device or email. Platforms see separate entities, each with its own chance to go viral.
- Results: at Tabs the creator program produced mid-six figures a month at its highest points. Content must be high quality ("not farmed from a YouTube video").
- It did not work for one of his consumer SaaS products. He used the same creators and principles; he tested seven or eight creators and none produced a viral video, a winner or a profitable result.
- His channel tests for his first micro-SaaS:
  - Organic multi-account creators: didn't work.
  - Instagram theme pages (which made six figures a month for his e-commerce brands): didn't work.
  - Google ads: not profitable.
  - TikTok ads: hit very hard.
- His lesson: the product or vehicle often determines which channel works, so "test a [ __ ] ton, fail a [ __ ] ton, and quickly" and move on. When you see a sliver of potential, "100x" it rather than just doubling down. Expect that 99% of what you try won't work.

## Bustm: why he chose e-commerce tooling and the go-to-market plan

- Oliver's new B2B SaaS, Bustm, is "your digital watchdog" for e-commerce merchants. It scans social networks, marketplaces, Shopify sites and the wider web for copycats, infringers and counterfeits using a brand's IP (product images, whole landing pages, products, ads), then helps take them down.
- Takedown tools: one-click cease and desist letters (including physical mail), one-click DMCA notices, and automatic detection of the offender's providers and enablers (payment processor, email software, inventory software) so notices go to each. It also sends a letter to the owner's mom, to "name and shame."
- Why lawyers don't solve it: a lawsuit is expensive and slow, for "a couple dollars out of a 17-year-old dropshipper in a different country."
- Why he chose this market: he has an unfair advantage in e-commerce.
  - He has been in e-commerce since age 14 and knows many people in the industry.
  - He has about 45,000 e-commerce entrepreneurs following him on Twitter/X.
  - He has sent millions of dollars of deal flow and thousands of leads to about 14 agencies and software companies, which proves he can sell through his personal brand.
  - He believes he could sign 100 brands "tomorrow" through his contacts.
- Why not a course or an agency: a course has no equity, is short term and would burn relationships. An agency is "a glorified job" where the only way to scale is more human capital, and clients blame the agency when anything goes wrong. He wanted software he could sell to e-commerce merchants that makes their lives better. He says he didn't want to "sell people [ __ ]".
- Go-to-market plan (pre-revenue at the time):
  - Go to every e-commerce event and conference, shake hands and take people to dinner. He'll fly to big merchants to meet face to face. He says there will be no "rocket science content blitz."
  - Push through his own channels and recruit other e-commerce Twitter influencers.
  - Invest in SEO now, as a long-term bet.
  - Cold email and cold calls, testing everything and doubling down on what works.
  - Super affiliates: about 15 well-networked agency owners and software owners (people with a book of 10 to 50 merchant clients). One example is a former head of acquisition at an e-commerce software company with a rolodex of 500 brands doing $25M+ a year, who could send intros. Oliver says paying such a person something like $5,000 a month for intros could be the fastest ROI, or offer them advisorships or equity.
  - He finds super affiliates by DMing people, getting on calls, asking connected friends and attending conferences ("nothing we do is rocket science").

## Website conversion and compounding optimization

- Oliver builds conversion-focused websites by studying e-commerce leaders instead of other B2B software sites. He names Javy Coffee (tens of millions a year, "probably one of the best optimized landing pages I've ever seen"), Carpe, Ora Nutrition and Seed as examples he takes notes from.
- He advises B2B founders to model their ads and websites on e-commerce players, whom he calls "the masters of conversion."
- He treats the website as never finished: pricing adjustments, banner changes, sticky cart and pricing card improvements, with a concerted effort each month to squeeze out small gains. His view is that 3% gains across every part of a business produce "a completely brand new business" in six months.
- He says things compound with time and he cites time as the most valuable asset, the same for everyone. His advice for the early stage is to first acquire a chunk of money and freedom, and ideally also build something you care about, since reinventing the wheel every month is exhausting.
- In-person distribution is "super underrated" for hitting your ICP: conferences pool thousands of ideal customers in one room, so "it's shooting fish in a barrel." He cautions against overcomplicating with too many channels.
