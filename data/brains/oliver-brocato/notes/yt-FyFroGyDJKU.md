## Oliver's origin: the fidget spinner hustle and early reps

- Oliver traces his entrepreneurship to a natural drive: in third grade he ran "pencil insurance," where kids paid one pencil a week and he replaced any lost pencil free. He had commission-based recruiters negotiating deals with other classes and kept the pencils in a fake tissue box, treating them as social currency.
- Around age 14 in 2016 he saw a senior (Alan) put fidget spinners on the map at their school. His reaction: "what does he have that I don't have? I could make these, I could sell these too."
- He built spinners with a donated 3D printer, sold to classmates, then expanded in layers: door-knocking local stores for wholesale, bar mitzvahs and events (3D printers on site, paid by the sponsor), and custom spinners with company logos as recruiting swag.
- When the local market saturated, he moved online to eBay and Etsy. Because he was early in the trend, he optimized listings, copied competitors' keywords, used the best photography, and put an insert in each order offering money off for positive reviews. His listing ranked at the top when people searched "fidget spinner."
- He says that opened him to e-commerce and he "hasn't looked back."
- Other early reps: a social media agency at 15 running Instagram accounts for local mom and pop shops. His first client, his barber, paid in a free haircut. He describes "scores of mini businesses," most of them failures, each adding wisdom.

## Compounding reps, pattern recognition and why business gets easier

- Oliver's view: success comes from experience and intuition rather than IQ. Each failed project is a rep that improves you "by 0.1 percent," and compounding makes success eventually inevitable.
- He compares it to machine learning: after enough situations you recognize which moves work, while newcomers "step on the landmine."
- Reps are also the "universal currency of respect." Doing 400 podcast episodes or 50 business iterations signals seriousness.
- Time in the game is the biggest competitive advantage. He says he spent seven years in the game before Tabs, with many failures.
- Concrete example of it getting easier: with a new SaaS tool (Study Buddy) he went from zero to 2,000 subscribers and $25,000 MRR in two weeks, profitably. He says he had a blueprint: the email flows, the ad types and creatives, the team and the Rolodex of contacts.
- Later-stage advantages he lists:
  - Network alpha: eight-figure operators share information in group chats, Discord and Slack, so he knew about a Facebook update a month early.
  - A Facebook ad rep he can call, instead of having no idea what to do if ads break.
  - Self-funding, so he can hire employees and agencies he once only dreamed of.
  - Lower fear of loss: losing $5-20K used to mean game over, and now a failed business just means starting a new one.
- His caution: he still has levels above him ("some people know how to take it from zero to one billion").

## Tabs chocolate: scale and numbers

- Oliver founded Tabs, a chocolate brand. In its first month it did $280,000 in revenue, sold out of all inventory in the first three weeks, then went viral and took a wave of pre-orders.
- He says the company hit $11 million in a year at 40-45% margins, and that he was 21 and a multimillionaire at the time of recording (May 2023).
- He first paid himself in January 2023, about two and a half years in. Before that he reinvested every dollar into inventory and growth, because inventory is what keeps growth going. He recounts being unable to afford an Uber to a Miami boat day or lunch while giving a talk to a design agency.
- He kept his relationship with money: he still values a dollar the same way and does not spend crazily.
- In July 2022 the business hit a wall: sales fell about 60% in one month and the brand was banned on every social platform. In the same stretch his co-founder said he was going back to school and doing the minimum for his contractual obligations, one intern-turned-roommate left, and another employee tried to extort equity. He was alone in an unfurnished Miami apartment he could not really afford and could not re-enroll in school for a year.
- He recovered by moving back to his parents' house and slowly rebuilding. He says there was no single switch or trick, just time.

## Twitter as a distribution and network engine

- Oliver started tweeting around September 2019 with zero followers and got nothing for a long time. It took off when he began documenting building Tabs: behind the scenes, tactics, strategy and the reasoning behind decisions, "unfiltered" and including the good, bad and ugly.
- He has about 21,000 followers and argues that is more powerful than a 3 million follower Instagram account because the audience is entrepreneurs, mostly in e-commerce, many running multi-million dollar businesses.
- What it produced:
  - Most of his team came directly from Twitter or indirectly through friends there who recommended people.
  - Mentors, advisors and friendships far beyond his geography. He compares it to being "confined geographically" before, with friends chosen by convenience in a two-mile radius.
  - Access: tweeting "I'm in X city, who wants to grab a coffee" gets him hosts, gym passes and stays. He says "anything is one tweet away" and calls it living life on easy mode.
- His YouTube channel (around 3,000 subscribers at recording) is not about direct revenue. He sells no course or Discord and says he spends roughly $5,000 a month on an editor, thumbnails, equipment and travel, with no monetary ROI. He invests because of the life impact, and he sees personal brand and social currency as something to double down on.

## Mindset: define the goal, embody it, keep going

- Oliver's core belief is self-confidence: he always felt "destined for more," and says he credits "the vast majority" of his success to it, since "if you don't believe it, how are you ever going to get there."
- On manifesting: he does not buy the spiritual version, but he does believe that if you define your goal, think about it and obsess over it, you will reach it, as long as you then execute. "Most people don't even know what they want."
- His origin of ambition: his best friend Jack's father left a normal career, bet on himself and became a hedge fund multimillionaire. Oliver saw that his own father was equally brilliant, and the only difference was that Jack's dad took the risk. Oliver says he vowed to bet on himself.
- He sees access to the top as more attainable than people assume ("anybody can do it if they want it bad enough"), though he acknowledges survivorship bias and that upbringing programmed him.
- On persistence he uses the woodpecker and boiling-point analogies: you can be 0.01 degrees from the boiling point with no visible movement, so you cannot quit.
- On starting: "The best time to have taken action was yesterday, the second best is now." The perfect moment does not exist, since college, parents, money or golden handcuffs will always give a reason to wait.
- His weekly takeaway challenge: take 60 seconds, close your eyes, define where you want to be in 5 and 10 years, write it down, and live it every waking moment by putting it on a mirror, laptop or phone screen.

## Staying motivated after success, and what money changes

- Oliver says he thrives in chaos and pressure (he has ADHD). Once the business ran smoothly, with systems working and the team strong, he was bored and struggled to find his earlier hunger.
- He warns that it is easy to get addicted to "cheap dopamine" and coast, which brings its own misery.
- His answer was to move into new challenges: personal brand, YouTube, and a new software business (Study Buddy), though he notes that advisors usually say to focus on one thing.
- He says money does not change who you are, since "we all bleed the same blood" and have similar fears, but it gives tools, freedom and independence. It validated beliefs he held at 16, with a caveat that this partly reflects survivorship bias. It also swaps survival problems for existential ones.
- On motivation shifting: early on it was spite and a chip on his shoulder (rejected from top fraternities, doubted by family and peers). Now he says he has less to prove, and motivation comes from being in rooms with people "a hundred times farther ahead," which humbles him. Bigger watches and boats inspire him rather than upset him, because it shows the level is attainable.
- On money as a scoreboard: he is not optimizing for money but for growth, and money follows the impact and value created.
- On the cost of the path: remote team, no office, friends busy on their own paths, so entrepreneurship can feel lonely. He accepts it as a sacrifice that comes with the level he wants, and says he does not envy the college social life he left.
