## Oliver's product thesis: build something intrinsically viral, then reverse-engineer the brand

- Oliver argues dropshipping is "a bit of a scam" as a beginner entry point. It is arguably harder than building a real brand, because you are always chasing the next fad and the next buck.
- Dropshipping gives a poor customer experience, so there is no LTV. You must be profitable on the first sale, with no room to play with acquisition cost.
- A real brand expects a customer to return 10 to 20 times over 5 to 10 years, which gives margin to spend on acquisition up front.
- He says "the real wealth comes from the exit, not from the cash flow." Every dollar of brand profit is worth 3 to 5x at exit, which is where generational wealth is made, "not off of selling trinkets from Alibaba."
- Most founders build the product and brand first, then work out marketing. Oliver reversed it: "how do I build a product that's going to go viral?"
- Tabs (sex chocolate) genesis: he saw a similar product in a TikTok with 8M views and 2M likes. The brand behind it was not sold online, had weak packaging and no social presence. He concluded it was an intrinsically viral product that someone who knew how to execute could turn into millions.
- He says the product was chosen because he could "market the [expletive] out of this," not out of passion for the category.
- Tabs' hook is explainable in three seconds on video: the chocolate splits in half, one half per partner, so the product needs no explanation or text.
- Results he cites: over $10M revenue this year at 40 to 45% net margin, over 1B organic views, $1M+ in sales in the first six months target and about $4M in year one.

## Building the physical product: sourcing, moq and making complexity the moat

- Oliver started with about $30,000 of savings from earlier ventures (fidget spinners, an agency, dropshipping, eBay and Etsy stores). It took 11 months from idea to first sellable product.
- Chocolate co-manufacturer search: he Googled "chocolate co-manufacturer," scraped the first 20 to 30 pages of results, and called roughly 100 companies asking about minimum order quantity (MOQ).
- Most chocolate co-manufacturers will not touch you unless you order very large runs (he cites around 2 million squares at the extreme). Fixed costs (setup, cleaning equipment) are the same for a 10,000-piece or a 10-million-piece run, so small orders are unattractive to them.
- Only one or two were viable. He picked on MOQ, pricing and samples, ordering about eight chocolates for a taste test with friends.
- His manufacturer specialized in alternative chocolates (superfood, CBD), so adding supplements was a small step. He did not invent a formula: he took the most popular natural sex supplements (usually pills or powder) and repackaged them as a luxury, destigmatized, shareable experience.
- Supply chain: about 8 manufacturers. Chocolate is made in Salt Lake City, Utah. Boxes, chrome poly mailers and custom molds come from China (boxes found on Alibaba, communication over Skype and WeChat). Boxes are labor intensive and he could not find a US maker.
- Lead times: poly mailers take about 90 days to make and ship, so he plans months ahead. If one manufacturer slips or he misorders, everything stops.
- Heat was a real problem he did not plan for: chocolate melts in summer, and customers complained when summer hit. Poly mailers that trap heat were part of the fix.
- He says the difficulty "became our moat": the barrier to entry protected him from the 300 ecom guys who copy a hot product, saturate it and rip you off.

## Luxury positioning, packaging and pricing

- Positioning: "We're not selling chocolate, we're selling an experience, we're selling a mood." Three tiny 20-gram squares sell for $29.99 because the pitch is "the best night of your life with your loved one."
- Every decision follows one value, luxury: scarcity (only three pieces rather than 50 or 100), design and unboxing. Details include gold foil stamping, socials printed inside the lid and an emoji under each piece.
- He designed the box himself in Canva, and the manufacturer's designer cleaned it up into 3D renders. His inspiration was the Truff hot sauce box: a cheap-to-make product (truffle-infused hot sauce) sold at around $30 on branding and packaging.
- For packaging he messaged hundreds of suppliers on Alibaba and got only three or four samples.
- Pricing: launched at $19.99 with no competitor anchor, calling the number "pulled out of our ass." Pills and powders at about $10 are not comparable because Tabs sells an experience.
- When he raised the price, conversion went up, not down. He now split-tests five price points in roughly $2 steps, sending about 20% of traffic to each.
- His logic: a high price signals quality. A trainer who offers sessions for $29.99 reads as bad; one who charges a lot reads as the best. At $4.99 the chocolate would look fake.
- At scale, tiny optimizations compound. Cutting one ounce from the box drops it into a lower shipping bracket and saves over $300,000 a year at his run rate.

## The TikTok creator engine: retainers, many independent accounts, copying winners

- Early plan was micro-influencers (50K to 200K followers, aiming for sub-$1 CPM), with free product plus small cash. It did not scale: only about 30 of every 1,000 outreach emails replied, rates were often unaffordable, creators ghosted or stole product, and sales spiked and crashed after each post.
- Breakthrough: he paid a creator named Keith, found on Twitter with zero followers, a $2,000 flat retainer for 30 TikToks a month (one a day) to run the brand account. By day five a video hit and the month produced $70K to $80K in sales. He calls this the "holy [expletive]" moment, because one creator on retainer is "set and forget" versus chasing influencers.
- To scale, he did not add creators to one account. He argues the algorithm rewards a consistent, niche style, and "too many cooks" confuse it. Instead each creator gets their own independent account.
- Creators create the accounts with their own email, password and phone number, and name them some variation of "tabs". He deliberately does not create or control the accounts, because tying many accounts to his IP risks bans. Hundreds to thousands of accounts exist.
- Casting: he hired "anybody and everybody." If they had a phone and limbs, they were in. He paid roughly $1,000 to $3,000 a month for 30 TikToks, with full creative control. The product is so viral that "even a monkey could get two million views."
- Sourcing creators: Twitter first (leveraging his personal following), then referrals (creators know other creators). Searching "UGC" on Twitter finds thousands.
- Copying: when a video goes viral he sends it to all creators to remake. "It's not about reinventing the wheel," and remakes often hit again.
- He later moved creators to commission or hybrid pay (for example 20% of tracked revenue), which limits his downside and weeds out weak ones. Tracking uses a UTM link in bio plus a discount code, run through Social Snowball.
- His mindset: "I don't want to be in charge of the TV show, I want to own the whole news channel."

## The one-two punch: viral video, then reply-to-comment videos that sell

- Oliver says the viral video is not what drives most sales. The follow-up videos do.
- Step one: optimize for eyeballs and top-of-funnel awareness. TikTok has the cheapest CPMs.
- Step two: respond to comments on the viral video with new TikToks. From the same account, this retargets the original viewers for free. In those videos you sell hard: address pain points and objections, say where to shop, and push urgency.
- Example: a video with over 30M views produced about $40K in sales that day, and the reply videos produced $130K the next day. A week of reply videos took that month to close to seven figures.
- He stumbled on it when a creator tried reply videos and he saw his live dashboard spike by about 800.
- He says this is specific to TikTok and he has not seen it work like that on Instagram.

## Repurposing and paid distribution: Discord affiliate army and meme pages

- Winning TikToks are archived without the watermark (using a downloader) and given a second life on Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, Facebook Reels and Pinterest idea pins.
- A VA with 15 accounts per platform worked at first (a Facebook Reel reached 8M views and about $30K in sales in a day, at zero ad spend), but the platforms noticed one IP and banned the accounts.
- Fix: a "make money online" Discord that started at 5 members and now has over 2,500. Members each set up their own accounts, get the profile picture, bio instructions and the library of viral videos, and post daily. They earn 20% of sales, tracked through Social Snowball.
- He gamified it: a gym-selfie channel, daily reactions fed into a wheel that pays three random members, and invite tournaments paying $100, $50 and $25 to the top three inviters. Some creators now promote the Discord rather than the product, plus shout-out swaps with other Discords.
- He says it has generated millions in revenue. A 14-year-old posting the same videos as everyone else drove about $50K in revenue, and Oliver says the algorithm is a black box: "nobody [expletive] knows," so scale wins.
- Paid distribution, not traditional ads: he pays meme pages with millions of followers to post already-viral content for 24 hours (about $100 to $2,000 per post depending on US engagement). He spends six figures a month on this and says it is profitable.
- On Twitter, a paid native viral tweet is followed a few hours later by a reply tweet like "I just found their website." He keeps it native because "if it feels like an ad it's not going to work."

## Maximizing AOV and LTV: funnel steps, melting insurance, SMS and tech stack

- Without the funnel, average order value would be about $25. Each step adds 5% to 20% and together roughly doubles it. His logic: two stores with the same 100 visitors can make $50 versus $500, so the winner is whoever extracts the most while keeping the experience good.
- Sequence:
  - Product page: one-time purchase only, with 1, 2 or 3 box options (2 boxes: free shipping and a discount; 3 boxes: bigger discount). He found that putting subscriptions here made buyers pick one box plus subscribe instead of 2 or 3 boxes.
  - Cart: "melting insurance" at $4.99, framed as a no-brainer (replacement, money-back guarantee, skip the queue, faster shipping). The take rate is over 80%, and the cost is near zero because he replaced melted chocolate for free anyway.
  - Checkout (Shopify Plus): upgrade to subscription for 20% off.
  - Post-purchase: one-click upsell for another box at 50% off via a Shopify app (OCU), then another upsell on the thank-you page, then emails nudging non-subscribers to subscribe.
- Stack: Klaviyo for email; OneText for SMS; Shopify Plus (about $2,000/month, saving on transaction fees); One Click Upsells; Social Snowball; Trello; Slack for the whole business; Shopify analytics and Google Analytics (no Triple Whale since he runs no ads).
- OneText: Oliver says it lets customers buy inside a text and stores the card. Because abandoned-cart texts are treated as part of the same transaction, he says he can text every abandoner rather than only the roughly 20% who opted into marketing, which he says "5x'd" that reach.
- Creator tracking started as a VA-maintained spreadsheet he reviewed monthly before paying, and later moved to a software tool for views and followers. His philosophy: more creators, more affiliates, more scale.
- Next moves he lists: open to international orders (over 40% of traffic is international), diversify from 99% Shopify D2C to Amazon, Etsy and Walmart, then wholesale and retail (gas stations, smoke shops, sex shops, hotels), and new flavors, moods and lines (shroom, delta-9 CBD, functional foods).

## Mindset, team and personal brand

- Do not put successful people on pedestals. He says the gap is experience, not intelligence: "it's about experience, it's about at-bats." He had about 20 failed ventures first and says "you can't cheat that, there is no shortcut."
- He is skeptical of college: he took a gap year, calls the business classes a joke and says he can return to school. He says many young people are being eaten by the system and that four years spent building a business beats a degree.
- His low point: in July sales fell as accounts were banned, his co-founder stepped back to school, and he moved back home to Tampa, isolated and depressed. It forced him to build a team and systems, and revenue went from about $60K a month in July to over $1M a month. Travel in Europe helped him recover. His takeaway: you cannot run a brand if you are not excited about life.
- Team: 8 to 12 core people, all contractors or agencies, mostly found via Twitter. He prefers few people and notes talent is attracted by a personal brand, and says "your business's value is the sum total of the average IQ of your business."
- Personal brand: only about 20,000 Twitter followers, yet it brought investor DMs, talent offering to work free, events and access. He is investing in YouTube and says it is not monetized but worth it for relationships and information.
- He charges $300 for 30 minutes of consulting to filter serious (seven- and eight-figure) operators, and says paying more is also a luxury-brand effect for courses.
- He warns against course gurus with rented Lamborghinis and small real profits, but says legit courses and masterminds exist and he has bought none himself.
