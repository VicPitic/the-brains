## Why counterfeits and IP theft hurt a brand

Oliver, who built, scaled, and exited an 8-figure e-commerce brand before founding Bustem, treats counterfeits as a direct hit to reputation, not just lost sales. His framing:

- A counterfeit uses someone else's trademark without permission so the maker profits from the owner's reputation.
- Shoppers who meet fakes can lose trust in the genuine product, even when the brand had no control over the situation.
- Fakes are usually inferior, which drives bad reviews on third-party sites from customers who received fakes instead of originals.
- Unauthorized sellers eat into margins and sales through price gouging and heavy discounting. This frustrates retail partners and can sever those relationships.
- Shoppers hunt for low prices, so they get tricked into buying a near-identical fake in place of the real item.

## Growth of the problem (figures he cites)

- 26% of shoppers saw a rise in counterfeits during the pandemic (Intelligence Node survey).
- US CBP seized 26,503 shipments in 2020 containing IP-violating goods, estimated at nearly $1.3 billion had they been genuine.
- 38% of surveyed shoppers came across counterfeit essential items online during the pandemic.
- Shopper distrust by marketplace: eBay (80%) and Amazon (53%) got the most votes for selling counterfeits; one in four shoppers said they stopped buying from eBay.
- Most-counterfeited luxury brands: Louis Vuitton (56%) and Gucci (52%).
- Bustem's own claim: businesses lose $29B annually to content theft, and over 2M DMCA notices are filed daily.

## The four types of IP and how each gets violated

Oliver lists what a business can own:
- **Patents:** the right to exclude others from making, using, selling, or importing the invention. Design patents cover ornamental appearance; utility patents cover function. In the EU they usually last 20 years.
- **Trademarks:** any word, phrase, symbol, design, or combination that identifies your goods and distinguishes you from competitors (Nike symbol, Apple logo). One trademark can cover a range of products, unlike a patent.
- **Copyrights:** protect original works (writing, photos, video, software code, art). Protection is automatic, but he recommends registering if you plan to sell or enforce.
- **Trade secrets:** formulas, methods, and processes you keep secret for economic value. The holder must proactively protect them.

Common violations: patent infringement, trademark use or deliberate lookalike marks, copyright theft of written work, photos, and video, and selling counterfeit products (which can hit patent, copyright, and trademark at once).

His principle on detection: the best defense is being mindful of violations yourself. The sooner you detect and stop one, the better your chance of protecting profits, goodwill, and market position.

## Step one: register your IP

- Registering IP is "the most elementary step" because it lets you prove you own it when you find fakes.
- IP generally must be registered in each country under national law, since copyrights, patents, and trademarks are not globally valid. Registering at home lets you start protecting the brand internationally.

## Choosing an enforcement strategy: litigation vs. takedowns

- Decide your goal first, then pick the strategy.
- Litigation can produce court orders to stop infringement, monetary damages, disgorged profits, and lawyer's fees. You must prove the fake listings misled customers and hurt your brand.
- He calls it a poor fit when the goal is fast removal of listings and protecting reputation and sales: it is time-consuming, expensive, and has no guarantee of success. Consider it only after weighing risks and rewards.
- His default is enforcing against the listings directly. The first step is taking them down, and the process depends on the platform. E-commerce platforms and social networks generally know the problem and support enforcement, so knowing each platform's process lets you act fast.

## Takedowns are whack-a-mole, so monitor and enforce at scale

- Taking down one counterfeit does not solve the problem. Infringers re-list across multiple marketplaces and countries, so individual enforcement hits its limit.
- His answer is scanning the internet continuously and submitting takedowns at scale with technology, monitoring and enforcing internationally across marketplaces.
- Bustem's approach (his product): automatically scan billions of websites for unauthorized use of store content, videos, headlines, and text. Then streamline takedowns with pre-filled DMCA forms and case management, 24/7 monitoring, instant detection, and bulk takedowns.
- Stated results: enforced takedowns on 100,000+ listings, reclaimed over $1M in lost revenue, and protected 120+ brands.

## Supply chain and anti-counterfeiting technology

- **Supply chain:** counterfeiters exploit loopholes. Use strong supplier contracts and maintain traceability.
- **Physical authentication tools he lists:** holograms and security labels, unique serial numbers and barcodes (traceability and recalls), RFID tags with encrypted data, and tamper-evident packaging.
- **Track and trace:** barcodes, RFID, and GPS to follow products through the supply chain, detect diversion, and find where counterfeiting happens.
- **Authentication apps:** consumers scan a QR code or barcode to verify origin and manufacturing details.
- **Online monitoring and enforcement:** watch marketplaces, social channels, and ecommerce sites for fake or unauthorized listings, using software to automate detection and takedown.

## Collaboration and customer education

- Work with law enforcement and industry associations to share intelligence, coordinate enforcement, and support legal action. Build those relationships before you need them.
- Educate customers: tell them where to buy legitimate products, list unauthorized products on the market and how to spot them, and explain how to report suspected fakes.
- Encourage customer reviews, since shoppers rely on reviews and ratings when deciding what to buy.
