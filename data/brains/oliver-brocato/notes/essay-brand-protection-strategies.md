## How Oliver defines brand protection and why he cares

- Oliver founded Bustem, a copycat detection and takedown tool for e-commerce merchants. Before that he built, scaled and exited an 8-figure e-commerce brand, where he "experienced firsthand the pain of knockoffs, copycats, and counterfeits."
- Bustem's stated results: takedowns on 100,000+ listings, over $1M in lost revenue reclaimed, 120+ brands protected, from startups to global enterprises. It is trusted by 200+ 7, 8 and 9-figure e-com brands, including Comfrt, Plunge and Mellow Sleep.
- He defines brand protection as proactively safeguarding a company's intellectual property, reputation and other assets, to prevent misuse, counterfeiting or reputational harm.
- The stakes he cites: counterfeiting is 2.5% of global trade (OECD), and businesses lose $29B annually to content theft. Counterfeits confuse customers and hurt a legitimate brand's trust, not just its revenue.
- His reputation-crisis example: if a trusted snack brand reported a contamination issue, customers would panic and abandon it even if the product was safe, and recovery of reputation and sales would be slow. Protection means safeguarding the brand before, during and after a crisis.
- A copycat copying your product images or descriptions hurts you three ways: it diverts traffic and sales, it can outrank your original content in search, and it confuses customers and dilutes the unique value of your products.

## The legal and domain foundation

- **Trademarks:** register your brand name, logo and distinctive elements so you have exclusive rights to use them in the marketplace.
- **Patents:** if you create new products or processes, patent them to block competitors from replicating them.
- **Copyrights:** register written content, software and digital media. Registration gives you the legal grounds to act against infringers.
- **Domains:** register your brand domain as soon as you create the brand, before a counterfeiter does. Consider brand-aligned TLDs (.com, .net, .org, niche TLDs).
- **Typosquatting:** monitor domains that use common misspellings of your name. They cost you traffic and trust and enable phishing and customer confusion.

## Build recognition and monitor continuously

- Consistent logo, colors and fonts across all packaging, marketing and online platforms strengthen recognition and deter imitators.
- Write brand guidelines for your team, partners and third-party vendors so the brand is represented uniformly.
- He says to build protection in from the beginning, as you build the brand, and to keep the brand management team on the same page.
- Registering trademarks and domains is not the end. He calls for continuous monitoring so you catch infringement before it harms you:
  - Trademark monitoring: check trademark databases and scan marketplaces like Amazon and eBay for counterfeits.
  - Social media monitoring: use social listening to track brand mentions and unauthorized use, since counterfeit goods and copycat products are often advertised there.
  - Search engine monitoring: watch results for counterfeit websites or fraudulent sellers using your name.
  - Tools he names: Google Alerts and Brandwatch.
- A strong online presence, he argues, connects you with customers and makes it harder for counterfeiters to trick them. He cites an OECD finding that brands prioritizing online presence handle infringement better, with less revenue loss. He gives Nike as an example of a brand fighting fake sites and social accounts.

## The enforcement ladder when infringement happens

- He expects infringement to occur despite your best efforts, so act "quickly and decisively." The escalation order:
  1. **Cease and desist letter:** often the first step. A formal notice asking the infringer to stop using your brand assets.
  2. **Legal action:** if it continues or escalates, file a lawsuit or seek an injunction, which can stop the infringement and win damages.
  3. **Online enforcement:** use platform takedown channels at Amazon, eBay, Google and similar.
- Bustem's own approach to takedowns: pre-filled DMCA forms, case management, 24/7 monitoring, instant detection and bulk takedowns. He notes over 2M DMCA notices are filed daily.
- When vetting a provider, he says to check that it has automated takedowns and can enforce on difficult social channels such as Telegram, has established relationships with relevant authorities, and has the right patents and experience for your needs.

## Technology, experts and combining old with new

- Use AI and automated tools to scan the internet for counterfeits and trademark infringements. You can do it manually, "but why waste time" when tools can, leaving you to focus on legal action and strategy.
- Going DIY is hard: it needs constant checking and infringements slip through the cracks. Many businesses hire a specialized brand protection or DMCA takedown agency.
- What agencies offer, in his view: monitoring, enforcement and legal support; knowledge of IP law; relationships with major platforms and regulators; global coverage across countries with different laws; and time savings through automation.
- Mix old and new: offline methods like customs checks and border protection stop fakes entering the market, while online tools fight cyber threats. OCR (optical character recognition) can find fake goods that don't show up in ordinary searches.
- Where the field is heading in 2025 and beyond:
  - AI-driven visual detection, real-time monitoring and cross-platform enforcement.
  - 31.8% of shoppers first discovered fake goods on social media (State of Brand Protection Report 2025), so every channel needs guarding.
  - Deep learning on images: a study reached 99.71% accuracy telling genuine from fake garments from smartphone photos. Brands no longer need to rely on keyword monitoring alone, since fakes can be flagged even when titles and copy are disguised.
  - Closer partnership with marketplaces, such as Amazon embedding AI in its brand protection tools.

## Internal alignment and consumer education

- Educate every key department about brand protection: sales, marketing, digital, legal, manufacturing, supply chain and finance. Teach them to recognize brand abuse, report it, and apply the protection strategies.
- Educate consumers too: show them how to identify genuine products and the harm of fakes by sharing unique product features, price guidelines and security indicators.

## Setting up a protection program

1. **Assess brand risks.** List threats (cyber-attacks, social media scandals, negative reviews), analyze each one's impact on finances and customer trust, and estimate likelihood and frequency from past incidents and trends. He warns small issues can escalate into major crises if ignored.
2. **Write a protection plan.** Set clear objectives, identify threats such as counterfeiting and trademark infringement and decide the action for each, and allocate budget for monitoring software, legal counsel and employee training. Define roles, protocols and a clear escalation path for internal reporting and for communicating with affected customers and partners.
3. **Engage a provider.** Research services, patents and experience, match offerings to your needs, and confirm partnerships with authorities, automated takedowns and hard-channel enforcement.
4. **Monitor and update.** Track incidents reported, response times and resolutions achieved, stay current on industry trends and emerging risks, and refine the strategy from new insights and stakeholder feedback. He says you can't "set it and forget it."

## Advanced stage: when infringers keep coming back

- The signal for moving up: you face a high volume of counterfeits and repeat infringers.
- His advanced playbook:
  - Connect the dots to find the source of the problem.
  - Shift into business intelligence gathering mode.
  - Integrate online and offline protection efforts.
  - Take a proactive approach to IP infringement.
- He says the right insights to inform decisions are what separate a regular strategy from an outstanding one.
