## How Oliver launched Tabs Chocolate and survived being banned from paid ads

- Tabs Chocolate is an adult-product brand Oliver co-founded with Jake (50/50 partners). Oliver says it went from zero to seven figures "pretty much solely through organic."
- At launch the brand was banned from Facebook, TikTok ads, Google ads and Snapchat ads, so he was forced into alternative channels. He had TikTok in mind from the start.
- In the weeks before launch he worked around the clock messaging influencers through DMs, texts, the TikTok Creator Marketplace and email.
  - Criteria: US-based, 50k to 250k followers (micro creators, cheaper), plus a few based creators.
  - He landed roughly 20 to 30 in the pipeline.
- Result of the one-off posts: revenue spiked when an influencer posted (a few hundred dollars up to a couple thousand on a good one), then dropped back to zero the next day. He called it a good sign but not a baseline.
- He found one-off influencer work unsustainable. It took thousands of DMs, emails and texts. Most people ignore you, those who reply charge exorbitant rates, and half of those who say yes ghost after getting the product. He built a team of virtual assistants to run it and still called it "a crap shoot."

## The TikTok organic system: one creator per brand-page account

- Oliver connected with Jimmy Farley and Kimiko, who were pioneering TikTok organic: create a brand-page account, put a creator on retainer, and have them post about 30 TikToks over a month (one a day).
- His first creator, Keon, did roughly $60-70k in sales in the first month, directly attributable to his account.
- Scaling problem: adding more creators to the same account hurts the algorithm. TikTok "doesn't want too many chefs in the kitchen." It understands an account's person, ethos and consistency, then shows good ones to an audience. Eight creators with eight styles lose the account's niche.
- Examples he gives of accounts that win by repeating one format: the Subway guy and the Coldstone ice cream account (Dylan Lemay), the same video over and over.
- His solution: scale horizontally. One independent creator per account, across many accounts. Over about eight months he scaled to 100+ creators and 100+ accounts.
- It is still one of Tabs' strongest channels and produces an "overwhelming" share of monthly revenue. It runs as a constant hamster wheel: bring in new creators, test them, cut low performers, double down on high performers. Many of the original creators have been cut over time.
- Oliver says he is not claiming to have invented the strategy but was an early pioneer, and notes someone else ran the astrology version of it better than he did.

## The affiliate Discord: 2,000 affiliates reposting content

- Oliver runs a Discord with about 2,000 affiliates. They take Tabs' viral content and post it on their own subsidiary brand pages (Instagram, YouTube and more) with unique coupon codes.
- Everyone posts the same videos at different times and in different geographies, and randomly some hit 1 to 7 million views. Per-affiliate coupon codes make revenue attributable.
- He says it has generated over half a million dollars in revenue in roughly four months, directly from reposts.
- How he incentivizes it:
  - 20% commission on any sale.
  - A base-pay stipend for posting 30 days straight.
  - Daily raffle spins: a VA compiles the names of people who posted and reacted, spins a wheel, and the winner gets $5, with three or four spins a day.
  - Challenges, such as inviting the most people in a day wins $100.
  - A wins chat running 24/7 (first sale, $1,000 in commissions), plus general, gym and game chats so it feels like a community, not just a payout.
- He describes it as applying the Andrew Tate clipping model to an e-commerce brand and says he gamified the whole thing inside Discord.
- On attribution: he admits organic is hard to attribute, but says he lives and breathes the business and has a "Spider Sense" for what is pushing, from watching views and channels.

## What's next for Tabs: from seven to eight figures

Oliver names three levers:
1. **International.** About 40% of all traffic comes from outside the US, and Tabs only ships domestically, so shipping internationally is a big unlock.
2. **More channels.** Currently fully direct-to-consumer on Shopify. He wants Amazon and Walmart, plus wholesale to mom-and-pop shops: smoke shops, sex shops, convenience stores, hotels, cruises.
3. **Paid ads.** He started with no connections; now if a Facebook account is banned he can call friends to get it live, or talk to a Facebook rep. They are testing Facebook and Google with a new landing page, PG-friendly ad creative and reframed copy.

Beyond that he will keep scaling existing systems and wants to return to his roots by working with bigger influencers at bigger spend. He also says Twitter theme pages are "super powerful, super slept on," but only work with a viral, edgy, eye-catching product. Tabs plays the game "on easy mode" in that sense.

## Advice: start with TikTok organic and stick to one thing

- For people starting or stuck, Oliver says the biggest opportunity now is TikTok organic. You can test and validate a product for free, no creator needed.
  - He would post three TikToks a day, or be the face of the brand himself.
  - He cites founders telling stories and showing behind the scenes, so customers fall in love with the brand's ethos, "which is incredibly powerful because obviously you can sell them anything."
  - He thinks no other channel lets an entry-level person go from zero to six or seven figures with only time and hard work as investment.
- Intuition is the moat. He argues the person doing eight or nine figures is not smarter, just has "seen more." Experience leads to intuition leads to success. The same principles apply whether selling chocolate, leggings or software.
- Focus beats jumping around. The people who win do one thing "like a machine" over years and compound. He warns against drifting from day trading to NFTs to crypto to dropshipping.
- Start now: he points to Airbnb's ugly first site as proof that the key is starting and seeing it through.

## Building a personal brand and finding partners

- Oliver has documented the Tabs journey on Twitter for about two years: behind-the-scenes, tactics, strategy, "sauce," plus thoughts and feelings.
- All his agency clients (G Fuel, with talks with Budweiser and Orangetheory) came inbound from that. The agency has no website, business card or logo.
- He spends roughly 30 to 60 minutes a day in DMs. He doesn't sell a course or run a Discord, and sees the return in friendships, connections, exchanged tactics and unexpected opportunities (even investor offers).
- He suggests a VA or appointment setter to triage DMs into a primary inbox and a general one.
- Plans: a YouTube channel, maybe a newsletter, and clipping long-form into TikTok.
- On partnerships: do exceptional work and people line up to work with you. "If you want to find a good partner you need to be a good partner yourself." Partnerships are like marriage, so get on a call, befriend the person and do some work first, rather than cold DMing a partnership pitch.
- His agency partnership with Jimmy came from both taking on clients on the side, then realizing the synergy. Jake came through a mutual contact and working together day in and day out.

- Routine: wakes around 6 to 6:30, goes to the gym right away (he says he has ADHD and exercise helps focus and productivity), is in bed around 9:30 to 10. He uses cold plunges and works with a fitness and life coach. He believes health, discipline and business are all tied together.
- On setbacks early on: the partner went missing, a right-hand guy tried to extort him for equity, TikTok banned every video, and he questioned leaving school. His view: the lower the lows, the more they recalibrate you, and every successful person has stories of getting scammed, going broke or nearly losing it all.
- He lives with fellow entrepreneurs Jimmy Farley and Luke in Scottsdale. He says surrounding yourself with people in the same trenches, who share strategies and compete, speeds up growth. He met them on Twitter and chose the location for the people, not the place.
