## Choosing a niche and building the right skill first

- Oliver says that to make money online, the answer may be less a business model than a skill set you think you're better at than most people, in something you enjoy.
- His argument: building a business takes heavy sacrifice and hard work. If you hate what you do, you compete against people who "love it" and "live and breathe it," and you probably fail.
- He says you can "literally make money doing anything." His examples: people who made millions in Roblox and Minecraft, and a Minecraft skins and maps seller doing over $1M/month. Oliver's own first business was Minecraft and it made his first million.
- He calls the social media boom one of the biggest opportunities of his generation. Pick an interest (cars, cooking, knitting), make short-form video about it, build a following, then work out how to monetize.
- He thinks younger people (10 to 30) have an edge because older, more experienced people don't understand social dynamics.
- Every young person he knows who is "absolutely killing it" is doing something around social media.
- His advice for the very first dollar is counterintuitive for a founder: go work for or apprentice under someone a few steps ahead of you. He calls this "probably the biggest shortcut."
- The core skill he wants people to learn is getting impressions, views and clicks through social media. If you can't afford to hire people for it, you have to learn it yourself.
- He notes that many of the best online businesses have an internal creator or content team, or the founder does the content.

## Distribution is the biggest lever in every business model

- Oliver's view: attention and distribution are the lever across ecom, consumer software, B2B software and info.
- Ecom: "that's like the biggest lever that separates winners and losers in ecom is your ad creative" (Meta ads). He allows this is an oversimplification given product and website matter.
- Consumer software: same thing, ad creative.
- B2B software: "the biggest cheat code of B2B software is a founder personal brand."
- His proof: with about 50,000 X followers, he changed his link in bio to a 30-minute consulting call. He charged $300, then $500, then about $1,250, and did over 100 calls in a year to a year and a half without promoting it.
- His B2B software business went from zero to $370K MRR in under a year and a half, mostly off his personal brand and Twitter following.
- He stopped the consulting calls because they were boring (everyone asked how to go viral on TikTok) and because he agrees you don't get rich selling your time. He prefers building assets with enterprise value, though he says truly passive businesses are mostly a myth.
- His working method: get eyeballs first, then work backwards to the best way to monetize them.

## Learning from early exposure and local role models

- Oliver saw it was possible through a high school senior who sold 3D-printed fidget spinners and advertised on meme pages (stories and 24-hour shoutouts), reaching hundreds of thousands of dollars a month at peak.
- He also followed the big ecom gurus of the time, but says the local example was what made it feel real.
- He started his own fidget spinner company, listing early on eBay and Etsy and ranking well organically by being early.
- He helped a friend's mom scale her boutique fashion brand, then worked for that senior, who mentored him. He acted as a VA on an app growth project that raised $10M, handling creators, negotiating and overseeing posts.
- Earnings were small, but the payoff was exposure, pattern recognition and intuition, so when he started his own businesses he wasn't "scratching and trying to figure everything out."
- His point: joining someone else's tribe shortcuts a lot of pain. Going alone from the start is "way, way too overwhelming."

## Picking a product: validate it, then execute better

- On Tabs (an aphrodisiac chocolate brand he took past $10M a year from age 19 to about 21), the lessons from earlier ecom were that logo-slapped products got saturated, ad returns compressed and margins dropped. So he wanted something novel and hard to copy.
- Tabs was a food and supplement product. It took over a year from idea to launch, and he says that in the make-money-online world a year is like ten for others.
- Virality was designed in: each chocolate square breaks in half, one half per partner, so a TikTok can show how it works without a word.
- Validation came from a competitor's TikTok with about 7M views and 2M likes. That competitor had no social presence, no ads and barely a website, which Oliver calls a "dumpster fire of execution." He brought ecom experience and improved the product.
- Cost: about $30,000 total to get a first production run worth about $100,000 retail, which sold out in the first three weeks.
- His rule: don't try to invent something wholly new. Do something with existing validation, then tweak, iterate and improve. Nvidia made chips for video games; Slash (a bank for internet businesses) started with sneaker resellers.
- On a client's competitor: a prospect already working with a competitor "means they have a problem" and a budget. He argues they may be better clients, and he challenged his COO's plan to avoid them.
- Moats: food products are hard because of co-manufacturers, formulation R&D and minimum order quantities. But "the biggest moat comes down to the execution." His example is Cal AI (photo calorie tracker), which went from $0 to $30M a year despite about 400 clones, because it executes better on ads, onboarding conversion and pricing, which lets it afford a higher acquisition cost.
- With AI builders, Shopify and tools like Cursor and Claude, building is cheap, so "strong operators will win."

## Study Buddy: a single painpoint and a scaling playbook

- Study Buddy is a Chrome extension where you screenshot a problem, AI answers and gives step-by-step solutions.
- His principle: find a very specific micro niche, solve one pain point very well, and have an "aha moment." Don't build "the everything app."
- It didn't need to be novel. It removed friction students already had (copy, open a tab, paste into ChatGPT, or text a friend).
- First attempt: he ran the Tabs playbook (creators, influencers, meme pages). He sank about $10 to 20K and got back only a couple thousand dollars.
- What worked: testing channels until TikTok ads, using his best organic videos as the ads.
- Back-end optimization: he changed pricing from $20/month to $5/week, added a trial and tested a 99 cent trial.
- Retention was strong. He turned ads off in the summer (acquisition cost roughly doubled out of school year) and the app still kept growing from word of mouth.
- Numbers: TikTok ads gave about $35 to $40 LTV against about a $7 CAC. Meta ads gave about a $20 CAC against about a $55 LTV.

## Bust: the MVP, free pilots, and charging early

- Bust (BustM) scans the internet for ecom brands and finds copycats, counterfeits and unauthorized resellers, then gets them removed. It targets their infrastructure (domain, registrar, host, payment processor) with complaints and evidence, with about a 70% success rate. Brands pay a monthly subscription.
- Timeline: idea in September 2024. He found a technical co-founder via Y Combinator's co-founder matching (no YC needed). The co-founder flew to Miami, slept on his couch and built the MVP in about 48 hours. Oliver gave him a sizable chunk of the business.
- Launch: he teased "a crazy AI tool for ecom brands" to his Instagram and Twitter audience and sought beta testers doing over $1M a month. He stayed secretive on what it was, and his DMs flooded.
- He gave 20 to 30 brands free access and says he regrets it. They didn't even log in, the Slack was "ghost and crickets," and the feedback wasn't honest.
- His rule: people who pay give real feedback. His first $5,000/month client "was on our asses" and produced more insight than all the free users combined.
- He disputes that you should work for free, even for agencies: a real price earns respect, and free "discredits you." If you can't charge, you aren't providing enough value. At most, give a discounted rate.
- Charging started in February 2025, about four weeks after beta began. March 1 revenue was about $30 to 35K MRR, and the second month about $80 to 85K MRR, a run rate near $1M a year.
- The software MVP made brands do hours of takedown "homework," which they refused, so he pivoted it into a service. He hired a virtual assistant from South Africa, ran a shared Slack channel and a Google spreadsheet, and did everything manually end to end. Brands paid for that.

## Fixing fulfillment and hiring to scale Bust past $80K to about $375K MRR

- Around spring 2025, revenue sat near $80K and then dipped. Clients churned because results were poor (they paid about $3K a month). Takedown success was about 20%, the team was reporting to wrong inboxes and resubmitting the same evidence.
- Team at that point: Oliver, one engineer and one VA. Oliver did all marketing, growth and sales, and the engineer was acting as a pseudo account manager in Slack.
- Fixes, in order:
  - He paused growth and gave clients a free month. He went from one VA to about four.
  - He pulled the engineer out of Slack and said the only job was writing code.
  - He hired an operations lead (a former chief of staff at another ecom software company) to own day-to-day delivery.
  - He hired Tony from a competitor to train the VAs. With SOPs and processes, success rose from 20% to 40, 60, 70 and then about 80%.
- He then went back to selling and took 600 to 700 sales calls himself.
- He replaced the first engineer, who was building a fully automated pipeline that would take years. The new engineer shipped easy wins (small automations in a day) and a client dashboard instead of spreadsheets. Oliver paid the first engineer out with six figures.
- The hire went in when revenue was about $30K a month with about $10K profit, against a $12 to 15K a month salary. It cost a negative month (around June), and since then revenue has grown every month. He says he was hiring "players," not stars: "get asses in seats."
- Current team: about 40 people, including a three-person dev team, an ops head, a client success manager, a head of brand protection with 10 years of experience, about 25 South African VAs, multiple sales people, and a pod focused on the first 30 days of new clients and at-risk accounts.
- Targets: about $375K to nearly $400K MRR now, with the aim of a million a month. He is learning org charts, company values, OKRs and a weekly CEO dashboard with department heads.

## Growth is trial and error, and where Oliver is focused now

- Acquisition channels so far: his Twitter following (about 40 calls a month, a ceiling he hasn't broken), cold email (about 15 calls a month; a cold email agency took volume from 100K to 600K emails a month and quality and bookings fell), and Meta ads (about 20 calls a month, near break-even; cost per call went from about $560 to $1,200, which he attributes to ad fatigue or macro shifts).
- He is now pulling back Meta, running dinners and events, and hired a head of partnerships to cross-sell and share leads with companies that serve ecom brands.
- He cites Hormozi: "one acquisition channel, one avatar, one offer until you get to a million a month," and admits founders often chase a new channel instead of confronting the skill gap behind a plateau. He thinks the answer is probably going deeper in one channel.
- His view: "growth is expensive and growth is very hard" because most things you test won't hit, and testing needs real budget, time and effort.
- He read Alex Becker's tweet on the Hyros funnel (5 sales call webinar versions, 25+ ads, over $100K in ad tests, 153 audiences, 22 landing page variations, 200+ hours). His takeaway: top operators make it look effortless, but "none of this is easy" and there is no silver bullet.
- Current priorities: a new pricing model (rolled out), a better sales hire, content aimed at bigger "enterprisey" brands (a better writer), an appointment setter, engineers, a client success manager and a lead brand protection specialist.
- Churn is about 6 to 8% monthly, per the host's mention, and Oliver acknowledges it could be better.
- Team lessons: ecom brands can be very lean (Primal Queen, a 100M-plus brand with three co-founders; Melo Sleep, zero to $100M in about eight months). Bust is the opposite: a human-capital business where "you're only as good as your team."
