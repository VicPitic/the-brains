## How Oliver defines and spots a copycat brand

- Oliver defines a copycat brand as one that imitates another business's products, services, or overall identity to profit from its reputation without original ideas or innovation.
- He separates copycats from counterfeits. A copycat gets as close as possible to a well-known brand (shape, packaging, colour, name) without clearly infringing, while a counterfeit is a fake product.
- He lists the signs of a copycat brand:
  - Follows rather than leads, so it lacks originality.
  - Lacks focus and tries to cater to everyone with a sprawl of products and services.
  - Messaging is generic, talks about features, and shows no empathy for a defined audience.
  - Built on a service-based Unique Selling Proposition instead of the customer's emotional state.
  - Single-use offers with no customer journey, so it has to keep chasing new customers.
  - Forgettable: looks, sounds, and acts like everyone else offering the same thing.
- His example scenario is a skateboard gear seller who finds a social page with their products, an almost identical logo and name, and a bargain-hunter audience instead of the premium one they built.

## Why copycats are dangerous

- They erode trust: buyers expect the original's quality, and if disappointed, the real brand takes the reputational hit, not the copycat.
- They take sales and dilute market share and profitability.
- They create consumer risk. A lookalike electronic device may not meet safety standards.
- They often infringe trademarks, which forces the original brand to divert resources from innovation to defending its IP.
- AI makes this worse. He points to deepfake endorsements, AI-generated fake reviews (the FTC's Impersonation Rule from April 2024 and its August 2024 rule banning fake reviews and testimonials), and WIPO guidance on generative AI and IP risk.

## What makes a brand authentic, and what copycats can't copy

- Authentic brands are built on a unique value proposition, innovative products, and a distinct voice, which builds loyalty and brand equity.
- They know exactly who they serve, keep things simple, and close the loop by collecting customer feedback.
- Oliver says original brands build on the "Unique Buying State of the Customer", not on features. He cites that about 65% of purchasing decisions are emotional, so their messaging targets the customer's emotional state before and after purchase.
- Feature-based positioning is short-term, because competitors catch up and force constant reinvention.
- Authentic brands design for the customer's stage in the sales cycle. They offer minimal, much cheaper entry offers so prospects can taste the brand before committing a large sum.
- Copycats can't easily replicate:
  - Customer relationships and trust built over years.
  - Organizational culture and accumulated expertise.
  - Brand story and values.
  - The innovation pipeline.

## Legal frame: passing off, confusion, and unfair advantage

- He frames most copycat cases around two grounds: likelihood of confusion, and unfair advantage or detriment to a mark's distinctive character or reputation.
- Quote he uses from Perry v Truefitt: "A man is not to sell his own goods under the pretence that they are the goods of another man."
- The more distinctive the mark (Kodak, Zappos, Apple), the easier it is to prove confusion. Generic or descriptive marks make confusion harder to establish.
- Cases he cites:
  - Reckitt & Colman v Borden (1990), the "Jif Lemon" case that set the passing-off test.
  - United Biscuits v Asda (1997), Puffin vs Penguin biscuits, where similar packaging misled consumers.
  - Red Bull v Big Horn (2020): no confusion was found, but the copycat still took unfair advantage of the branding.
  - Thatchers v Aldi: Aldi's "Taurus Cloudy Cider Lemon" did not infringe, because the names were aurally and conceptually different.
  - William Grant & Sons v Lidl: the court found it hard to see the Hampstead gin redesign as coincidental, giving Hendrick's a reasonable prospect on intent.
- Unfair advantage can be found even without intent to exploit the reputation.

## Five ways Oliver says to deal with copycats

**1. Be flattered, then run an IP audit.**
- Reframe the anger: being copied signals the business is successful enough to imitate.
- Before calling an attorney, check trademark, copyright, trade dress, and patent exposure.
- Document consumer confusion, and screenshot and archive evidence with timestamps.
- For minor, clear infringement, a well-crafted cease and desist often resolves it cheaply. If the copying is extensive, intentional, and causing measurable harm, hire an IP attorney.

**2. Mark your territory with IP protection before it's needed.**
- Register trademarks for name, logo, taglines, and distinctive product names, including variations.
- Register copyright for your most valuable assets (site content, product photography, marketing videos, training materials), because registration unlocks statutory damages and attorney's fees.
- Keep dated records of asset creation, product launches, and market entry.
- Fight when the copying hits revenue, causes confusion, is in your core markets, involves your registered marks, or when you must enforce consistently to keep strong rights.
- Monitor but don't engage when the similarity is superficial, the market or geography is different, enforcement would cost more than the damage, or only unprotectable ideas are copied.
- Use platform tools such as Amazon Brand Registry and Project Zero, the TikTok IP Protection Center, and AI monitoring plus DMCA takedown services, since swift action can resolve things in days rather than months.

**3. Differentiate and stay customer-focused.**
- He quotes Bezos: "If we can keep our competitors focused on us while we stay focused on the customer, ultimately we'll turn out all right."
- Time spent obsessing over imitators instead of customers erodes the advantage.
- Invest in deep customer understanding, superior service delivery, and continuous value creation. Customers with good experiences become less price-sensitive.
- He notes a copycat watching you is neglecting its own strategy, which tends to lead to mediocrity.

**4. Confront the copycat directly and professionally.**
- Reach out before involving lawyers: it can reveal their motives, resolve things faster, and preserve relationships.
- Be professional, not personal. Ask questions first, state specific concerns, explain the business impact, and offer solutions such as changing branding or messaging.
- It works best with small operators, no sign of malice, recent copying, and a willingness to find an acceptable outcome.
- Skip straight to a cease and desist or DMCA notice when the copying is intentional, informal contact was ignored, registered marks or copyrights are involved, confusion is already happening, they operate at scale, or they copy multiple competitors.
- Document all communications either way.

**5. Out-innovate them.**
- He calls innovation the ultimate defense: by the time a copycat replicates today's offering, you've moved to the next generation, which makes copying less economically viable.
- Tactics: adopt emerging tech first, foster experimentation, fund R&D even when business is good, and stay close to evolving customer needs.
- He adds strategic partnerships, patents and trademarks for key breakthroughs, and co-innovation with engaged customers who then resist switching to imitations.
- Operational quality (reliability, delivery speed, support) counts as innovation and is more defensible than product features, because it's embedded in the organization.
- Innovation compounds, so the gap to imitators widens over time.
