## How Oliver builds a no-ad organic marketing engine on short-form video

- Oliver co-founded Tabs Chocolate. He says it did about $9 million in revenue last year at a 40% net profit margin, entirely from organic strategies with no paid ads (Meta and TikTok ads are blocked in his sex/supplement category).
- The product is aphrodisiac chocolate with natural supplements (maca, epimedium, DHEA) that normally come as pills or powder. He turned a taboo into a "shared luxury experience."
- He started in e-commerce at 14 with fidget spinners. He later co-founded a leggings brand that grew 0 to 7 figures on early TikTok with micro-influencers, then got squeezed out because it had no patents, IP or ownership. "We simply slapped our logo on it."
- For the next product he set two requirements: inherently viral and engineered for TikTok, and a space with high barriers to entry so nobody can dupe it from an AliExpress supplier.
- He found the idea in a random TikTok about a competing aphrodisiac chocolate with 7 million views and 2 million likes. The competitor had no website, no social presence and terrible branding and packaging. He built a premium, camera-friendly version: each square sits in a divot with emojis underneath, and the bar breaks in half, one half per partner.

## Build virality into the product first

- His rule: nothing else works unless the product itself is built and primed for virality.
- A viral product needs to be edgy and a scroll-stopper. It can't be a commoditized good you'd find at a mom-and-pop shop, on Amazon or at Target. It has to be novel.
- Examples he cites:
  - Cloudy Vapes: a melatonin diffuser, like a vape without nicotine. It takes something people know and adds a twist.
  - Mini Katana: tiny anime swords, eight figures a year, all from their own TikTok channel.
  - Peachy: slime, a category already satisfying and big in short form. The founder is on track for over $25 million this year, has never run a paid Facebook ad, and just launched his first email and SMS campaign.

## Why influencer outreach failed and horizontal scaling worked

- Early micro-influencer outreach was a logistical mess:
  - Instagram rate-limited DMs.
  - Influencers ghosted or charged "an arm and a leg."
  - Spreadsheets, VAs and agencies didn't fix it.
  - Sales were a roller coaster: one influencer video drove $500 to $1,000 in a day, then sales returned to zero.
- His conclusion: "if we put out enough short form we will inevitably win," so the job is systemizing output.
- He found a dropshipper called Key via a tweet, who promoted high-powered flashlights only through a TikTok brand page posting 1 to 3 videos a day. Key had a $30,000 sales day.
- Oliver pitched him: stay in your zone of genius, make high-converting viral content, and be an affiliate for Tabs while Oliver handles e-commerce. Key posted over 90 videos in month one and drove $8,000 in revenue.
- That revealed a whole community of creators like Key on Twitter/X. Oliver onboarded them the same way, each with their own independent TikTok account, and scaled horizontally to hundreds of creators and hundreds of accounts.
- His reasons for one account per creator:
  - TikTok favors accounts that follow a specific formula and style. Many creators on one page means "too many cooks" and TikTok stifles views.
  - Separate accounts give clean analytics on who drives views and sales, which matters when paying on performance.
  - It plays the law of averages and large numbers.
  - Diverse creators bring diverse audiences: young creators get shown to young people, an LGBTQ creator to LGBTQ audiences. Each account acts like a miniature ad set.
- Follower count barely matters on TikTok; video quality does. He points out Charli D'Amelio has a secondary account with a default avatar and a random username that still gets huge views.
- He is explicit that this is a "spray and pray" model: work with many creators, expect most to ghost or make weak content, and operate at enough scale to see returns. His minimum test is 3 to 5 creators for 3 months. Judging after half a month is like loading Facebook ads or Amazon PPC with an absurdly low budget and being disappointed.

## Finding, paying and onboarding creators

**Who to recruit**
- He doesn't want "pretty UGC types" with nice-looking videos. He wants people whose videos convert and drive traffic and sales.
- His niche is dropshippers who already promote their own stores through organic TikTok. He found most creators by building in public on Twitter, posting "we're hiring" tweets with a Google Form link, and onboarding "anybody and everybody."
- On fear that creators will steal the account: he says it won't happen, and worst case a contract protects you.

**Pay structure (hybrid)**
- Keep upfront cash as low as possible, but creators need to eat, so he pays a retainer of about $500 to $1,000 a month plus 20% of revenue they drive.
- The 20% applies only to attributed revenue (UTM link or promo code). Most sales aren't tracked that way, since people see a video and search the brand organically, so he says the true cost is more like 4% to 10% of revenue.
- Tools he recommends: Exolyt for analytics; Social Snowball for UTM links, promo codes and paying creators at scale.

**Onboarding**
- Give a very general brief (good lighting, broad basics).
- Give a document of past viral videos rather than scripts. "What goes viral once will go viral again," especially with a twist or a different face.
- Give creative control and freedom. He hires talent and lets them run. The only asks are one TikTok a day and consistency.
- Communicate by iMessage, not Discord or Slack. Creators are part-time people with other jobs and brands. His VA texts every creator each morning ("good morning, we're excited for you, send us a link once it's live").
- Build community: monthly Google Meet or Zoom with bonuses for most-viewed, best-performing and funniest video. When a video goes viral, it's sent to everyone and everyone remakes it, so information isn't siloed.

## The "one-two hook" content strategy

- **Hook one:** every video is optimized for virality and brand awareness. It's general, not an in-depth product pitch, and the product appears but isn't the headline. The goal is as many eyeballs as possible. Early on he borrowed concepts from competitors and tangential spaces, since things that went viral will go viral again.
- **Hook two:** once a video hits, make follow-up videos that reply to comments on it. This uses TikTok's reply-to-comment-with-video feature, which puts the comment in the new video. TikTok retargets viewers of the original viral video. These follow-ups push the product, the website, FOMO/urgency and objection handling.
- All creators shoot for virality first, then convert that attention.
- Example winning format from Key: "finding Tabs in my Airbnb." It was milked with other creators ("finding Tabs in my sister's boyfriend's room," "in the school parking lot"), then new angles were needed as it saturated. Oliver says the creative innovation came from creators; his role is building the systems to bring on more of them.
- He doesn't point creators to the website in the video itself. Brand and product mention is enough in hook one.
- Team: a lean, contractor-based team. An operations lead handles day-to-day, VAs check in, and nothing is full-time. Agencies or specialists handle Instagram meme media buying, email, conversion rate optimization and Google ads.
- If TikTok Shop were available to him, he'd bolt it on, since TikTok is heavily pushing shop-enabled videos (some creators list a 99-cent product to game it). He can't use it because of his category's restrictions.
- Alternative to spray-and-shoot: focus on one channel with a full-time person. He names Midday Squares (a TV-show-style behind-the-scenes account) and Mini Katana (high-production stunts, like catching a bullet with a sword). He prefers spray-and-shoot because it mitigates risk when pages in his space get banned or shadowbanned often, but says to evaluate both.

## Giving winning content a second life: meme pages

- He takes his most validated TikTok creatives, shrinks them onto a white background, adds a caption, and pays Instagram and Twitter meme pages to post them.
- Theme networks reach hundreds of millions of followers and billions of impressions a month. Proven-viral content plus built-in distribution gives another viral round with no new production.
- You need an inside man or agency with direct relationships, because the space is run by 16- to 22-year-olds in Telegram groups. His media buyers run their own pages of roughly 3 to 4 million followers.

## Syndicating content with a Discord of reposters

- His rule: if it went viral on TikTok, it will likely go viral on Instagram Reels, Facebook Reels, YouTube Shorts and Pinterest idea pins.
- First attempt: a VA opened about 10 accounts per platform and posted manually. He also tried Repurpose.io. It worked somewhat but didn't hit the scale he wanted. "I didn't want to put 10 reposts a day up, I wanted to put 10,000."
- He built a Discord of kids who want to make money online. He coaches them to open pages with names like "tabs chocolate 1" or "the viral chocolates," gives them photos and captions for the feed plus the viral creative library, and asks for one short-form post a day.
- Results to date: over 7,000 members, over $172,000 paid out, and viral reposts at 11 million, 9 million and 7 million views.
- Pay: a percentage of revenue they drive, tracked via UTM link and promo code in Social Snowball.
- Gamification:
  - Daily: everyone who posts and reacts goes on a wheel, and five random people win money.
  - 30 straight days of posting unlocks a salary.
  - A community feel, including a gym channel where members share progress selfies.
- He originally wanted members to make original content but says it was too big an ask. Reposts of proven viral videos outperformed their own mediocre content.

## Reusing the content flywheel elsewhere

- The constant stream of content can be clipped into compilations for the website, email and SMS.
- He says you should "never pay a UGC creator again" to make ads, since you already have the footage for paid ads.
- He sees a big opportunity on Amazon. On Shopify you must keep hitting view counts monthly to sustain sales, but on Amazon a product that blows up from this strategy can reach page one or two and get evergreen sales from Amazon's algorithm. He cites a friend, Robert Oliver, doing this and crushing it.
