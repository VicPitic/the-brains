## Starting from a Chrome-extension tinkerer to a 30-person HR-software exit

- Ilias began at about 13, learning graphic design and making Chrome extensions for wikiHow. He helped with around 20,000 articles. One extension showed a sun, cloud, rain or thunderbolt icon depending on how urgent the article-verification queue was.
- Building and hosting those extensions himself taught him domains and websites by around age 15. He later studied software at college, partly for fundamentals self-taught people miss ("how TCP works", servers, Linux, system architecture).
- He freelanced during college, going from logos to web design to front-end development after a UK ticketing client asked him to build his design in HTML. A friend's referral led to a Belgian startup, and then a co-founding role.
- He says curiosity drives it: you want to learn something, Google it, ship it, and the feedback and traffic (for example from a Reddit post) motivate you to do more.
- He thinks seeing other people build in public makes building feel possible, and that the indie-hacker community and Product Hunt make this much easier than it was 10-15 years ago.

## How officient was started: sell the solution before writing code

- Officient was an HR platform that centralized employee data: assets (laptops, keyboards, monitors), documents (passports, IDs, pay slips), holidays and sick days. This information normally sits scattered across Excel and other systems.
- The idea came from a co-founder who was a sales director at a large Gent startup. He had met the problem at every company he sold to, and had already sold the solution to a customer before any code existed or the product was online.
- Development began around February, built straight to what customers had asked for, with no long development cycle. The first modules were things like asset planning.
- Growth: about 30 employees and around 20,000 B2B customers in three years.
- Ilias credits a strong salesperson on the founding team for how smoothly and quickly it grew.

## Why co-founders and a spread-out team beat going solo

- Officient had four co-founders. The first employees came within about half a year (a marketing person and an extra developer), which led to quick growth.
- After a year, Ilias could take a two-week holiday or time off sick and come back to find the company had grown about 4%, added around 10 customers and shipped a big feature without him.
- He describes solo work as constant doubt: is this the right plan, how to split time between marketing, dev, sales, support, cold emails, referral campaigns and design. Without outsourcing, many things get done "a little bit but nothing that happens amazingly well".
- He notes that most big companies that look like one-person stories were co-founded by several people, and that finding co-founders makes things much smoother.
- Officient's founders split roles: two development co-founders, one salesperson, and a product/CEO/finance person. Others covered funding, accounting, hiring, security (important for HR data), product information and customer support.
- He admits he's currently more solo, with one co-founder, and that this is a struggle: the structure that a team enforced is now only in his head instead of written down.
- He respects the solo path (own your ideas, no one to answer to) and notes that Danny recently decided to stop going solo and hire.

## Running an async, documented company

- Officient's Notion was about 200-500 pages, covering HR processes, product processes and ways of working, including what makes a good pull request and good code (for example, return early instead of if/else).
- When someone made a mistake in a PR, a reviewer would link the relevant Notion page in Slack or as a PR comment, so the team learned from the documentation.
- The company was split into four teams (development, customer success, product, sales). Each had its own documentation and a responsible person at the top who set the rules for that area.
- They were pretty async. No meetings without an agenda or plan.
- Slack conventions: emojis to claim and finish tasks (an eye emoji for "I saw it", a checkbox for done), a weekly to-do list in Slack, and Jira (today he'd use Linear) to track work.
- No standups ever. In the early years you just posted the PR link in Slack and @-mentioned the reviewer. Later (around year three) they added a dev/progress channel where people wrote what they were working on that week.
- Sales and customer success ran through a defined flow: inbound call, sign-up as a lead, a salesperson calls, then onboarding (visit the office or online) and customer support.

## Productize an SEO and marketing service

- Ilias learned marketing in officient's second year, when he left the dev team for a while. He learned landing pages, search, Ahrefs and Semrush, optimized ads, and studied AdWords and Facebook ads.
- He believes technical people have an advantage in marketing, because it's full of technical work: Facebook pixels, conversion tracking, retargeting funnels, Zapier flows into email sequences, keyword research and backlinks, or a Next.js blog instead of just WordPress.
- His advice for launching a service like his agency: have a strict, clear offer for a set price. Example: for $1,000 per month, 3-5 blog posts a month, on-page SEO (titles, descriptions, image alt tags, internal links), then off-page link building, and improved Google Business Profile and Google Maps presence.
- Without a defined structure, work becomes random, such as a few blog posts here and there. The client then asks "what are you actually doing for us?" and leaves because they don't see an outcome.
- Spell out the expected outcome: for example 60 quality posts over a year, 5-10 links per month, a weekly or monthly strategy check, and a target such as being top 10 for a keyword. Then each month sells the next step ("five more posts, ten more links").
- Make the onboarding and expected result clear so the client can say why they're paying you.

## Getting the first clients

- He says to pick one acquisition method that works for you.
- Step one: ask people you already know whether they need the service (a hot email or call), and ask for referrals.
- Post a lot of free, useful content on Twitter, such as how to find keywords or a secret way to find backlinks, so people see you as an expert.
- Cold email works if it's very specific to a niche. At officient, which targeted HR companies and startups with roughly 10-50 employees, they found a list in the local area and sent a personalized video, with the prospect's name on the thumbnail, attached to the email.
- Automation idea for an SEO agency: find a list of websites in a niche, run them through Hunter for emails and names, and use a screenshot API to capture each site. Then email a screenshot of their site, three keywords they currently rank for, and how you could improve those rankings. He says that beats a generic "we offer marketing services" email, since prospects get around 50 of those per month.
- He mentions Lemlist as a tool that can personalize emails with custom text and images.

## Plan, write down processes and set expected outcomes

- His main advice is to brain-dump everything onto one page: how you get customers, onboarding, the emails you send, the benefit you provide, future plans, growth targets for the year and coming months, how revenue turns into hires. Then structure and systemize it over time.
- He says this gives much better results than "messing around trying things in the arena", and compares it to programming before programming: you spell out the algorithm in words or flowcharts, and then it's easy to automate.
- At officient, every experiment started with an expected outcome. For example, if they added another email to a sequence, they checked whether it actually improved anything. Without an expected result written beforehand, you can't know whether you succeeded.
- Recommended books: Alex Hormozi's "$100M Offers" and "$100M Leads" for building a good offer and getting leads to it, and "The E-Myth" for standard operating procedures.
