## Marketing budgets should work like a financial portfolio, not a series of campaigns

Cody argues digital marketing should not run on campaigns. A campaign is a military idea with an objective, an initiative and a timeline, borrowed by the "Mad Men of Madison Avenue" from their time in the service. It made sense when firms had to send one message to as many people as possible and needed timelines to measure success or failure and to limit ad fatigue.

- Digital gives instant feedback loops, so it is "insane to fix things that aren't broken."
- He wants start and end dates eliminated.
- "If your agency is flighting digital ads you should fire them." Flighting means they are not learning and getting better with every growth cycle.
- Agencies that assign arbitrary budget amounts to communication goals and start from zero each year are running a structure designed for an 80-year-old constraint set.

## The financial life analogy: stop restarting from zero every year

Cody compares the campaign model to a person who, every year, burns their savings, quits their job, bombs their house and starts each January homeless. Nobody would run their financial life that way, so he asks why an agency would do it to a marketing budget.

- A financial portfolio holds both short-term and long-term initiatives, and so should a marketing budget.
- Funds in a portfolio are distributed by age, income level and appetite for risk.
- He says more important than short- versus long-term thinking is doing things that compound in value.
- The goal is marketing initiatives built into portfolios that mitigate risk and grow in value over time.

## Three marketing resources and risk tolerance by digital maturity

Cody names three types of marketing resources: money, time and mental energy. Each should be allocated along a spectrum of short-term and long-term needs, and each organization should have a different portfolio composition.

- Companies early in adopting digital have less to lose, so they should take on higher risk.
- Companies further along in their digital evolution have more to lose, so they should have lower risk tolerance.
- Example he cites: Fortune 500 companies that launched new websites and wiped out organic traffic by not understanding SEO fundamentals and erasing content that was ranking. In one case, about half of organic traffic was lost in under 14 days because the IT team deleted the site map, unpublished content and changed the XML structure.

## What goes in the portfolio and the job each channel does

Cody's example portfolio splits budget across search ads, display ads, content production, email marketing and SEO. The pieces are separate but work together as one unit.

- **Search ads:** target people who are buyer-ready and closest to a purchase decision, so the goal is getting them to choose you.
- **Display ads:** educate the target segment about the brand. They reach people still in discovery who do not know the product exists. This changes if display is used for remarketing.
- **Content production:** "the foundation of an organization." It educates customers and grows what he calls digital gravity.
- **Email marketing:** both short- and long-term. It nurtures a lead close to buying, and the CRM list is an asset that gains value over time.
- **SEO:** the long-term investment, comparable to the 10% of yearly income that goes into accounts with compounded growth.
- Print is not excluded on principle and makes sense for some clients, but for many it is more cost-effective to put resources into digital, where it is easier to track what works.

## Operating rule: no end dates, do more of what works

The campaigns in the portfolio have no end dates. The decision rule is simple:

- If something isn't working, stop it.
- If it is working, do more of it.
- Don't flight ads and don't do unnecessary work; shift effort toward what works and away from what doesn't.

He calls the idea "so simple that it's deceiving how powerful it is," and says that done right it makes massive impacts for organizations.
