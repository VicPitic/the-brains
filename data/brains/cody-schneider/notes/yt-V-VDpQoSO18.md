## AI UGC ad and organic social content as an agency opportunity

- Cody calls AI content the biggest money opportunity right now and says "you could get an agency to like 40K a month in like 90 days doing this."
- Traditional UGC ads for Meta/social cost about $80-$120 per video from a human creator (e.g. on Billo). Testing 100 scripts that way is about $8K, which is why it was never economic.
- His method: write many hooks, bodies and calls to action for one value prop, then render them with HeyGen's built-in UGC avatars and ElevenLabs' voice library.
- Example: a test for an AI audio-journaling app (Record Book AI, co-built with Casper Studios) used a "New Year's resolution to journal more, but writing is hard" script. He reports about 15 cent CPCs with US targeting and roughly $2.50 leads, with a goal of getting leads to around $1.
- Playbook: generate about 100 AI variations, find the winners, then give the winning scripts to a real human creator to do their own version. He expects CPC to fall further once a human delivers them.
- Agency packaging he suggests: about $3K/month to run Facebook ads plus unlimited AI UGC avatar creation and constant testing of around 100 creatives.
- Imperfect avatars still work: "it doesn't have to be perfect... it just has to work." The best avatar he used even had visible acne.
- Organic version: build about 20 TikTok accounts, write 1,000 scripts, have avatars speak them, and schedule the shorts across the accounts. Even at 1,000 views each, he puts the effective CPM near $1.
- Pricing for organic: $3-5K/month for X accounts and X pieces of creative. DTC ecom brands are looking to buy this.
- Story format: a simple narrative where the product is the hero ("I had this problem, found this thing, it helped"), often with no hard call to action.
- LinkedIn video: he gets inbound from founders and brands wanting it. He suggests about $5K/month for 30 videos scheduled to a business or founder profile.
- Mindset: "it's about I test the variations at a scale that I couldn't previously."

## SEO as TikTok and Instagram search, plus ecom bottom-of-funnel SEO

- Cody is testing social as the next search engine, since Gen Z goes straight to TikTok or Instagram for queries like "cool bars near me."
- Experiment with a friend who runs TikTok meme pages: use AI avatars across multiple accounts, each speaking different scripts aimed at one target keyword (e.g. "AI essay writer"), to own the front page for that term.
- Open questions he flags (still early): whether volume is what matters, and putting the keyword in the title and the first ~10 seconds of the video.
- Listicle videos work well, e.g. "top five bars in Echo Park this month," with the client placed at number one.
- Freshness matters: he has seen pages hold page-one for about 30-45 days then drop off, so content must be refreshed. He notes this makes a good low-churn agency business.
- Ecom SEO: he says there is more bottom-of-funnel SEO opportunity than ever. His new company, LandingCAD (landingcad.com), ingests a product catalog, finds every long-tail keyword related to the brand, and builds collection pages and blog posts programmatically.
- Typical ecom agencies charge about $45K over six months for roughly eight blog posts that do nothing, so brands are jaded. His pitch is being ROI positive within 30 days.
- For small catalogs (1-10 SKUs), he does "best product for X / for Y / for Z" variations, with dashboards showing revenue impact and Klaviyo pipeline from those pages.

## Programmatic SEO workflow, topical relevance and conversion tactics

- On the HubSpot traffic drop, he argues the pages hit were generic business terms not core to its product. Real companies ranking for relevant topics is what holds.
- For SaaS, integration pages work well. Example: "how to X with HubSpot" tutorials if your software integrates with HubSpot are a natural vehicle for signups. For ecom, "best collagen for X" works for a collagen brand because it is topical.
- Content workflow: find long-tail keywords related to the company, scrape what ranks on page one, put that in the context window, and use the strongest model to draft the article.
- Optionally use an NLP model to pull the keywords that should be included, to get a strong Surfer SEO or Phrase score.
- After it ranks, have a human polish it. He frames two audiences: the Google robots, and human dwell time on the page.
- Comparison and alternative pages: do "X vs Y" and alternatives pages programmatically. Comparison intent is "the perfect search intent for your software."
- Conversion layout for blog posts: a call to action after the first paragraph and every other screen, plus a popup at 20% scroll rate. He says clients see "five to 10% signup rates" when the offer is self-service.
- Why founders say SEO doesn't work: they rank for unrelated terms, and the call to action sits at the bottom of a 2,000-word article where only about 1% of readers scroll. Fixing both produced signups "overnight."

## Directories built with AI scraping

- Cody is "obsessed" with YouTuber Frey Chu's directory case study (a dog park directory).
- His insight: directories used to be hard because of data organization. With AI agents and scraping, unstructured data can be turned into structured data at a scale that wasn't previously possible.
- Example method: scrape Google Maps data, use reviews as source text, add yes/no columns (does it have benches, shade, etc.), and query the review data to fill them.
- He is considering spinning out a directory arm at his agency. He notes link building for niche directories is easy and they can rank quickly.

## Podcast SEO and the AI podcast flywheel

- He says he could make any B2B founder "famous" to their audience with a podcast.
- Podcast SEO is simple, with four factors: keywords in the show title, in the show description, in the show notes, and getting downloads.
- Method: name the show with a broad long-tail B2B phrase (e.g. "B2B Marketing Podcast"). He ranks for "growth" on Apple and Spotify by keyword stuffing and getting downloads.
- A content shortcut he describes (idea he shared but doesn't plan to run): use Google's deep research agent to gather everything on a topic (he tested it on Canva's SEO case studies), feed the source text into NotebookLM to generate a two-host podcast.
- Download flywheel: run cheap Twitter traffic with global targeting to the podcast URL, which lifts chart rank, which brings discovery, which brings more downloads.
- He knows people running about 100 podcasts at once, one per long-tail keyword, with roughly 10-minute episodes, mostly affiliate marketers.
- His own background: starting in 2012 with Amazon affiliate sites (around 20 sites). He says growth marketers come either from degenerate SEO/affiliate or from product, and end up in the same place.

## Picking app ideas: small scope, proven categories, undercut on price

- Cody's focus is products with a very small scope, in spaces already proven at a high price point that he can undercut. He says many teams fail by trying to build a whole platform.
- Distribution: free views on TikTok for good content. He says average paid TikTok CPM is about $8-9, while paying Clippers (college editors, often through Discord networks) runs about $1 per 1,000 views, with bounty-style bonuses.
- Coaching apps: replace a formerly expensive human service with an AI version. Example: a running coach app that plugs into Strava for about $29/month (or $7.99/week) versus a human coach at roughly "400" a month (he says the figure loosely). Even at 80% as good it is competitive and an impulse purchase.
- He says the most effective form adds a voice agent that keeps context. He cites Bardy (an AI business coach with voice) as having raised $8M. Nutrition coach AI is another idea.
- Skip "AI therapist": "nobody wants an AI therapist"; people want a coaching mechanism toward a goal.
- Trackers: he praises retention from trackers, citing a friend's app, Sunflower Sober, which he says has among the fastest daily active user growth he has seen. The friend came up in the era of free Facebook traffic.
- Lock-in: data lock-in matters. In Record Book AI, 365 journal entries plus insights create switching costs. He contrasts this with API-wrapper clones.

## Exact-match domains, tool pages and spinning out features

- Exact-match domain play: buy a domain matching a long-tail tool keyword. He bought aiinfographicgenerator.com for $10, with about 10,000 monthly searches. The tool ingests a blog post URL and outputs a downloadable, embeddable PDF.
- He says exact-match domains are easy to rank, then a "link magnet flywheel" compounds. A real tool people use after a Google search also reads as a legitimate product, so the site is less likely to get hit as a niche site.
- Tool pages for clients: for a construction company he built a remodel cost calculator, embedded an AI-generated app on the page, built links to it, and ranked page one.
- Build out the rest of the site around the head keyword with long-tail blog pages (e.g. "infographic template 4"), which feed users into the tool. Display ads such as AdSense are another option.
- Spinning out features: customers of his transcription product (Swell AI) said the transcription was the best they had tried and all they needed was transcription. He bought an exact-match domain with about 22,000 monthly searches, will copy that feature's code into a new brand and price it separately.
- General rule: build one product, find the killer feature, spin it out as its own standalone.

## B2B vs B2C, and building then selling small software businesses

- Cody advises that if you aren't great at marketing, there is "zero percent chance" of making it in B2C, where you compete against the most aggressive marketers. B2B buyers are less price sensitive.
- Audience pricing example: podcasters have little money, YouTubers have more, and webinar hosts have more still. He chose webinar hosts for Swell AI because of that.
- Even so, he is building B2C Record Book AI, and says a modest outcome (about $20K MRR, sold for around $1M) would be fine.
- Quick-flip pattern he describes: build a site to about $2K MRR in a three-month sprint, sell it on MicroAcquire for about $100K (he cites up to a 50x multiple with no employees), and repeat a few times a year. Keep the ones that keep growing.
- B2B tip: do consulting for a specific group, find a shared pain point, build software that automates it, then offer it as software (e.g. a $1,500 custom job becomes $99/month).
- App stores give a sticky, shareable base if ASO, TikTok and SEO start working.

## Cold email setup that Cody sees working

- Infrastructure: he uses Mailreef (spins up a dedicated AWS server for cold email). About $400/month sends 50,000 emails/month, and he says a million sends a month costs about $8K. He also mentions Hypertide and Thor's Hammer as similar tools.
- He reports deliverability on par with Gmail or Microsoft inboxes.
- Setup: buy about 30 domains on Porkbun, create 5 inboxes per domain, and send 20-30 cold emails per inbox per day.
- Lists: scrape Apollo using one of the Apollo scraping companies, then validate emails.
- Email format: short, about the length of a tweet. First line sets context ("saw you host webinars"), second line gives social proof ("we helped X company do Y in Z time"), then a hard offer or call to action ("want us to do this for you?").
- Female sender names: he says they tested and worked "100%." He knows a company running about 25 fictional female sales reps on LinkedIn, run by an AI agent, that is "absolutely crushing it."
