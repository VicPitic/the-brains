## Digital gravity: why Cody replaces the marketing funnel

- Cody argues the funnel is "the entirely wrong way to think about marketing." People don't enter at the top and exit as buyers. They interact with a brand, drift away, get pulled back, do "three rotations," then touch down and buy.
- His replacement is "digital gravity": building mass on the internet through content and marketing everywhere the customer shows up. More mass means more influence in the niche, and more passers-by get pulled into orbit.
- Touchdowns turn into revenue. Revenue is reinvested into more mass, which creates a growth flywheel.
- He says he used the same approach for a musician (helping get him signed to Sony) and at Rupa Health (Employee 6, multi-hundred-million-dollar exit).
- Long purchase cycles make the funnel picture worse. When he did marketing for doors and windows, a hotel group buying 2,000 shower bases decided over about five years.
- He says "campaign" is a World War II hangover: a campaign starts and ends. His view is "you're not doing marketing, you're marketing," continuously, like an app or website that is never "done."
- Email is the one channel that can't be taken back after sending. His team's rule: three people must proof the copy before any send.

## Resources by stage: where to market

- Funded companies have time, money and resources, so the answer to "where should I market?" is "everywhere."
- Bootstrapped founders usually have time and mental energy but no money. Their resources shift as the company scales.
- Cody's volume rule: marketing is a surface-area game. He cites a TikTok Shop agency owner whose best affiliates post about 10x more videos than low performers.
- His batting-average analogy: a .300 hitter who swings once rarely touches the ball, but swinging 10 times gets them on base.
- He calls "one Instagram account" and "a blog post a week" old-school thinking.
- For founders who have raised money, he says making yourself famous is "the game you're playing." It makes the next round easier. At Rupa, growing the CEO's and the company's Instagram brought healthtech investors inbounding to ask how they did it.

## B2B playbook, layer 1: scraped list, matched ads, cold email

- Start with a small target audience, often around 50,000 people. Scrape every email. Apollo.io is about $99/month for roughly 10,000 emails, with industry filters. LinkedIn also works.
- Upload the list as a custom match audience to Google Ads, Facebook Ads, LinkedIn Ads and wherever else they spend time. On Facebook he expects about 30 to 40% of emails to match. Ads then run indefinitely in the background.
- Ad format: UGC-style AI avatar ads aimed at the pain points. Tools: HeyGen for the avatar and ElevenLabs for the voice. The founder makes a digital clone of themselves, because "people buy from people, not brands."
- The ad story is a hero's journey: "I had this problem, I went on a discovery journey, and I ended up here, better."
- Test about 100 variations (hooks, settings, backgrounds, pain points). Put 80% of spend on the best performer by lowest CPC and keep 20% always testing. Promote any new winner into the 80% campaign.
- Cold email the same list at the same time, using the same problem-solution angle as the ads.
- Infrastructure he describes:
  - Mailreef, a server for managing sending inboxes, about $400/month for 50,000 cold emails.
  - Smartlead or Instantly, about $89/month, as the sending software.
  - Around 30 domains with 5 inboxes each, all sending under the same name in variations.
- He separates three email types and keeps them on separate domains:
  - Transactional: send from the core app domain.
  - Newsletter: send from its own domain.
  - Cold: send from throwaway domains. Founders often cold email from the core domain, then land transactional mail like password resets in spam.
- Both channels reinforce each other. Prospects often see the ads months later and search their inbox for the original cold email, then come back as a lead.
- He rejects the fear of "burning the audience": "they don't care about you at all," and "cold email doesn't work" is wrong when messaging is specific. Example: dock management software for shipping yards. The message named a daily pain (dock check-in) and one in five recipients said yes.

## B2B playbook, layer 2: build a media arm

- His core idea is to layer growth curves. After ads and cold email, add a podcast and newsletter to the same list, then clip the podcast into shorts for LinkedIn and Instagram.
- Content source: record customer conversations in a slightly more formal setting. They already contain the industry's pain points.
- Newsletter format: import about 200 contacts a day. Subject line is "podcast + episode name." Body is "Hey [name], the newest episode of X is live," then a one-paragraph summary and five key takeaways.
- Tools he names: MailerLite and Beehiiv (he has also used others). Because the content is industry-specific, it reads as educational and is "way more approachable" than a transactional cold email.
- Rupa Health results:
  - Podcast reached 180,000 downloads/month after 6 months and ranked top 20 in US medical podcasts.
  - Big labs that would not take their calls started inbounding. Cody used the media placements as leverage to negotiate down the fee percentages Rupa paid.
  - They sold ad placements in the podcast and newsletter to health brands. Athletic Greens subsidized the entire growth team's cost. He calls this "negative CAC," turning a cost center into a profit center.
- He says every B2B company should build a media business. Examples he gives: Arrow Electronics (about $6 billion market cap, with a large media arm), Red Bull, and Y Combinator, which he describes as a media company selling venture capital.
- Repurposing: his product Swell AI takes the long recording and produces clips, show notes, blog posts and white papers, which are then scheduled across every platform. This replaces what used to take a team of five.
- A newsletter case: an 18-month client in life sciences has a 130,000-person list. Each send drives about 10,000 clicks, sent twice a week, so roughly 80,000 clicks a month (rounded to 100,000), at about $450. He says that audience pays about $5 a click elsewhere.
- A podcast lead from a Fortune 500 company led to a $2.2M proposal, which they accepted.
- The first 90 days of a podcast show little, and "you're just trusting the process."

## Social and Instagram follower ads

- Short-form content from TikTok or Reels posted on LinkedIn can go viral right now.
- Instagram-only follower ads: run for Impressions, with a call to action in the caption to follow the account for more content like this. Cody says this grew a musician's Instagram at around $0.30 per follower, and grew Rupa's account to 100,000 followers using the same method.
- Reason: the closer an ad is to native content, the less you pay for it. That is why he tests 100 variations, to find content that doesn't feel like an ad but still produces revenue.
- Larger accounts also earn free impressions.
- Categories are mental real estate. People hold only two or three brands per category. HubSpot's "inbound marketing" is his example. He advises attaching to an idea people already hold and evolving it, because creating a new category costs too much.

## Consumer playbook: huge TAM and impulse pricing

- Cody's warning: don't start in consumer. "You have to be unbelievable at marketing to win," it's far more competitive, but winning there is the best outcome.
- It requires a huge total addressable market, because content has to be broad enough to reach a large share of the population. His example is a women's health app where about one in three women have the problem. Ebook downloads there cost about $0.40, then he upsells into the product. Ebooks can be written with AI and published with Gamma.
- Pricing tactic: charge $7.99 a week (about $29 a month). It sells as an impulse purchase and turns into roughly three months of revenue. A $10 CAC then yields a roughly 10x payout over the customer lifetime.
- Paid acquisition: Facebook and TikTok ads optimized for the signup or paid action via deep link. Run many variations.
- UGC creators: about $1,000/month per creator for a video a day, cross-posted across their accounts. They act as a test bed for what goes viral. Format: hook, pain point, then the product as hero. The goal is an ad that feels like entertainment plus education.
- Remix the winners: spin up about 10 TikTok accounts, use ChatGPT to rewrite scripts, HeyGen for the avatar, ElevenLabs for audio, and CapCut to remove the green-screen background and put the app behind the avatar.
- Pick a unique name nobody else uses so you don't compete with similarly named companies on Google. Many viewers Google the product after seeing a video.
- Pixel every site visitor (Facebook, TikTok, Google) to retarget indefinitely, add an email capture for a nurture sequence, and use customer-match lists.

## Influencer arbitrage and programmatic SEO

- Influencer outreach at scale:
  - Search YouTube by category keyword, scrape creator emails from their bios, and cold email them for pricing. Do the same on Instagram with a tool called IG leads, plus TikTok.
  - Roughly 10 to 20% reply with pricing. Build a table of price versus average views to find mispriced creators, since it is an imperfect market.
  - Deal structure that works for him: a 3-video package (for example $1,500) plus an affiliate commission, with about 30% mentioned and recurring payouts. Three videos build enough trust, and the recurring commission creates a moat, since competitors would have to buy creators out of the contract.
- Benchmark: Cal AI and Rizz GPT founders told him they pay about $5 CPM, versus roughly $10 average TikTok CPM when buying through ads.
- Programmatic SEO (Cal AI example: diet plans, nutrition plans, weight loss, calorie counts):
  - It works once the site has domain authority around 40 or higher, built from backlinks, so you can rank for low-difficulty long-tail keywords.
  - Process: scrape what ranks on page one for the target keyword, feed it into a context window, create an outline, then write the post section by section (he finds this gives the best quality and readability).
  - Publish at scale (he mentions 10,000 articles). When a page gets its first session, a human edits it. His team has an SEO/content specialist whose job is rewriting anything humans have seen.
  - Conversion: a CTA after the first paragraph, one call-out in every scroll frame, and a full-screen popup at 25 to 50% scroll, plus pixel everyone for retargeting.
- He also mentions a sober-tracking app, Sunflower Sober (with an AI "sponsor"), growing quickly through Facebook ads at a sensible price.
