## Why Cody built an AI content engine: owned media, social discovery, and the erosion of search

- Cody started in e-commerce (print-on-demand), then worked at a B2B agency doing digital strategy for building-materials manufacturers (windows, wood paneling, shower bases). That taught him marketing when purchase decisions have a very long time horizon.
- At Rupa Health he joined as person six and ran content from zero. He says the company went from a $20 million to a $120 million valuation in about six months, by building owned media channels with their own distribution.
- He describes three trends behind Swell AI: search eroding as the default way to choose vendors; buyers knowing more than the salesperson; and social becoming the place discovery starts.
- The agency lesson: the biggest customer feedback was that buyers "knew more than the salesperson" about the product. His response was to create owned media channels to cut through the noise.
- His thesis for Rupa, which marketed to physicians (high purchasing power, hard to reach): start from what the audience wants to hear (other practitioners talking about their businesses and research), then record those conversations and publish them.
- At peak the Rupa content team was about 25 people including freelancers and writers. Content operations became a headache to manage, which is what pushed him toward AI automation.

## The content engine: one long-form interview feeds every channel

- Cody's core workflow: publish a long-form podcast weekly, chop it into clips, and schedule the clips to social platforms daily. That covers every place people discover a brand.
- Younger people increasingly begin discovery on Instagram, LinkedIn and TikTok, using social as a search engine for awareness content, and may then go deeper into blog posts. He expects this to flow up the age ladder, as Facebook did from colleges to "my grandma".
- Rupa repurposed podcasts and live classes into clips, blog posts, newsletters and white papers.
- Swell AI's first version was a Google Drive folder: customers uploaded MP3s, Cody ran the software locally and manually emailed back show notes and blog posts. It started with podcasters because friends already used podcasts to grow their businesses.
- Outputs Swell templates can produce: show notes, newsletters, 2,000-word blog posts, keyword identification, takeaway recaps, course structuring (coaches drop in a two-hour recording and ask for 5-10 minute sections), and email copy for podcasters to paste into their ESP.

## Why social beats search for trust

- Cody argues there is distrust of content "that doesn't have a face to it". Incentives are aligned on social: if a creator recommends a bad bar, people stop trusting that creator. Websites face no repercussion for bad information; they mostly just want a sign-up.
- Test he uses: ask someone to name the author of the last article they read from Google; most can't.
- He still runs programmatic SEO as one of Swell's marketing strategies. He does not say to abandon search, only to shift more resources to social because that is where people increasingly begin.
- AI-written SEO content has flooded results. He cites Google's recent content quality update, after which hundreds of sites dropped to near zero overnight while Reddit and Quora (user-generated forums) benefited.
- Example for B2B: if the ICP is interior designers, a founder who hosts a weekly podcast interviewing interior designers about their careers and mistakes, emailed to them each week, builds large trust.
- Warm audiences improve outbound: he says open and response rates on cold emails rise when prospects have already been receiving your content, so a BDR reaching out lands on a warm lead.

## Becoming a media company: results and examples

- Cody's biotech client (about 20 people): built an email list of biotech founders in the US and started a weekly podcast about 12 months earlier. Result: nearly everyone in the industry listens weekly, investors send inbound for their portfolio companies, warm intros arrive from people wanting to be guests, and ICPs he says could not be reached by cold email now come inbound.
- The same media asset gave the small company leverage in negotiating with large organizations such as Roche and Genentech.
- Marketing becomes a profit center: at Rupa they built a top-20 medical podcast in about six months, reaching about 150,000 downloads a month. They sold ad space to Athletic Greens, and that deal paid the entire salary of the growth department.
- Long-standing examples he points to:
  - Arrow Electronics (a $6 billion market cap component reseller): selling a commodity, so differentiation comes from content.
  - Red Bull: "selling sugar water" but built one of the coolest brands through content, including its F1 team sponsorship. He says its media house makes more money than product sales.
  - Refine Labs / Chris Walker: a weekly podcast pair (one interview show, one solo deep dive) chopped into clips and distributed on social; he has done it for 10 years.
  - Comedians: sit down for conversations, chop clips, distribute across channels.
- He calls this "still a blue ocean". He and his team only stumbled on the process in 2020; AI tools now let it scale for the first time.

## How to adopt AI in marketing without a company overhaul

- Cody pushes back on "AI transformation" framing. His method: find 5% of your current process to automate, then 10%, 15%, 30%, 60%, until about 80% is automated, without changing company structure or workflow.
- He says companies that swing to the overhaul end of the spectrum tend to fail at implementation.
- The team's role shifts from "grunt labor" to curator, focused on high-touch work humans do better.
- Claim: a team of five "10x marketers" given these tools becomes "100x marketers".
- Example: a team writing two articles a week can write 20 a week because the first draft takes about two minutes from source material. Saved templates also lock in brand voice, which makes output more consistent.
- Swell's "text sources" feature lets a team supply a raw unstructured text file, extract insights, write a LinkedIn post per insight in the brand's tone, and save the sequence as a reusable template.

## How Swell AI works under the hood

- Transcription: audio is chopped into pieces and transcribed in parallel, then stitched together, so a 60-minute episode doesn't need 60 minutes of compute. The transcript then acts as a "walled garden" the AI writes from.
- Clip identification mimics a human editor: find the core ideas in a 45-minute episode, extract the core insight for each (using "entity identification"), write a roughly 10-word hook for it, then find a roughly 30-second clip that supports the hook.
- Product progression: MP3 first, then MP4 (video clips), then text sources. Recent additions: auto-cropping, and on-brand caption templates with overlays.

## Content library as the differentiator, books from interviews, and the roadmap

- Cody's view: building products and doing distribution are both getting easier, so the differentiator is a content library only you have (industry experts, proprietary data).
- Vision: query your full back catalog (white papers, internal docs, hundreds of episodes), use results as source documents, and repurpose into a LinkedIn post or other asset. Swell would also pull trend data from TikTok and YouTube Shorts APIs and surface clip and content ideas, like a "Pinterest" of your own library.
- Book workflow: a customer with about 150 episodes consolidated them into categories and is producing about 20 micro-chapters. He uses a template with his tone, style and voice plus the whole-book outline, then combines sections in post. The goal is a first draft to the publisher to get the advance. Cody compares it to how "How to Win Friends and Influence People" was written (five journalists interviewing 300 people over about a year and a half), with the first research pass automated.
- As model token limits grow, an entire back catalog could go into one source document: generate an outline, edit it, then draft the book.
- Roadmap: send clips straight from Swell to scheduling (starting with YouTube Shorts), then pull social performance data to find best performers and suggest similar content from the library (about a year out), plus SMS and email management. The aim is one place for source content, repurposing, distribution and analytics, in service of testing, doing less of what doesn't work, and more of what does.
