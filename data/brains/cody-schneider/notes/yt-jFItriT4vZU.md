## Growth flywheels: layer channels in sequence

- Cody's growth model at Rupa Health (a three-sided marketplace of specialty labs, practitioners and patients) was layering flywheels. Find a channel, test about five things in it, and put all mental energy, money and resources behind the one that shows traction.
- When the growth curve hits its inflection point (the S-curve ceiling), he layers on the next channel. That is how he gets "this compounding, hockey stick style thing."
- Results at Rupa as first growth hire:
  - Signups went from about 30 a month to 280 in five months.
  - A podcast went from zero to a top 20 medical podcast in six months.
  - An email list went from zero to 400,000 subscribers in about a year.
  - A weekly Wednesday webinar drew about 5,000 viewers.
- Physicians are constantly sold to, and inventory to reach wealthier audiences is expensive. So Cody made content from day one. He found physicians care about patient outcomes, not business, and built content around that.

## Early-stage rule: transactional marketing first, long-term bets later

- Early on, Cody focuses on marketing that makes an action happen tomorrow: revenue tomorrow, not six months from now.
- He sees first-time founders start SEO and other long-term investments in month one. His view is that this is a mistake if you might not be around when the payoff arrives.
- His early channels are paid ads, cold DMs and cold email. Lock in all three, and one will show more traction.
- Portfolio analogy: a young person spends about 80% of income on surviving and 20% investing; as they mature the split swaps. A company does the same. Only shift to the longer-term portfolio once short-term channels cover the next 12 months.

## Validate before building: landing page plus ads

- Cody tells young founders deciding between ideas: "don't build anything."
- Stand up a landing page (he suggests building it with Claude Code), run ads to it, and see which idea gets the cheapest signups.
- The strongest signal is someone who goes through the flow, starts a free trial, or gives a credit card with no product, only screenshots of what it could be. That is instant validation.

## Facebook ads for B2B: how it worked and how it works in 2025

- **Older approach (Rupa):**
  - Scrape the entire target audience's contact data: directories and all of Google Maps.
  - Use tools like Hunter.io, or bulk data sellers like Targetatron, to buy lists (for example every nurse practitioner in the US).
  - Build a crawler to scrape practitioners' websites for emails, since many used Gmail addresses.
  - Upload the emails as a Facebook customer match list. Only about 30% matched because they were business emails.
  - Use that seed to build lookalike audiences and an exact target cohort.
- **Current approach (as of August 2025):** targeting has improved, so creative is now the filter for finding the audience.
  - Make creative that speaks directly to the segment (for example local business owners) and target all of Facebook.
  - Set conversion events (signup, then payment) and send both back to Facebook.
  - Build about 100 pieces of creative. Run them as CPC campaigns to find the cheapest clicks. Spin the winners (for example five) into a conversion campaign.
  - Typically one to two of the five outperform on the payment event.
- Cody notes people don't use Facebook for B2B and says "please continue not using it," because it leaves him more inventory. He says it "especially right now just prints money."

## Unit economics: it's fine to lose money on the first month

- Back into the numbers: "every dollar that we spend turns into $5 of customer lifetime value," with a payback period of about three months. Then set spend to hit a revenue target.
- Real example from a past company:
  - CAC about $89.
  - Average revenue per customer about $39 a month, so payback about two months.
  - Customer lifetime value about $600.
- First-time founders must accept that they lose money on the first transaction. Lifetime value is what matters.
- He cites the "law of shitty clickthroughs" (Andrew Chen, *The Cold Start Problem*): early on CPCs are cheap, and as competitors and incumbents enter, the arbitrage disappears and CAC rises. Product improvement raises LTV over time.
- Some categories won't work for ads. He says Graph will find it hard to do ads, so it needs unusual marketing strategies.

## Underpriced attention: find the arbitrage

- Channel-product fit: for any product at any moment there is a channel with an unexploited arbitrage. Find where people spend time online that is not yet saturated with advertisers, or is hard to market in.
- Early Facebook gave one-cent CPC clicks because of abundant inventory. He says this is how Wish.com became a billion-dollar company: it abused new, underpriced media.
- **Local TV streaming ads (programmatic):**
  - A friend from out-of-home advertising works in this space. Cody has seen data of about 80 cent CPMs on local TV.
  - You can target from a customer match list.
  - Attribution is direct: someone watches a Hulu ad, then searches on the same IP and buys, and it ties back to the household.
- Cumbersome entry processes are a plus. He thinks of them as "illiquid markets."

## Influencer marketing in illiquid markets

- Cody avoids marketplace hubs with posted per-post prices. He wants illiquid markets and negotiates directly.
- Process:
  - Pay roughly $800 to $1,000 per creator, around $900 for 30 posts in one example, and run about five creators at once.
  - Roughly every 10 to 20 posts, an outlier format appears. Hand that format to the other creators to iterate on. This creates a flywheel.
  - Scale up or down with 5, 10 or 20 creators.
  - Cut underperformers aggressively and judge on CPM.
- The arbitrage: average platform CPMs are about $7 on TikTok and about $5.50 on Facebook (as platforms report them). Getting creator CPMs under that, for example around $2 per thousand views, beats paid ads.
- This works because about 10% of posts generate about 80 to 90% of an account's views. You are betting on the outlier, and that is how you get the discounted CPM.

## Building Graph.com: an AI agent for marketing analytics

- Graph aims to replace the data scientist you would hire. A friend hired an ex-Uber data scientist at $200k to answer data questions.
- The problem: getting insight requires a data pipeline, a data warehouse and fluency in tools like Tableau, Looker Studio or Power BI. He says learning those takes months and "you literally have to use the software to get the insight."
- The product:
  - One-click setup for data connectors.
  - Graph manages and continuously optimizes the data warehouse.
  - Chat on the side and a dashboard in the center, for example "build a stacked bar chart showing new users versus total users over time" with a line of best fit, week over week.
- The data warehouse is the hard part. A client had about 25 million rows of Facebook data alone, which hits rate limits if plugged into a generic AI solution.
- The vision: first augment the data person, then replace them. Graph watches your data constantly, researches online, and Slacks you insights and opportunities.
- Finance stack: Stripe Atlas ($500, Delaware C-corp, with perks like instant Carta and Mercury setup) and Fondo for accounting, so 90% of energy goes to building product and selling.
