## Early path into marketing and the lesson from his first company
- Cody started by figuring out how to make money on the internet at 16, selling things through e-commerce, back when free clicks from Facebook pages were available.
- He started a company that took off, dropped out of school and traveled, then watched it implode because he "wasn't building a brand."
- He calls that failure the best thing that could happen early, with low stakes: when building companies, "focus on brand because that's the only thing that actually has value long term."
- He later studied business economics and worked at a B2B marketing agency serving Fortune 500 manufacturers (wood, windows). He designed strategy, built processes, and built the analytics infrastructure so teams had a North Star for what was working.

## Where his specialty sits and how his career moved
- A friend at Rupa/e.l.f.-style beauty growth company showed him a graph of word-of-mouth growth with ads layered on top, compounding about 30% month over month. Cody saw it as blue ocean, moved to San Francisco a week later, and scaled it for about a year.
- He then joined a DeFi savings-account crypto company growing about 3x month over month for four months. The Terra Luna collapse eroded the business model overnight.
- Takeaway he drew: his expertise is companies that already have product-market fit and an audience and need distribution. He went back to consulting for early-stage startups, building initial growth infrastructure (what others call "fractional head of growth," which he calls "dumb"; it is a stopgap to get the first 100 users).
- With co-founder Max he built side projects, then launched Swell AI in September 2022 (AI writer for podcasters). It took off as ChatGPT hit in December 2022 / January 2023.
- Draft Horse AI launched about 45 days before this interview (June 21): an AI SEO engine where you plug in your website and it writes content at scale. Long-term vision: "have your blog grow itself" toward a business goal like signups.

## How he learns marketing and how to start
- He learned from YouTube videos and Reddit threads. He argues digital marketing "can't be taught" in a classroom because it changes so fast; a university program is two years stale.
- "You're either in the pit or you're not": either you do it daily and understand what's going on, or you don't.
- His starter exercise: make a blog about a topic you're interested in, figure out how to get traffic to it, and figure out how to sell something to that traffic. Do that and "you're in the top one percent of marketers."
- Learning loop: follow one topic, note unfamiliar terms in each video, research those, repeat. Document what you learn in public to attract people with the same interests (inbound).
- He talks weekly with growth friends (heads of growth, CMOs), swapping what's working and where the arbitrage is. Example: a friend ran the same strategies across Facebook ads at Deliveroo, Snapchat ads at Depop, and TikTok ads at a new company.

## Niche sites, then and now with AI
- Traditional niche site: rank a site around product searches (e.g. "best binoculars for birding") and monetize traffic via ad networks (AdSense), affiliate commissions (e.g. Amazon, roughly 4-8%), or selling your own products. One friend runs a gardening site with ad income plus a newsletter list and periodic PDF how-to guides.
- Big media agencies also run thousands of niche sites and sell the traffic to consumer packaged goods brands.
- The shift: writing content was the biggest cost. A thousand articles at about $100 each meant roughly $100K for okay-quality writing. With AI, he says a thousand articles through Draft Horse costs about $500.
- In July 2022 AI content became "good enough" to be usable from a marketer's view.
- His workflow: find a niche, pull low-difficulty, low-competition keywords, feed them into Draft Horse, publish 2,000+ word articles (e.g. "how to start a [X] business" variations). Low-competition keywords start ranking immediately. He builds initial sites in about 10 hours.
- Cheap content makes the long tail viable in a way that was previously cost-prohibitive.
- He expects the window to last about 18 to 24 months before it is built into every tool and CMS. He calls it a land grab.

## Differentiating when content costs nothing
- If AI writes the average, experts become more valuable. His view: companies will capture expert knowledge (Fireside chats, podcasts, PDFs), load it into a vector database, and have AI write only from that corpus, so the output is unique because nobody else has the data.
- Roles shift: instead of fresh grads repurposing content, you need a curator who feeds good material in and reviews the output; the middle is automated.
- Stack he uses for this: Pinecone (vector database) and LangChain. The same approach could produce news-style articles by loading 10 sources and writing against them.
- Draft Horse mechanics: it generates a blog post outline (what an SEO manager would hand a writer), then writes to it, tracking what it has written, is writing, and will write, to stay cohesive.
- Roadmap he describes: Search Console data feeding back to Draft Horse to refresh underperforming content, a tracking tag to report signups, automatic internal linking, automatic indexing, and images with exact-match alt text.
- Scale example: on one day he was writing 3,000 articles across three properties (1,000 on marketing for his personal site, 1,000 on WordPress for Draft Horse, 1,000 on podcasting for Swell), live in about two hours.

## Publishing workflow: ship rough, polish what gets traffic
- Upload text first, get it live, then add images, video, CTAs, and rewrites after an article starts getting traffic.
- His reasoning: if nobody sees it, it doesn't matter if it's not perfect. This depends on company stage; startups can tolerate imperfection, while regulated firms like accounting must be more careful.
- Even for a media brand with no software, he would still use AI content at scale. He describes companies producing 10,000+ articles with CTAs to a newsletter or show.
- Marketing split: transactional marketing (paid ads, cold email: act now, results soon) vs long-term investment marketing (SEO: effects show about 12 months later).
- Math he uses: if about $5K buys 10,000 articles and even a fraction rank, you get an inbound engine. Spending 50 cents now to create one free signup within 12 months "pays for itself," so he does it until it stops working.

## Twitter growth system
- In early 2023 he saw an arbitrage: Twitter was giving unusual free reach, like Facebook in 2012. He went in to sell tools through it.
- Results: about 400 followers at the start of 2023 to about 8,000, and 2.2 million impressions per month for the $5/month Twitter Blue fee, which he calls "the cheapest CPM on the internet right now."
- Process: same as all marketing: pick a channel, turn it into a system, read the data, do more of what works.
- Concretely: 10 tweets per day (70 per week), written every Sunday over a glass of whiskey. He reviews the past three months of top tweets by impressions, then writes 70 new ones remixing those ideas. This creates a loop that learns what his audience engages with and feeds more reach.
- Style: bare-bones, just the essence of an idea, documenting what he is doing.
- He is half-automating it with AI and thinks the full loop (write from vector database, publish, read impressions, write more from winners) can be automated, but Draft Horse is the focus.

## Distribution over product and the two moats
- "I see worse products win all the time because they have better distribution." He says product-first founders (especially in SF) assume a great product gets found; good product is just table stakes now.
- With software and content easy to clone, he says only two things create a moat: owned media (an audience nobody else can reach) and proprietary customer data.
- His own apps are Tailwind components plus an OpenAI API wrapper, yet he tweeted that the site is worth more than his townhouse because of distribution.
- He cites the book The Innovation Stack: stacking about 8 to 11 ideas creates something new. Unit economics pitch for Draft Horse: content that cost about $100 before costs about $1 now, "a no-brainer."

## Podcast growth stack: SEO, iOS ads, newsletters, SMS
Used for zero-to-one podcasts (biotech, marketing, insurance examples). Layer four things:
- **Podcast SEO**: keywords in show name, description, episode titles and show notes drive ranking inside Apple Podcasts and Spotify, where he says roughly 90% of downloads happen. Downloads act like domain rating. Rewriting old show notes on a 500-episode show has produced about 30% week-over-week growth in some cases.
- **Facebook ads to iOS-only devices**: use a clip from the show as the ad, test around 20 clips, put spend behind the lowest CPC. Link to the Apple Podcasts episode URL (a deep link that opens the app) so a play becomes a download. He also uses a podcast subscribe deep link ("podcast://" followed by the RSS feed URL) which prompts a subscribe, making each new episode an instant download.
- **Newsletters**: for a biotech client he scraped industry emails, added about 200 per day via automation, and sent the best-performing episode first, then each new episode weekly. Reported results: roughly 40% opens and 5% CTR on these cold emails. He calls it a gray-hat arbitrage that will go away.
- **SMS** (Klaviyo-style bulk texting): he reports about 90% open rates and 40 to 50% clicks on new-episode texts. A friend is testing WhatsApp in Europe.
- Flywheel: more downloads give better podcast SEO and chart position, charts are scraped by aggregators like Chartable straight from Apple categories, and that brings more downloads.

## Growing a newsletter from 33K toward 330K
- Starting point: use paid ads and work the math backwards: if a subscriber costs $1 and the newsletter earns more than that per subscriber over its life, buy them. He notes Sam Parr built The Hustle largely on Facebook ads and sold it for $40M in about two years (his figures as stated).
- A friend gets $1 signups from Facebook using a screenshot of an Apple Note as the ad image for an AI newsletter.
- He suggests pairing paid ads with a newsletter cross-promotion network (a tool he could not name on air; sellers add "newsletters you might like" to their emails). He has seen about 20,000 subscribers added in seven days that way.
- With capital, he would test every channel (likely TikTok for a millennial/Gen Z audience), compare cost per signup, then scale the cheapest.
- Without capital the playbook differs; his advice depends on starting capital.
