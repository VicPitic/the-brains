## Starting a content-led company by turning podcasts into a repeatable sales channel

- Cody's route into this: e-commerce, then a BD marketing agency doing digital strategy for Fortune 500 manufacturers (doors, windows, "very unsexy businesses"), then Rupa Health as employee six.
- At Rupa, he helped take the company from a $20 million to a $110 million valuation in about six months.
- Rupa marketed to practitioners (doctors), who are high income, hard to reach and marketed to from every angle. His team concluded early that they had to build a media arm to create a relationship and drive demand generation.
- That arm became a live class series, a podcast series and thousands of blog posts, which produced strong SEO.
- Lesson he drew: podcasts, especially in B2B, are a way to produce media on a regular cadence that can be chopped into clips, blog posts, newsletters and more.
- Swell AI started in July 2022. Cody was consulting, had spun up a podcast for a client, and saw AI was "good enough". His co-founder Max, already deep in embeddings, built a first version within 24 hours.
- That MVP was a Google Drive folder where friends uploaded MP3s. Cody sold it to about 10 people, ran the files locally on laptops and emailed the generated content back by hand. There was no website; he emailed friends who used webinars and podcasts to grow and said "send us the recordings, we'll handle the rest."

## Bootstrapping and defensibility choices at Swell AI

- Swell is self-funded. Cody owns other companies that cash flow it, and Max had exits that cover him.
- They were approached by VCs early and chose to build "something that's sustainable" and profitable instead.
- His reasoning: something new drops in AI every week, so it was unclear what raising would mean for long-term defensibility.
- Defensibility now comes from a complex workflow product: take raw media, turn it into any form and publish it anywhere.
- Pricing: a free tier, then usage based. Cost is based only on the upload time of the source file (effectively the transcription), with unlimited clips and unlimited text afterwards. He says this works because model costs are now low.
- Positioning: a prosumer product, not a high-end enterprise one. The initial wedge was podcasters; the target is now "your AI content marketing team".
- Customers include local news channels as well as individual creators.

## Why human source material beats prompting an LLM

- Cody's view: an LLM is trained on the average of the internet, so its output is average. Unique ideas still require talking to experts, and "thought leaders are actually now more valuable than they ever were."
- Workflow he describes: record conversations with experts (e.g. five hotel marketing experts), drop the recording in, get a transcript, then prompt for a blog post outline, then a post written from the outline.
- Tell the AI to reuse the vocabulary in the transcript so the tone, style and voice match the recording. He says this lands you in the top ~5% of content versus a bare prompt like "hotel marketing strategies for 2024".
- General principle: give the AI a "walled garden" of source material to work from, which orients its context and raises quality.
- Other source material he cites: PDFs and reports, or scraping everything on page one of Google for a target keyword (blog posts, YouTube videos) into the context window. He says this gives output "10x better" than prompting the LLM alone.
- Experts often dislike writing but are happy to talk, so interviews, fireside chats and webinars all work as sources.
- Cody says the prompt-chain is the real product. He argues "I don't think anybody actually wants to chat with AI". Chat is a sandbox for finding the prompt chain, and the value is in templated, automatic outputs.

## Podcasts as "sales in disguise" for early-stage companies

- Cody's quote: "Podcasts are one of the most effective sales tools that exist currently."
- He advises early-stage companies to create a show.
- Outreach tactic: invite your target ICP onto the show. For clients using Swell, with a 10,000-subscriber newsletter and clips offered back to guests for their socials, he cites a roughly 20% response rate on that outreach.
- Result: you are suddenly in a relationship with your target customer.
- Distribution: email the episodes to a newsletter. People open it and spend an hour with you each week, which he says you could not pay them to do. Industry experts on the show act as "almost this honeypot" that creates inbound.
- Byproducts include clips, social posts, blog posts and newsletters.
- He says video podcasts are taking over and that YouTube looks like the winner, based on the data Swell sees.

## Repurposing webinars into long-tail SEO and clips

- Example: clients with 500+ webinars from the last three years and no plan for them.
- Tactic 1: turn each webinar into blog posts. Prompt for 10 long-tail keywords related to the webinar, write a post per keyword, publish and embed the webinar in each post to drive more views.
- Tactic 2: cut the hour-long webinar into 5-10 minute insight clips for YouTube.
- Cody says the clips often outperform the source: a webinar with 97 views versus a clip with a hook at the start that did close to 1,000 views from the same content.

## AI "employees" and custom models as the next layer

- Cody is focused on automating whole marketing roles. Example under construction: a social media manager agent, internally called "Jenna".
- Its loop: take a long-form upload, make clips, schedule and publish them to social platforms, analyze performance, find more clips like the top performers in the back catalog, and repeat as a growth flywheel.
- He sees the same pattern for Google Ads, Facebook Ads and programmatic SEO (keyword research, write posts, check what drove signups, write more).
- His view: even if an agent is only ~50% as good as a human, at a price like $149 a month it still creates impact, and it improves over time. Agents would "show up in your slack, and they start working."
- Differentiation will come from custom models trained for a specific outcome. Clip selection is his example: general models are trained on average Shorts, TikToks and Reels, whereas training on the top 1% of clips would improve selection.
- Cost: Swell was quoted about $1.2 million to train such a model. He says it was closer to $9-10 million about 18 months earlier, and that on-demand GPU rental (he names sfcompute.com, e.g. 10 H100s for four days) and distributed compute are lowering the barrier.
- Prediction: in a couple of years a model could be trained for roughly $100-150k, creating defensibility for a company.

## Layering every growth lever, including a TikTok account network

- Cody describes his background as "degenerate growth tactics". Swell's mix includes:
  - cold email at close to 100,000 emails a month
  - paid ads across all channels
  - programmatic SEO for inbound traffic
  - a "TikTok cloud network" of about 100 accounts posting daily
- TikTok network mechanics: virtual machines plus proxy networks per account so they are not shadow banned. AI writes scripts, HeyGen avatars read them, background is removed, and a product recording is placed behind the avatar.
- Numbers: each video gets about 600-700 views, which across 100 accounts adds up to about 3.2-3.5 million views a month. He says branded searches for the platform rise over time.
- Principle: "take every growth lever that exists, wherever your target customer is," then layer the strategies on top of each other to "break the laws of physics" in marketing. Be present everywhere with educational content, media content or an offer.

## For You pages make content a numbers game

- Cody's argument: TikTok invented "for you" content that the platform picks for you; YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels, LinkedIn and Twitter have since copied it.
- Implication: you cannot predict what the algorithm will favor, so volume matters. Posting once a week gives four at-bats a month; more posts means more chances.
- Example: a friend posts 5-6 short videos a day on LinkedIn with about 7,000 followers and gets about 2.7 million views a month. One of those five videos usually goes much more viral than the rest.
- Baseball analogy: "if I hit 100 times, I'm gonna get on base 20 times."
- He applies this to B2B: produce many variations rather than a few precise pieces.

## Marketing advice: only start what you can repeat, and learn by doing

- Best advice he received: "Don't even start the thing" unless it is repeatable. Ask whether you can do it indefinitely, every day, week or month, for two years or more. If not, "don't even start the thing because it won't make impact for your company."
- He contrasts this with "campaigns", a term he says came from the military via post-WWII advertising, with a start and an end. He prefers sustainable cadence.
- Advice for aspiring marketers: start a blog, get traffic to it, and sell something to that traffic. He says doing that puts you "in the top 1% of marketers in the world".
- He says the information is free on YouTube now (he runs a tactical YouTube channel), so courses are unnecessary. Imitate what others do and be a practitioner: "trial by fire", because tactics change fast (he was troubleshooting cold email that morning because what worked three days earlier had changed).
- Use AI to learn: ask Perplexity ("complexity AI" in the transcript) for a Google Ads curriculum and a YouTube video list per item; he estimates about 10 hours of study.
- Get technical: if a marketer can prompt AI to write Python scripts they become "god tier".
- Example: for a friend's company he scraped Toast's site for lead data, used a proxy to Google each company name and address to find its website on Google Maps, saved results to a folder, then ran a spreadsheet through Hunter.io and Phantombuster to find emails. He says this was hacked together with AI-written Python and no-code tools like Zapier, and would previously have needed engineering talent.
