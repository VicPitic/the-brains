## Why YouTube creators plus affiliate commissions work for early customers

- Cody calls YouTube creators plus affiliate marketing "one of the best ways to go about getting your first customers", especially for SMB products or tools with one killer feature.
- The reason: it is "basically a product demo that's in disguise." He reaches out to creators, has them make videos about the product, pays a flat fee per video, and adds an affiliate commission on the traffic they drive.
- He calls it an inefficient market. Creators sold through aggregators, networks or agencies know their value. Independent creators, especially smaller ones with 10,000 to 50,000 subscribers, "don't know how to price themselves."
- Example math: contact 100 creators, about 50 respond, and around 10 underprice themselves so much that they are "basically a marketing arbitrage." He then finds the winners among those 10 and puts them on retainer.
- ROI is trackable, unlike short-form (TikTok, Reels, Shorts), where viewers see a video and then search Google. With a 10-minute YouTube video and an affiliate link in the description, he can see the exact dollars each creator drives.
- Scale claim: a friend (Cody McGuffy, Everbe) went from zero to 6 million ARR in 18 months, bootstrapped, using only this strategy. Cody's framing: with 100 creators posting about your product monthly, "you're going to be everywhere overnight."

## Finding creators and getting their emails

- Start by searching YouTube for videos in the product's category (his example: Google Ads tutorials for a tool sold to people who run Google Ads). Filter to videos of 4 to 20 minutes from the current month. Every creator making that content is a prospect.
- Creator emails are visible via "view email" on the channel profile, but it sits behind a captcha and YouTube limits it to roughly 8 opens per 24-hour rolling window.
- Ways around the limit:
  - Do it manually: about 8 a day, roughly 50 a week, so about 100 creators contacted in two weeks.
  - Hire someone on Fiverr. He paid one person $125 to scrape 10,000 emails in a category.
  - Use a YouTube channel email scraper endpoint on RapidAPI, which he believes uses offshore human captcha-solving. He has used it before.
- For automation: scrape via RapidAPI, load the contacts into Instantly, and send the cold email from there.
- Stormy AI (founder Robert) is a tool he recommends. It searches for creators who have already made videos on a topic (e.g. Claude Code), finds their emails, drafts the outreach, and negotiates pricing with an AI agent before bringing in the human to confirm. He describes it as early stage and useful for a one-person team.

## The outreach email and follow-up cadence

- Subject line: "paid collaboration", all lowercase. He says they have tested everything and this performs best.
- Body, in his words: a short note like "hey [name], I love the content you're posting on YouTube. Can I get a cost breakdown for a three video package and I'll give you an affiliate commission of 30%." He dictated it with Superwhisper.
- Send about 10 follow-ups in different phrasings. Creators are "constantly getting blasted", so persistent follow-up is required, even though it is "a pain in the ass."
- Ask them for their price first rather than naming one.

## Why ask for a three-video package plus an affiliate commission

- Three videos beat one. A single video is "a flash in the pan" for the audience. Three videos at different angles, spread across six to eight weeks and mixed in with their other content, signal that the creator actually uses the tool.
- The affiliate commission does two jobs:
  - It lets him track the ROI each creator produces.
  - It creates lock-in. A creator earning, say, 10 grand a month in commissions is uncomfortable raising rates or taking an exclusivity deal from a competitor, because it would hurt the relationship. Good creators naturally grow and raise prices with other brands, so this keeps his pricing from rising.
- For tracking affiliates he suggests Rewardful as one of the best-priced options for a small or vibe-coded tool, and says most tools work in a similar way.

## Spotting underpriced creators and negotiating

- Put every creator's quote for the three-video package in a spreadsheet next to their subscriber count. Divide cost by subscribers to see what he pays to reach each audience, then rank to find the cheapest per audience.
- Better still is a composite of subscribers and average views per video; he typically uses both.
- Negotiation tips:
  - Counter low. "I would cut that in half and work your way up from there."
  - Often ask "what can you afford / what can you pay," since many creators don't know their price.
  - Or say "creators of your size we typically pay X per video." If they come back higher, it turns into a negotiation.
- Example of the underpricing he looks for: a creator who says "Hell yeah, I'll do it for $200 a video" when they should be charging around a grand.
- On CPMs: he doesn't focus on them. He cares more about whether the creator already makes content in the category, because they understand the audience and how to talk to it. A smaller but highly engaged audience that trusts the creator converts very well.
- He treats such a video as an asynchronous, evergreen video sales letter, product demo or webinar that "lives forever."

## Turning winners into retainers and scaling the cycle

- After the first cohort, check the affiliate data. Of roughly 10 creators worked with, he expects two to three to drive about 80% of the revenue.
- Go back to those winners and propose a retainer of one video per month. He says they will "100% say yes" because creators want recurring money.
- Then repeat: contact another 100, find the 10 who underprice themselves, work with them, pick the two winners, and add them to the retainer list. Over time this grows to about 100 creators on retainer, each one a known ROI-positive source, with 100 videos a month dropping in the niche.
- He describes this as astroturfing the category with content. A knock-on effect: when one video goes viral, around 10 other creators make videos reacting to it, and many join the affiliate program, creating a "spiderweb effect."
- Operating at scale gets hard because he must verify that videos actually dropped. Tactics: a form submission when a video goes live, which feeds a Make automation, plus a spreadsheet pulling in each video's views and graphing the impact.

## Mispriced attention and why timing matters

- He frames the whole play as finding "mispriced attention": high-quality attention that is relatively cheap. The pattern he cites is "find misaligned things that don't make sense and then exploit it as aggressively as they can."
- Examples he gives of businesses built on mispriced attention: Wish.com became a billion-dollar company in about 18 months on Facebook ads, when 1 cent link clicks were available and nobody else advertised cheap imported goods. Zynga is cited as the same strategy.
- Caution on competition: "by the time you're hearing about it, it's already cooked." Do not buy a course on this; "they're just reselling their used clothes."
- Where it works best: a niche with a specific pain point (his example: depot resellers). Highly competitive categories like dropshipping will be very expensive.
- Even in competitive spaces there is room (he compares it to real estate in 2008). You just need a process to contact thousands of creators, for instance 1,000 or 10,000, to find the underpriced ones.
- He says it is currently "medium rare" rather than fully cooked.

## Work with creators who already have success

- His view is that people now discover products passively, through For You-style feeds, rather than actively searching.
- A creator has to balance getting distribution on the platform with giving enough entertainment and value to earn reach.
- His advice: "don't hire somebody for this, find somebody that's already doing this and work with them." They have already figured out the algorithm for that category, and it is much harder to train someone to understand the YouTube algorithm than to give an already-successful creator resources to include your product.
- Do not start from ground zero.
