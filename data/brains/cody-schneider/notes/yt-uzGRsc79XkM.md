## Digital gravity: the core marketing worldview

- Cody's central idea for marketing is "digital gravity". He says "you're trying to build digital mass on the internet so that you have more digital gravity within your sphere" (your niche).
- Digital mass comes from content. More content creates more gravity, which creates a larger inbound that "naturally happens over time".
- He rejects the traditional funnel as "a very old way to think about it". His model: a human is like a comet flying by that gets pulled into orbit, and at different points in the orbit they are closer to touching down and purchasing.
- He frames marketing as "building relationships at scale". A brand is a one-to-many relationship happening simultaneously.
- His answer to "what marketing should I do?" is "everything". His background is early-stage startups: you have a product and an audience that wants it, and the job is building distribution that takes a brand "from zero to one" until the whole industry knows it exists.

## Make marketing a lifestyle, not a campaign

- Campaigns start and stop. Cody wants marketing activities you can do "indefinitely into the future", chosen as a lifestyle for the next five years or so, at a weekly, daily or monthly cadence.
- Sustainable and repeatable beats scrappy one-offs. His example of the wrong approach: a Reddit post that produced leads once, which "was once a quarter" and can't be repeated.
- Doing two TikToks a week for 12 months beats a video a week for only 30 days. The longer you sustain something the more it compounds, and sustainability also reduces burnout.
- His filter for any activity: don't do it if it can't be automated or delegated. Automate as much as possible, then delegate the rest.
- Cadences he says work:
  - Twitter: 70 tweets a week.
  - Short-form social: a post a day.
  - Podcast: one interview a week, paired with a weekly newsletter about the episode.
- Using the podcast-plus-newsletter tactic alone, he says he has taken shows to the top 20 of the US medical, marketing and lifestyle podcast charts.
- A new channel is ugly at first. For client podcasts, the first four episodes are rough and slow, then it gets into a rhythm around day 60, cruises by day 90, is a no-brainer by month six, and by month nine "my whole industry knows I exist".

## Twitter system: volume, data, and remixing top performers

- Cody's view is that For You page content has changed everything. TikTok invented it and LinkedIn, Twitter, YouTube Shorts and Instagram Reels all adopted it. Platforms serve content based on taste, to keep you on platform, not based on who you follow.
- Because platforms decide what is good content (whatever keeps audiences watching), he treats it as a volume game, "at bats". Five clips a day gives 150 data points a month versus 30, so conclusions are more reliable.
- His weekly routine:
  - Every Sunday he writes 70 tweets and schedules them from 7am to 5am.
  - Weekly he reviews the data for top performers and remixes them.
  - He no longer writes new tweets. He recycles top performers every six weeks to two months, because "people have short attention spans so you can say the same things over and over again".
  - Some of his tweets reliably get 250,000 to 300,000 impressions each time they run. He calls it "playing your greatest hits over and over again", with new material peppered in to add "new DNA to the gene pool".
- Roughly 8,000 tweets have gone out over about 18 months.
- Data workflow: X removed the CSV export from analytics, so he built a custom Python script that calls the API and writes a CSV, then analyzes it in a Google Sheet.
- Results (as of August 2024):
  - About 27,000 followers, 24,000 of them gained in the last year.
  - About 5,000 new followers a month, growing about 7% month over month, compounding.
  - About 2.5 million impressions a month on Twitter.
- General rule: "do a bunch of stuff, look at the data to see what works, do more of what's working and less of what isn't" to build a growth flywheel. The same approach applies to shorts, emails and podcasts.

## Turning social reach into an owned newsletter

- Every tweet gets an automated call to action about five minutes after it posts, pointing to his newsletter on "growth tactics and startup ideas". He calls these "junk food for entrepreneurs" and "the honey pot".
- The same CTA runs on LinkedIn, YouTube and other channels.
- Organically this adds about 2,000 to 2,500 subscribers a month.
- He layers "gray tactics" on top: sequencing people he knows are interested in the content into the list daily. That brings growth to about 5,000 subscribers a month.
- Past about 20,000 subscribers, he says lists can grow about 30% a month compounding with these tactics, which means going from zero to 100,000 subscribers in a year for clients and for himself.
- Owned-audience stats double as outreach leverage. When pitching himself for podcasts he cites a newsletter (he says 50,000 subscribers) plus monthly Twitter and LinkedIn impressions, and says about 20% of the people he reaches out to reply.

## Email is the answer: cold email, nurture, and newsletters

- His tweet: "what marketing should I do? email. cold email, nurture, newsletters". He says email is his favorite channel and he could talk about it for days.
- Case: a friend's SaaS for interior designers (turning 2D designs into photorealistic interiors) had lots of top-of-funnel leads but poor activation and no email nurture at all. Cody's fix was a nurture sequence, which he handed over as a template.
- His claim: he has seen companies reach a million in revenue with just cold email, email nurture, and a newsletter to those same people carrying product updates and industry news.
- He says email looks unsexy because it's old, but the depth is technical: deliverability, open-rate management, A/B testing.
- His team builds custom software behind the newsletters they run, and uses AI more and more.
- On cold email resilience, he has done it about six years and gone through roughly 15 cycles where "it's dead, game over", and each time you adjust to the new stimuli.
- Example of newsletter content as outbound: for a winemaker friend on the California Central Coast, they scrape local business owners, sequence them into a twice-monthly newsletter, and write it from a 30-minute Zoom chat ("story time with my friend Chad from the ranch"). Cold recipients email back saying they love it.
- On sharing your process, one of his most viral tweets is just how his team's cold email infrastructure works. He reposts it about every six months and wins new clients each time. "People like sausage making", i.e. behind the scenes.

## Pillar content (podcast + newsletter) and repurposing

- He calls pillar content "the biggest arbitrage right now for B2B". His reasoning: a weekly podcast means people sit and listen to you for 45 minutes, which builds far more trust than anything else, "basically a church leader" with a congregation.
- Podcast plus newsletter together builds deep trust, so by the time prospects reach sales they are "buy ready", having binged the back catalog, and say "here's the check, let's go".
- It also warms outbound: when the sales team reaches out to people on the list, they aren't cold ("I listened to your show").
- Clips drive impulse purchases but not high-ticket sales, so match the content type to the business. Long-form builds trust for high ticket.
- One anchor piece becomes everything else. In roughly an hour and a half of founder time, one conversation is handed to an editor/team who produce clips, social posts, blog posts, newsletters, white papers and ebooks. Cody is building Swell AI around this idea (content repurposing powered by AI, evolving into "your AI content team").
- He says founders' scarcest resources are mental bandwidth, money and time, with mental bandwidth the hardest.

## Content strategy and clip-farming at scale

- What goes wrong in content: people focus only on sales. His ideal is the intersection of sales, entertainment, and your product as the solution to a problem.
- Example (a friend's company, Reveal AI, a qualitative analytics tool that scrapes TikTok comments for market researchers): content analyzes a known brand's TikTok comments, shows the tool used, and shows the outcome. It attaches to something the public already knows, adds a novel thought, and the product is the solution. That arc "creates a hook, creates a what-if, then gives them a solution".
- The other half of content is volume, which he treats as at-bats (batting average logic).
- Experiment he is running: a proxy network with virtual machines to create many TikTok accounts. He calls the planned product ClipWave. It takes a podcaster's or musician's back catalog, chops it into clips, and schedules them across many accounts.
- Math he cites: 100 accounts each doing 30,000 views a month is three million views a month, and he asks what happens at 1,000 or 100,000 accounts. He notes this is lower quality than fully human-run networks, such as the agency model he says is reported to charge about $35,000 a month.
- He expects platforms to eventually crack down on such networks and on AI-generated garbage via shadow-banning, and says there is an arbitrage during the window before they adjust.
- Platform feedback is free data. YouTube shorts and TikTok show viewed vs swiped away and retention, so you can see within an hour whether a clip is a dud. His method: find the outlier, then work out why it was an outlier and repeat it.

## Ops, hiring, and agency design

- Define a single North Star metric per offer up front and get everyone to agree on it. Examples:
  - Programmatic SEO: impressions, clicks, signups (signups matter most; impressions and clicks are leading indicators).
  - Newsletter growth: subscribers.
- Internal selling tip: get your boss to agree "this is what I'm measured to" and show a dashboard; then everyone optimizes toward it.
- His agency sells productized services per channel: cold email, email newsletter growth ("sub growth as a service"), full-service podcasting, unlimited Google/Facebook ads, programmatic SEO. Team members don't cross over between channels, each has a finite scope of work, documentation and training.
- Hiring approach depends on company type. For venture-backed early-stage growth, hire autonomous people who decide, act and then report, not people who ask permission or pitch internally. For cash-flowing businesses, build processes and systems where people are nodes with finite scope.
- He believes an "AI automations engineer" will be one of the most valuable team members by early 2025, since they save cost and also raise revenue through throughput. Example he cites from a friend: cutting real estate teams' time costs by about 60% in four weeks.
- Many industries still run on paper intake forms, so connecting a PDF reader to a CRM through tools like Airtable and Zapier can have an overnight business impact.
- He notes search volume for "AI automation agency" is rising vertically.
- On saturation: there is plenty of market, like hundreds of window-washing companies in Denver that each feed an owner. "We're just building window washing companies on the internet."
- He sees growth as spurts rather than a line: zoomed in it is spiky, zoomed out it looks smooth. He expects fine-tuned, use-case-specific models (for example clip making or SEO writing) to arrive within a couple of quarters and beat humans at their tasks.
