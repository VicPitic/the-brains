## Branding vs. planned purchase cycles: when the goal is branding

Cody starts client conversations by mapping the customer's journey, specifically the questions a customer asks throughout the purchasing decision. He says an agency's job is to understand the brand, understand the customer, and build the bridge between them.

- Clients usually underestimate the purchase cycle. They think customers decide much faster than they do.
- Simplified model: purchases are either impulse (within the next 24 hrs) or planned (a more deliberate process).
- His rule: "If your customer won't make a purchase within the next 48 hrs your marketing goal is branding."

## What branding is: owning an idea in the customer's head

Cody defines branding as associating your company name with an idea, a moment, a feeling or a need. The aim is to be synonymous with that idea in the person's mind.

- Example: a physical therapy clinic. Not everyone it markets to needs care today, but they will get hurt at some point. The goal is that when a customer tears a shoulder, they immediately think of the clinic (his example name: Boise Physical Therapy).
- Big-brand examples of the same association: Gillette is razors, Dawn is dish soap, Kleenex is Kleenex.
- Practical takeaway: for products bought on a delay or triggered by an event, market to people before the need arises so your name is the first thing they think of when it does.

## Branding raises mental switching costs

Cody argues you don't stop branding once the association is established, because "branding increases mental switching costs."

- Switching costs are what you pay when you move from one product to another (iPhone to Android, Tropicana to concentrate, Whole Foods to Trader Joe's).
- Each brand has physical pros and cons, and each customer weighs them differently. What gets forgotten is that mental switching costs also exist.
- People build relationships with brands the way they build relationships with people, using the same emotions. The deeper the emotions, the harder it is to switch.
- Switching is hard because it takes a lot of mental energy to uproot the association with a brand, and the idea that brand has attached itself to, and then attach a new brand to that idea.

## How associations get lost, and how to defend yours

Cody says associations are most vulnerable when a brand does something that hurts the relationship it holds with the customer's idea. That opens a gap for a competitor to replace the associated brand.

- Defense: keep developing customer relationships, grow the level of affinity, and talk to customers regularly about things they want to hear.
- Done well, this makes the brand-and-idea association hard, "if not impossible," to uproot.
- Opportunity side: if a competitor stumbles, a rival brand can take over the association.

## Checklist for branding clients or your own projects

Cody's three things to think through:

1. How the customer's purchasing decision actually happens (map the real cycle, not the assumed one).
2. How the customer wants you to talk to them about the idea you want to be associated with.
3. How to keep developing the relationship with that customer at all stages of the journey.

His closing distinction: "Marketing is mass communication but branding, branding is mass relationship development."
