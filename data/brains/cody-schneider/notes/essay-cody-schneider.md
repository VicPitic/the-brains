## Distribution beats product when feature parity is the default

- Cody says "product is shit" is a deliberate provocation: he argues distribution is more important than product, and "product always wins" is a Silicon Valley mistake.
- If product A and product B have feature parity but A has better marketing and distribution, "product A is going to win every time, full stop."
- NetSuite is his go-to example. People who use it "despise it" with "a visceral hate," yet it still wins because of distribution and a switching-cost moat.
- He argues AI is pushing the cost of creating digital products toward zero. Competitors can copy any feature quickly and cheaply, so feature parity becomes universal. Engineering bandwidth used to be the biggest constraint in organizations.
- With no software differentiation left, he says the only way to win is better distribution.
- He notes this applies to digital products; physical and retail products have different constraints.

## Owned media as a moat (a16z, Arrow Electronics, Red Bull)

- Cody says the best companies build owned media brands that sell to a well-defined target customer. "Our target customer is everyone" produces terrible media.
- **Method:** define exactly who you sell to, their pain points and the outcomes they want, then work back to the media to make.
- **a16z:** he says it sells a commodity (money) and differentiates through media distribution, by acquiring podcast networks and YouTubers across technology and VC.
- **Arrow Electronics:** a massive electronics components reseller. In the early 2000s it bought electronics blogs for their email lists and engineer audiences, then sponsored them to market directly to the engineers who asked which components to use. He calls it a flywheel for new customer acquisition.
- **Red Bull Media House:** treated as its own company that has to survive on its own.
- He advises running owned media like a separate company with its own P&L and sustainability.
- With a 400,000-person email list and a weekly podcast with 30,000 downloads, "you almost do whatever you want within an industry." Any new product line already has rails to launch on.
- **Cost of the grind:** he says it is hard, like "a diet" or "a lifestyle choice." Unlike a product built once and sold many times, media needs fresh ideas every week.

## How owned media funded itself and gave leverage at Rupa Health

- Rupa Health was a marketplace aggregating specialty lab tests so people could order from multiple labs through one portal.
- Cody's team sold sponsorships on their podcast to the labs. Selling those slots covered production and the whole marketing and distribution operation, so it was cost-neutral on the line items.
- It also gave negotiating leverage with labs much larger than Rupa. When a lab pushed back on the percentage Rupa took per test, they offered a media package instead:
  - the lab's CEO on the podcast
  - 3 live classes
  - 20 posts across Rupa's social channels
  - 4 email write-ups sent to the full list
- He says this changed the leverage in the deal: "it allows you to punch above your weight."
- His point is that owned media is both distribution and negotiation leverage, and it is hard for competitors to copy.

## Media partnerships and the "digital gravity" model

- Cody coined "digital gravity" and owns digitalgravitybook.com. The idea is to build digital mass on the internet so you have gravity in your category.
- A small mass has little pull. A larger mass catches more of the "comets" passing by and gets them into orbit around your brand. "You're Jupiter, you're not the Earth."
- **How to build it:** content engines and owned media that widen your footprint, so you are discoverable in social feeds and AI chat answers. He says people now discover products mostly on social media and AI chats.
- **Partnerships example:** with Alex Lieberman (co-founder of Morning Brew), who sells to a similar audience, he set up a marketing partnership. They run a shared webinar series every 6 weeks and share all leads. He plans 10 to 15 more deals like it over 12 months. He says this is only possible because he has an owned media network. With a 1,000-person list he'd be uninteresting to someone with 200,000.
- **Funnel view:** he says the funnel isn't clean. People sit at different stages at different times, so a large content library gives many touchpoints. His example was a company selling shower bases to hotel chains, where deal cycles ran 3 to 5 years. Keeping them top of mind meant making entertaining and educational industry media and weaving pains and outcomes into it.

## Brand as the only moat, and why content beats rising ad costs

- Cody started in print-on-demand dropshipping, scraping Etsy, Redbubble and Amazon for winning products and remixing designs. It made money, but he built zero brands, so when the market shifted he had nothing to lean on.
- **His 2026 metric:** branded search volume. He tracks it in Google Search Console, looking at searches for the Graphed name each month, and builds a dashboard on it. The question is whether it rises month over month.
- He lives by "patience, persistence, and compound interest."
- **Why content:** in a paid ads category, cost per click might start at $1 and drift toward $5 as the arbitrage disappears. The way to combat that is to get more people searching for your company by name instead of competing for the same clicks.
- Education, thought leadership, and talking to pain points and desired outcomes are the best-performing content in his experience.
- Content makes organic customer acquisition cost slide toward zero, which offsets rising ad costs. He calls the rise in costs "the law of worsening click-throughs."
- He says to tell internal teams that brand is the number one filter, because eroding it prices you out of the market over the long term.
- **Suggested first step:** take a small share of budget already going to SEO or PR, pick one thing to do well (a weekly webinar or podcast plus an email newsletter), and run it for 12 months, then measure the impact. He says he has seen PR budgets spent spinning stories, and that the same money in owned media gives far more leverage.

## Using social as a testing ground for ideas

- Cody's process: publish ideas, check impression data, and see what resonates.
- He says "For You" feeds (TikTok was the originator) show content based on what keeps people on platform, with no need for a social graph or followers. That gives an immediate feedback loop he treats as a "stress test" of an idea in public.
- When an idea gets traction, he goes deeper: a dedicated podcast episode, a 5,000-word blog post, or other formats.
- He says blogs and newsletters give slow, vague feedback (an email or two from friends).
- Podcasters can find the common thread across their top 20 episodes of the last 2 years, put the transcripts into an AI context window, and ask for a book outline and section drafts to beat up.
- He says average ideas are now a commodity. "Good ideas are now the hardest thing to come by."
- **His writing process:**
  - dictate stream-of-consciousness thoughts with SuperWhisper (Option+Space on his MacBook)
  - run them through software he built that reorganizes the logic and applies examples of his past writing
  - polish the final output himself, as a curator
- He was taught to write with "martial respect for the economy of words."

## Rolling out AI: bottom-up tooling, status incentives and an internal newsletter

- **Bottom-up over mandate:** Cody says large "digital transformation" pushes are a mistake. Companies should give individual contributors the tooling to automate their own daily workflows. He says companies that lock down tooling lose, and those that supply it win.
- **Example of lockdown:** a friend's former SDR left a company that gave him an Apollo API key (a data enrichment tool for prospecting) for one that refused, which made him far less effective.
- **Example of a bad mandate:** a software company set a weekly minimum token usage, so one employee had AI split and recombine spreadsheet columns just to hit the limit.
- **Automate what people hate:** nobody likes lead enrichment in a CRM. Give them a HubSpot API key and a Perplexity API workflow to do the research and write it into HubSpot. He says this reduces burnout.
- **Share and incentivize:** create a Slack channel or fireside chat where people post "here's the thing I built," with a Notion tutorial so others can deploy it. This creates status, and status creates a flywheel.
- **What drives people:** "money, time, and status." Status is the easiest lift for organizations (a call-out from a boss or leader, a badge of honor).
- **Internal newsletter:** at a previous company he sent one every Tuesday morning documenting what they saw working. It made him a thought leader internally and gave him leverage to get more resources. His point is to market internally like you market externally.
- He suggests asking employees to write LinkedIn posts about how they use the product.
- **Measure outcomes, not activity:** a high performer's leading indicators (messages, conversations) may look subpar while revenue is strong. The hard part is defining the outcome. Early-stage companies often can't say if success is signups, revenue, pipeline or closed deals. His line: "I don't care how you get there. As long as you get there."
- **Automate what exists first:** look at what you do manually and replicate it with the tool. Treat AI like Excel or PowerPoint, a tool in the belt, and avoid "pie in the sky" ideas.

## AI guardrails, domain vocabulary and token abundance

- **Set "nos," not "yeses":** it is easier to list lines you can't cross and let people experiment inside them than to list approved actions. Anything that could get the company sued should not be in the team's vocabulary. He says to hire people who love the game of finding arbitrage within the rules.
- **Codebase guardrails at Graphed:** their code base spells out what agents may do (only TypeScript for front end and back end, and defined UI specs, e.g. what a "card" is). He says typed languages suit AI because it is discrete and must pass checks.
- **Speed example:** he asked Cursor for a hover-isolation feature on a line chart legend, and it wrote the code and opened a PR. He reviewed and shipped it within an hour. He says this is only possible with that infrastructure set up from the start.
- **Standardized data:** a Google Analytics 4 connection looks the same for customer A and B, so the agent can be good at it once. He says messy data warehouses make it hard to build a semantic layer or knowledge graph.
- **Average output problem:** LLMs trained on the internet give the average, and value sits in the top 10% or top 1%. Test: ask Claude to write a sales email as a top 1% direct response copywriter, then ask it to edit as a 40-year veteran newspaper editor conscious of the economy of words. The result is far higher quality.
- **Vocabulary:** he says the hardest part is having the words to describe the outcome. He built a podcast for himself to learn graphic design terms, e.g. describing a TV-fuzz texture as "5% opacity" with "a compounding split of dots that are growing in size."
- **Domain experts get more valuable.** Deep technical knowledge lets you direct AI the way you would brief a VA, but with far better results.
- **Token abundance:** he says the cost of intelligence is heading toward zero. He cites an open-source model from China that benchmarked just below Claude Opus 4.6 at 1/20th of the cost within weeks. "How do I not think from a what-we-can-do constraint?" With everything possible, deciding the right thing becomes the hard part.
- **PM example:** a friend's product managers were each building internal products with Claude Code and pitching them, but nobody wanted to buy them. It "looks like work is happening."
