## Using AI to produce content: audit tasks, then feed it source material

- Cody's rule for adopting AI: audit your current systems and workflows, then automate the parts AI can handle so the team focuses on high-impact work. He estimates "80% of them can probably be automated in some way."
- He says not to pay consultants such as McKinsey or Gartner to rebuild everything "AI ready". Ask which tasks AI can take over today and implement those.
- Example: a friend consults for real estate firms, audits their workflows, builds AI-enabled operations, and automates about 60% of daily tasks.
- AI's core strength, in his words: taking unstructured data and structuring it. Example: give it a 45-90 minute transcript and ask for 10 insights on digital marketing.
- He aims for AI to get work 80-90% of the way there, with a human doing the last 10%.
- He treats AI as a tool "like Excel", good for some jobs and bad for others, and doubts AGI is near. His reason is that the compute needed to mimic a human brain's neurons doesn't exist yet.
- Even automating 10% of your own work puts you "so much farther ahead" in five years. He calls AI automation skills the most desired skill set, and says "AI automation" is the fastest-growing keyword he has seen. His tip: if you're a coach with nothing to sell, build an AI automation course and "a 1000% sell."

## Why AI content sounds average, and the fix: a walled garden of source material

- Cody agrees that AI content is average when it writes from the model's general knowledge. LLMs learn from the whole internet, and "the average is pretty average."
- The target is top-10% or top-1% content. To get it, give the AI constraints and source material, "a walled garden that it can work from."
- Podcasts and calls make ideal source material because they capture real human expertise.
- Example: asking AI to write "Google Ads for apartments" gives useless output. Interview someone who only runs Google Ads for apartments, then extract their knowledge from the transcript into a blog post, LinkedIn posts, a Twitter thread and clips. He says the output is "10x" better.
- For coaches and creators: record every call and conversation, then repurpose it into clips, blog posts and other formats. Coaches use Swell AI to turn calls into follow-up next steps and one-pagers for clients, plus clips and keyword-based blog posts.

## SEO playbook: AI blog posts built from what already ranks

1. Pick a target keyword. His example: "social media management for fintech startups."
2. Collect everything ranking on page 1 for it: YouTube video transcripts and blog post text.
3. Put it all into one context window.
4. Prompt 1: write a blog post outline for the keyword, based on the source material.
5. Prompt 2 (chained): write the full article from that outline and the same source material.

- He calls the result "top 5%" of content writers you could hire.
- His test results: this type of content reached page 1 "in like 24 hours." His personal blog (domain authority about 12) ranks around position 3 for "Google Ads for apartments," with about 30 minutes of dwell time.
- His reasoning: the content works because it draws on information already shown to provide value, not on the AI's general knowledge.

## Content funnel for creators, coaches and course sellers

- Cody says short-form (TikTok, Shorts, Reels) can't sell high-ticket offers directly. The trust-building formats are podcasts, email newsletters and long-form YouTube.
- The playbook he sees working:
  - Short-form is top of funnel: it introduces people to you.
  - It leads into owned media: email plus long-form content.
  - You stay in front of them on social every day.
- Why long-form wins: an hour a week in someone's ears is a one-to-many conversation that builds deep trust. Buyers arrive ready: "you don't have to convince them of anything." He cites a sales call that morning where the prospect said they'd watched all his videos and just wanted the link.
- Symbiotic loop he describes:
  - Record a weekly long-form podcast with guests.
  - Chop it into short-form clips.
  - Email episodes to every contact in your target audience.
  - Send clip viewers to a lead magnet, then into a drip nurture of more content.
- He says the $3k-style purchase usually follows about six weeks of emails, episodes and social exposure.
- His "digital gravity" analogy: gravity is the byproduct of mass, and digital mass is content. More content means more gravity, which means more influence and inbound. He says funnels don't work like a top-to-bottom slide. People "fly by," enter your rotation for weeks, then buy.

## Sustainable content production for a solo founder

- Content is a grind. He says to optimize for longevity: posting daily for 30 days won't move a business, but posting daily for 2 years will.
- Lean on other experts for source material. Interviews with guests cost about 2 hours a week (roughly an hour of prep and an hour of talking), and the knowledge comes from them.
- Build long-form content that gets chopped into clips, then repurposed into blog posts and newsletters, and automate as much of that as possible.
- Time saved goes to human work: sales and client-facing time.
- Distribution test he poses: know exactly where your buyers spend time, online and offline, and be in their lives daily, weekly and monthly. His example: marketing to photographers only on LinkedIn, when they're on Pinterest, Instagram, TikTok and YouTube. If you can't answer, "drop everything else" until you can. Knowing this puts you in the top 10%.

## Social posting: volume, remixing winners, and the For You page

- Cody's logic against "saturation": TikTok-style For You feeds (now on Instagram, YouTube, Twitter and increasingly LinkedIn) reward content by consumption, so only good content gets shown. You aren't clogging a followers' feed. A TikTok video can hit 1M views with no followers.
- On Instagram, he says you can post 4-6 reels a day and one will take about 80% of the views.
- His Twitter routine:
  - Posts about 70 tweets a week, scheduled in advance.
  - Weekly, reviews the last 3 months of data and takes the top 10% performers.
  - Remixes those winners with new angles. Some tweets resurface every 6 weeks and reliably get about 100,000 impressions.
- Claims from the transcript: 85% of his account growth came in the last 12 months. He says to "get back to the basics": hooks plus interesting ideas.
- On repetition: "short memories," so reposting a tweet from 3 months ago goes viral again. He says the posting ceiling is "2x or 3x higher than what people think." Once a day is the minimum, and the ceiling is roughly 4-6 per day on Reels.
- More posts mean more at-bats and more data for deciding what to make next.
- "Chicken and rice" analogy: same core ideas, different seasoning per channel (Twitter, shorts, blog posts).
- Each channel gets one action to drive: Twitter, read and click to something you own; shorts, click or go Google the brand name (what influencer/UGC content has become); blog posts, sign up or download. Write more of the 20% of posts that produce 80% of sign-ups.

## Automating job roles with AI agents

- His view: automate tasks, and a job role is a bundle of tasks. Example: a social media manager's loop is publish, see what works, find top performers in the back catalog, make more.
- Swell AI is testing an internal agent (called "Jenna") that does clip identification, captions, auto-cropping for active speaker, scheduling and publishing, data analysis, and re-clipping top performers from the catalog.
- He expects solopreneurs to hire AI agents like employees. The price he gives: about $150 versus $1,800/month for good offshore talent. Even at roughly 50% of a human's quality he'd take it, and one agent could manage 100 accounts.
- An SEO agent could do keyword research, verify volume, write, publish and index, check which posts produce sign-ups, then write more from the best performers.
- He thinks AI avatars aren't something people want to interact with, but an AI phone-answering service for restaurants (plugging orders into Toast or Square POS) is a likely standard, and he's working with a company in that space.
- Host's example (not Cody's): automated outreach to new trial signups.

- Parents both owned businesses. He started with graphic design, then t-shirts, print-on-demand and ecommerce, then marketing ecommerce, then a B2B marketing agency doing digital strategy for Fortune 500 manufacturers (windows, doors, wood paneling).
- His advice: follow whatever opportunity presents itself, and "walk through it or run through it." The best things happened by pulling the string rather than planning.
- Agency lesson: successful agencies do one thing well and say no to more than they say yes to. His full-service agency work was hard to systematize.
- Rupa Health: he joined as employee 6 and says the company went from about a $20M valuation to $110M in about 6 months. Headcount went from 6 to 70 over 18 months. Rupa is a marketplace for specialty lab test ordering, taking a 7% rate on top of margin.
- His bet there: content is the only durable differentiation when product and global talent are easy to copy. They launched a weekly podcast and weekly live classes and turned each into blog posts, clips, social posts and newsletters.
- Results he cites: the podcast went from nonexistent to a top-20 US medical podcast in about 6 months (around 180,000 downloads a month when he left). Live classes became a revenue center by charging for continuing education credits. At peak his content marketing ops team was 15-20 people.
- AI turning point: he says around October 2023 outputs got "80% of the way there" to usable. He left Rupa and co-founded Swell AI with Max to productize that workflow: one expert conversation becomes a week of media, so a one-person marketing team can function like ten.
