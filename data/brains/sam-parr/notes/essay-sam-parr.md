## Why Sam sold The Hustle: the $20M-by-30 goal and the "time billionaire" idea

- At 21, Sam set a target of $20M liquid by age 30. His logic: at $20M "you're set", it's "kind of hard to screw that up", and family needs (braces, health care, risking a new venture) stop being decisions.
- He worked backwards from that number. He hit it at 29-30 by selling The Hustle to HubSpot after about four years of running it.
- He says what he wants most is to be a "time billionaire" (a phrase he credits to Tim Ferriss). Running a company felt backwards: "everyone's like, 'you're the boss, you could do what you want.' I'm like, it's kind of the opposite. The more people I have, they own me a little bit."
- He took the exit partly because he was burnt out and the economy was uncertain in late 2020. He feared Facebook and Apple tracking changes would hurt ad revenue. He felt "I've only been at this for four years, I'm set after this."
- He still believed he saw a path to $100M a year in revenue if he had kept running it ("if you're willing to run it for 20 years, it's pretty straightforward"). He chose certainty over upside.
- On regret: the HubSpot stock he received went from about $350 at announcement to a peak near $850, and he thinks he could have gotten more by waiting six months because the ad market boomed. He says he has "zero regrets".

## How The Hustle made money and how media companies are valued

- Revenue streams before the sale:
  - Conferences (Hustle Con): seven figures a year before COVID, with tens of thousands of attendees. These went to zero in the pandemic.
  - The daily email: well over $10M a year, mostly ads.
  - Trends: a paid subscription newsletter and community.
- At sale, the next 12 months were projected at about $20M in revenue. He recalls roughly 1.7M subscribers and rounds to 2M.
- His rule of thumb for media company multiples: roughly 2x to 10x revenue depending on growth, "more likely, it's always in like the 4X range".
- A source of his caution about internet businesses: they can go from a $10M run rate in two or three years to zero in one or two, which is partly why he moved money into real estate.

## Why HubSpot was the right buyer and how they valued the deal

- Sam had assumed a WeWork or Salesforce would buy The Hustle. HubSpot approached instead, and he thought it was "smart" because HubSpot sells to small businesses, who are exactly what The Hustle's readers were.
- HubSpot was already an advertiser. They knew how much revenue and how many leads they got from advertising with The Hustle, and concluded "we should own this."
- Valuation was strategic and based on customer value, not a revenue multiple. They estimated how many customers HubSpot had acquired through The Hustle, the lifetime value of those customers, and how many more they could expect over the next five years.
- Negotiation took about 90 days, starting around October 2020, and closed at the end of January 2021.
- Takeaway: an existing advertiser who can measure their return from your audience is a natural strategic buyer.

## Deal structure: cash up front, no earn-out, and an exit from the CEO role

- Sam received "tens of millions in cash upfront" plus a large amount of HubSpot equity, with no earn-out. The stock was given over a short period and he did not have to hit targets to earn it.
- The timing was awkward: he had just hired a replacement CEO (Jordan). His condition was that Jordan would run the company and Sam would only host the podcast (My First Million). HubSpot agreed.
- HubSpot also paid him to host the podcast. Their media group is described as having about 75-80 employees.
- No-earn-out was partly HubSpot's own preference ("we don't like earn-outs"). Sam was also clear with them: "I want to own all of it or I want to own none of it."
- He acknowledges the tradeoff. Keeping a stake is a "second bite of the apple", but it costs your time. He doubts his personality would fit owning 10% of something he no longer runs.
- He says holding a minority only makes sense if the buyer can plausibly grow the business many times over (for example a $1B sale heading to $10B). HubSpot's market cap was around $45B at peak, so The Hustle was not a big needle mover for them.

## Running a business as a war, and the "sell, rest, then go all in" playbook

- Sam compares starting and scaling a company to going to war. He notes that veteran friends say it was the hardest, scariest, most stressful time and also the happiest, and that they "wish I could do it again."
- His analogy for his own cycle: training for an athletic event. Put in base miles and rest, then "when I'm ready to go race, I'll go all in." He says he is not ready to race yet but will be soon.
- He modeled his life on a few people:
  - Felix Dennis (author of *How to Get Rich*, founder of Dennis Publications and Maxim, died worth roughly $800M-$900M). Dennis wrote that he wished he had gotten as rich as he could by 35 and then retired, instead of staying "punch drunk like an old boxer", which hurt his health and relationships.
  - Travis Kalanick: sold Red Swoosh around age 30, then built Uber.
  - Mark Cuban: sold MicroSolutions at 31-32, took about eight years off, then built Broadcast.com and sold it for a billion.
- His synthesis: take a hit in your 20s or 30s, chill, reflect, find the next thing "very methodically", then go all in, which may let the second company be even bigger.
- He believes he will operate again and says the next time he will start "from a little bit of a different place": because he does it by choice, not need, it will be "more fun."
- Not rushing: he has not yet started the next company.

## Choosing people to learn from

- Sam says he built his life by following people who play at a higher level, and that hosting Hustle Con (speakers included founders of WeWork, Casper, Away, Headspace and Bonobos) gave him access to many of them.
- His best friend, Jack Smith, started a company at 22 and sold it at 28 for $800M in cash, raising little money. He lives simply in Hawaii and "wants nothing". Sam learned from his curiosity-driven approach and says wanting stuff can make you depressed.
- Another role model, John Arrow (founder of Mutual Mobile, an app development agency started in Austin, roughly 500 employees), works on the company about two days a week and spends the rest tinkering with small experimental projects, such as a vending machine on a Segway bought off Alibaba and a QR code on a stop sign that pays $1 per scan.
- Sam's attitude toward side projects is that they are "just my art", done for expression, not money.

## Investing approach: boring, diversified and relationship-driven

- Sam says he doesn't love investing but finds it "so much easier to make money that way."
- Allocation as he describes it: most net worth in HubSpot stock and Airbnb stock (his wife works at Airbnb), plus a Vanguard Total Index Fund. Selling the private company diversified him away from a single illiquid asset.
- Angel investing: he runs an AngelList syndicate that has put about $15M into roughly 60-70 startups. He expects strong returns, but credits his network, not skill: he lived in San Francisco for eight years and knows founders who go on to raise from Andreessen Horowitz. "It's a huge unfair advantage."
- Real estate:
  - Bought Austin property that has appreciated, which he says needed little skill.
  - Bought a 20-acre ranch for an Airbnb called Marathon Ranch, a fitness-style stay, turnkey and furnished, with guests booked from April 1. He plans five more cabins.
  - His thesis: a normal short-term rental might return about 15% cash-on-cash, but making it distinctive could push that to 25-30%.
  - Why real estate: the downside is limited (worst case he loses maybe 10-30% in a bad outcome, not zero), and it can be handed to family without it falling apart the way an operating company might.
- On construction delays (a house expected to take 18 months now looking like 24), he blames permits and the city as much as the builders and notes that real estate people don't hustle like internet people.

## Real estate financing and tax lessons he picked up

- Bigger deals can be easier to finance: "the bigger the deal, the easier it is to get financing." On a primary residence the bank leans on your W-2 income, but on larger buildings (he guesses about $5M or eight-plus units) the building itself is the collateral and banks often underwrite it.
- He found out late that he could borrow tens of millions at around a 1% interest rate by clicking a button, using his balance sheet. He admits this mostly works if you are already rich and says it's why the system favors the wealthy.
- Cost segregation (accelerated depreciation) was something he had just learned about and was excited by. His discussion partner explained that real estate professionals can take depreciation in one year to offset earned income. Sam says he wishes he had been in real estate before the exit.
- Non-recourse agency debt (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) was raised by the host, not Sam. Sam only says he leverages his balance sheet to invest.

## Why internet businesses beat other options, and his one action step

- Sam's realization about building online: "I can just write something and people will pay me to read it. Or I can just sell information or pixels... I'm like Picasso. Just give me a can of paint and a canvas and I'm going to create something that creates wealth out of thin air." Learning this felt like finally getting glasses.
- Early path: his parents ran a produce brokerage (buy a million dollars of onions from a farmer, arrange a trucker, sell to Walmart for $1.1M, keep about $100K). He worked for Mike Wolfe of American Pickers, ran a hot dog stand as his first licensed business, and left school to move to San Francisco after cold emailing Airbnb's founder.
- His one action step for anyone seeking financial success: "Make a list of 50 people who you admire who are way out of your league and cold email all of them and keep following up until they tell you to F off."
