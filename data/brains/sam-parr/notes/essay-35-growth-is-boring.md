## The price of steady, profitable growth is boredom

- Sam uses Warren Buffett's time on the Washington Post board as a case study of what a steady, profitable company feels like from the inside.
- He quotes Roger Lowenstein's book: the Post was "the antithesis of an entrepreneurial company," and "it is striking how little the Post did do during Buffett's tenure on the board."
- Revenue grew at "a solid but unspectacular rate of 12 percent a year." The only dramatic change was profitability.
- Post president Mark Meagher resigned with a long letter urging the Post go private, since he saw "no point in such a stodgy company holding public capital."
- His successor Richard Simmons dryly remarked after yet another proposal was killed, "The sage of Omaha has spoken again."
- Sam's takeaway: the cost of a company with good, steady revenue growth and profitability is potentially boredom.

## Boring is not the same as bad: the payoff

- The Washington Post is inherently an exciting place to work by default: daily deadlines, breaking news, impact. Once Buffett's financial discipline took hold and the company became more successful, it became much more boring.
- Sam notes that Buffett's roughly $10m position in the Post turned into north of a billion dollars.
- He says the boredom is "100% fine and can be awesome," and that he "would kill to have his track record."
- He argues that being the best at anything (sports, art, business) likely requires decades of tedious, boring work.

## There are many ways to define a successful company

- Sam lists several valid paths: get big fast (his example is OpenAI), grow a family dynasty over 100 years, or stay a small mom-and-pop business.
- The point is that "success" depends on how the founder defines it, and none of these is the single right path.
- Buffett's approach is alluring to Sam because the businesses are more predictable, the "aw schucks" attitude makes the results seem attainable, and he explains things simply.

## Match the company you build to your own tastes

- Sam admits that as an entrepreneur there are times he wants what Buffett has or wants to do what Buffett did.
- His caution: founders don't think enough about the day-to-day work required to achieve that outcome, or about how it fits their own tastes.
- Running or working at a company with Buffett-esque values can feel like boredom, and that is a real cost to weigh, not a footnote.
- Reading the Post story from an employee's perspective is, to him, a useful way to see what it takes to build a Buffett-like company. He says it is "not for everyone, that's for sure."

## Sam's self-awareness as a starter, not a compounder

- Sam describes his own specialty as starting companies and the more exciting parts of business.
- He treats the Buffett story as a reminder to himself that greatness "might occasionally require accepting tedious and boring."
- Practical read: if you are drawn to the exciting early phase, expect that a steady, high-profit compounding business may demand a different temperament than the one you enjoy.
