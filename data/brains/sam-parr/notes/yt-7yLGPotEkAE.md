## Building a business: start with tiny deals and climb the deal ladder

- Sam's early sales were deliberately casual. His hot dogs sold for about $5 in the afternoon and $8-10 in the evening ("drunk people"). His roommate-matching service took about $300-400 per room, on an honor system: find a place, then call and pay if it came through us.
- His lesson: "it's about value given." A hot dog is worth an afternoon, a roommate is worth a year, so you take a small fraction of a much bigger value and the price is higher.
- He undervalued himself for a long time. He sold his first startup (a roommate-matching app) for a job and bonuses worth about $100K, and says he only realized this year that big companies badly need people who move fast and ship, which they struggle to do.
- He says he is still a poor negotiator and has other people (including his wife) negotiate for him.
- Deal sizes climbed in steps. At the Hustle he started selling a $1,000 sponsorship, then his team sold tens of thousands, then hundreds of thousands, then multi-million-dollar deals. The bigger the deal, the longer it takes: many meetings, jargon, RFPs, becoming an approved vendor. He calls enterprise sales hard and process-driven, unlike the casual early deals.

## Getting the first sponsors and underpriced early deals

- His first sponsors (Lawtrades and Wealthfront) paid $1,000 on a "street style" deal: "just give me a grant, and if it works you advertise again; if it doesn't you've only lost a grand."
- He had to Google what an invoice was and numbered his first one "0035" so it wouldn't look like his first.
- Early on he told advertisers he'd send their message to 50,000 people and to pay whatever was fair after seeing the clicks. After roughly 10 clients he moved to $25 CPM, with past results as proof. The range he later found was about $20-30 CPM.
- His view: this is mutually beneficial. Early publishers are underpriced, so smart advertisers and media buyers should find small audiences early and buy a year of ads, then ride the growth.
- Growth timeline: Hustle Con was decided May 1 and run about six weeks later (mid-June), making about $60K profit. He lived off it for roughly 8 months, then ran it again and made roughly $200-300K profit. A newsletter written about the speakers built an email list, which became the media company. By the third event he was doing $300-400K in revenue (about half sponsors, half tickets). The newsletter went live around April 2016 and reached 100K subscribers in about a year.
- Hiring plan: costs were about $25K/month, so he sold $30K/month of sponsors to create $5K of profit, enough to hire a salesperson. That took him about two months. The salesperson took ad revenue to about $100K/month within two months.

## Trends: copywriting and pricing lessons from launching a paid community

- The Hustle's ad business reached roughly $500K-$1M/month, but Sam disliked it. Sponsors limit what you can say, and his teammates carried quotas, so criticizing a company could cost them money.
- Two to three years in he launched Trends, a paid subscription research community. It took off, and he pre-sold about $50,000 from a single Word document that explained it.
- On copywriting: he calls it the most important skill in business and life. It means understanding what motivates people and persuading them, whether to buy, to agree with you, or to like you. Trends sold on copy alone.
- On pricing: he charged $299, which he now calls a mistake. His rule: "299 is no man's land." Charge either about $99 (impulse buy) or $1,000-2,000, because someone willing to pay $299 would likely pay far more.
- He says he would run Trends differently if he owned it now; HubSpot doesn't care about its revenue. He suggested to HubSpot that they drop the price to $99.
- Trends Guides: a higher tier priced roughly $2K-$10K/year, which Sam describes as a Gartner-style model. It gave buyers niche research on one specific business model (e.g., how the Hustle does X) plus access to a peer community. They pre-sold tens of thousands to $100K. It was mostly the same as selling the $299 product, except buyers needed approval.
- He says he could build a research business into nine figures in revenue and plans to launch one eventually. He doesn't regret selling to HubSpot given his information, mindset and goals at the time.

## Selling the company: cut to the chase and disclose the bad up front

- He sold the Hustle to HubSpot for more than $20 million. He also holds a lot of HubSpot stock.
- His mistake: when asked for a number, he said these companies sell for 2.5-6x revenue, so buyers naturally start at 2.5x. "Right when I said that I was like [expletive]."
- What he did well: when a corporate development person emailed about partnering, he asked directly whether they wanted to buy. If yes, he said, he'd email them everything wrong with the company, since they likely already knew the good, and they could walk away if any were a deal breaker.
- They said none were deal breakers. He sees this as taking their later negotiating leverage ("we just found out...") off the table and making them say no fast. He compares it to dating in your 30s, where you ask about kids on the second date.
- He made up this approach himself.

## Studying other builders, speed and long time horizons

- On Ted Turner: small companies have only two advantages over big ones, speed and focus. Turner moved fast, was an outsider (a Southerner in Manhattan), and clawed his way up from billboards to a radio station to a local TV station to CNN (about 25 years, then about 10 more for CNN to work).
- His takeaway: willingness to dedicate 30 or 40 years to something is itself a competitive advantage.
- Why he researches other businesses obsessively: to avoid repeating common mistakes, because "you are the average of the five people you read and think about most," and because seeing what's possible normalizes it. His example is Roger Bannister's four-minute mile, followed within months by others. Living in San Francisco around huge successes made big outcomes feel like "do I want that" rather than "can I."
- He says founders who claim they don't do research usually do more than they realize. His example is Gagan Biyani, who knew competitors' revenues and positioning in detail.
- He also does it because it's fun and inspiring.
- He hunts for talent early: the Hustle hired many nobodies, about five of whom are now "big shots," and several had no Twitter when hired.

## Focus: say no, track one number

- At the Hustle there was no company Twitter page for roughly the first two years. The weekly "numbers meeting" covered one question: did the email list grow 3% this week? If yes, why; if no, why. That was the focus for about four years.
- He cites Herb Kelleher at Southwest: asked about adding salad, Kelleher asked if it would make them a cheaper airline, and when told no, said no. Sam's version: "you just say no all the time."
- His view is that the first big pile of wealth almost always comes from one thing, roughly 80/20. Focus on that, get enough money to never worry again, then explore.
- After the sale he shifted to exploration mode: a year of focusing on his body, the podcast and angel investing, then following whatever seems fun ("like a dog catching a smell"). He plans to "go to war" again eventually and is reading and plotting now.
- He still runs small side experiments to stay sharp, such as an $89 copywriting course (Try Copy), which sold about $20K at first and about $1K a day later. The method was "copywork" and the landing page was aimed at a 4% conversion rate. He launched it with no tweet, from Facebook group posts, on a Gumroad page with no custom URL.

## Tools don't matter: reduce friction and keep momentum

- He has "a disdain" for people who say they can't start without a certain tool, domain name or camera. "I think that those people are losers and that's a loser attitude."
- His Try Copy course made $20K on a Gumroad page with no custom URL. He says ConvertKit versus Mailchimp, or iPhone versus a fancy camera, "matters not one single bit" for nearly everyone starting.
- His rules: use your phone (it's friction-free) and pursue an idea within minutes. If a domain setup takes 20 minutes, use a generic name instead. He picks zero friction and momentum over polish.
- Tool choice can matter if you already know the company will be big, but even then you can usually switch later.

## Growing a podcast and the 50-person cold outreach challenge

- My First Million is at about 2 million monthly downloads, which he says is likely top five among business podcasts. He doesn't fully know why it worked.
- What he thinks would work: buying ads on other podcasts, spending a lot, and doing it with many shows. But you only retain listeners if the show is good. Podcasts are the hardest medium to grow because you "can't hide bad" with three hour-long episodes a week. He credits his co-host Sean's talent.
- He says copywriting skill carries over to podcasting because both are about influencing people.
- His challenge: make a list of at least 50 people you wish you could be in a room with (to learn from, do business with, befriend or date). Use a spreadsheet with columns for name, email and Twitter handle, then "messaged" (repeat about 8 times) and a mark for no reply or replied, with notes. Find emails with an email finder plugin.
- Cold email and DM them, follow up every two days, and don't stop until they tell you to go away. Replies sometimes come on the eighth attempt.
- His goal is usually just to get on their radar, not a specific ask. Under 30 is a big advantage because successful people like to help young hustlers. Send flattering updates on what you're working on, e.g., "I don't even need a reply, but I'll send you an update every couple of weeks."
- Results he cites: after about 6-8 months, people say "I'm in town, want to hang out?" He emailed the founder of Pandora about 50 times before getting a reply and now has dinner with him and their families. He did the same with Kevin Ryan (Gilt, Business Insider, MongoDB).
