## Sam's path into media and why email newsletters worked

- After moving to San Francisco, Sam cold emailed the Airbnb founders and got a job offer. They fired him the day before he started because he hadn't disclosed a pending DUI during the interview process. He calls the firing warranted ("lying by omission").
- He joined a startup that did "okay," then a second one where he was killing time looking for a big idea. He started a conference called HustleCon.
- He read Dale Carnegie-style books as a kid ("how to make people like me") and was drawn to salesmanship. His friend Neville Madora showed him a video on the power of copywriting, which convinced him written words could persuade at infinite scale.
- Newsletter insight: he noticed internet-marketer friends sold large amounts relative to the size of their email lists. He then saw an interview with Ben Lear (Thrillist) describing their newsletter-era numbers (the interview was from roughly 2008-2010; Sam watched it in 2014) and concluded it would still work.
- He started a blog of viral stories, getting about a million visitors in month one. Two early viral posts: a recap of Pandora CEO Tim's story of going 2 years unable to pay full wages (told live at one of Sam's events), and a story about a friend buying LSD on the Silk Road for medical use.
- The math he used: if a million people sign up and he emails them 5-6 days a week, it should be far more lucrative than chasing site traffic.
- Early on he had to convince advertisers that newsletters mattered; a friend told him a newsletter could never make more than $1 million. Sam's counter: in an email app on your phone, nobody cares whether it's Chrome or not.
- He says the playbook still works. More competition makes attention harder, but tooling is far better than when he had to duct-tape WordPress together. He hears that ad rates have since come down.

## Headlines, copywriting and writing for your target reader

- Sam starts any piece with the headline, sub-headline, first sentence and image in mind, in that order. Readers drop off sharply after the top, so he obsesses over it.
- Each sentence should be "a slippery slope that drags you down to the bottom." He admits this is manipulative but compares it to a good fiction book keeping you on edge.
- He writes for his target person (basically himself), uses jargon and doesn't dumb things down, and assumes the reader is smart. Non-target people can consume it voyeuristically.
- Analogy: Nike's ads show ripped pro athletes, but most buyers aren't ripped. Appeal to the ideal customer; others will watch anyway.
- He cites Shane Smith (Vice): the best way to get through someone's "[ __ ] detector" is "don't [ __ ]."
- Insider jokes and inside baseball make core fans love you more: "cults have rituals, put them in your content." Don't fear that some readers won't get it.
- Pure-utility content is a strong position but harder to do.
- On AI writing: he doesn't use it for writing. He uses it for research and ideation, for example finding studies and story hooks (the "Michelangelo effect" story, and a study showing you tend to resemble the people you grew up around) for copy about why groups matter. He says it's bad at long-form sales copy and journalistic writing, and that AI writing is an instant turn-off when he reads applicants' work.

## Content strategy: serve your taste, accept the tradeoff with views

- On My First Million (about 850 episodes, millions of monthly listeners), Sam says podcast downloads are very consistent month to month, only slightly higher each time, while YouTube views are highly variable.
- When the team chases views, those are typically episodes he hates doing, and view counts "go through the roof." Episodes he loved often get little traction.
- They tested it deliberately for a month or two: chase numbers, then not, and compared the revenue difference. His default is 80% do what they want, 20% serve the listener.
- YouTube is driven by thumbnail and title ("get rich doing this") and it is "easy to appeal to the lowest common denominator."
- Sam's rule on the audience: don't dumb it down for the broad audience, write for the target person. He doesn't track audience breakdowns closely ("the analytics are pretty [ ] and I just don't care").
- He doesn't push "everyone should be an entrepreneur." He says most entrepreneurs fail, and the show's message is that there are a million ways to get done what you want. One example is "Sarah's List": companies with 500+ employees, good maternity leave, pay, and stock options with 10x potential, for his wife's career.
- He had his mother-in-law, a Haitian immigrant in her 50s, on the show. He told her the basics of Shopify when she wanted to start a pillow business; she built the site in 2 weeks and the business now makes millions a year. He says he enjoyed that interview as much as Ray Dalio's.

## Advertising independence and editorial integrity

- Sam is not against advertising but does not want it to be the main way he makes money.
- Example: at the Hustle, an advertiser (SoFi) had a CEO who got into trouble over sexual misconduct. The Hustle's headline was "SoFi, more like So [ ]," and they ran it even though it cost his salesperson commission. He says he hated having to choose between ad revenue and telling the truth.
- He notes subscriptions have the same issue: he knows which stories drive subscribers and could game them. His view is that "everything's gameable," so be aware of it and try to stay true.
- This is a reason he prefers revenue models (subscriptions, events, community) that don't depend on advertisers.

## Building Hampton: studying old communities and the business model

- At the Hustle he launched "Trends," a paid research product on deconstructing businesses. He didn't remember the exact numbers but says it reached roughly $4-5 million a year in subscription revenue with about 3 people, at about $300 a year. A companion Facebook group reached about 20,000 members in a year and people said the community was better than the newsletter.
- After leaving, he studied older community businesses: YPO (founded around 1958), Vistage (he says roughly $600-700 million a year in subscription revenue), and World 50 (Fortune 500 executives, hundreds of millions in revenue). Their longevity (30 to 60+ years) convinced him to build a community business that could last 50-100 years.
- He used the "ikigai" framing: what you're good at, what you love, what the world wants and will pay for.
- First attempt targeted VPs of marketing, which he calls stupid and which failed. His partner pushed him to try CEOs, and it worked.
- First format: 8-person core groups with a moderator meeting monthly on Zoom. As Zoom fatigue and post-COVID demand for in-person grew, about 20 months ago they pivoted to in-person groups in each city. They now have 18 chapters.
- Hampton requires at least $3 million in revenue to interview; the average member does roughly $20-30 million. He bootstraps and has raised no money.
- He says Hampton's main member sources are people who've heard him mention it and member referrals. They spend very little on ads.
- He made a deliberate decision early that Hampton "can't be the Sam show." He's been to few of the chapters. His advice: you can fix being the central figure once you reach about 20 employees, but you must get over thinking no one can do it as well as you and over the fear of letting go.
- His partner Joe is CEO of Hampton. Sam says he is "not a very good CEO."

## Scaling Hampton to a billion in revenue: the moderator model

- Sam says the way to a billion in revenue is more chapters through trained moderators.
- About 90 moderators currently, mostly with executive-coach backgrounds, paid to run groups. They are conveners, not coaches: they draw people out ("last month you said this, give us an update"), handle scheduling and enforce group norms (some groups fine members $100 per minute late), and help organize retreats.
- Plan: let applicants become moderators, put them through a 10-day training, then send them to their own city (the example list included Berlin, Paris, Birmingham, Alabama). Hampton has marketing that already generated about 100,000 applicants to join.
- It isn't legally franchising, though he says it works like one: the brand stands for something, moderators are trained, and there is give and take on local flexibility.
- He believes this could produce hundreds of new moderators per year and tens of thousands of members, with a built-in global ecosystem (a member in Atlanta flying to Hong Kong has a local network).
- Scope is "tech enabled": an HVAC company counts if it uses technology, but not someone who owns 10 apartments in Miami. He isn't in the real estate business and rules out physical spaces like a club.
- He thinks the same model should be done for other industries (for example dentists), noting groups for religions and new moms already exist.

## Studying winners and the A-B-C goal method

- Sam's core skill, in his words, is studying people and reverse engineering them: "I'm going to steal that from you."
- Process: become a historian for roughly 6 months. Find the first employees and current employees of comparable businesses and interview them about everything that made them succeed and what dragged them down. Then copy the winners, avoid the losers' mistakes, and do it in your own niche with your own taste and style.
- He describes it as like Nike versus New Balance: similar products with small differences that change everything.
- He says this raises the odds of winning above reinventing the wheel, though the plan isn't guaranteed (he avoids the word "playbook" because unknowns will pop up).
- Example of studying events: he recommends studying Bisnow (sold for about $60 million in 2016, bought by Morning Brew; hosts 2-hour 9-11 a.m. events and real estate newsletters across markets; he estimates it may now be around $60-70 million in revenue).
- The ABC method: set a Z goal (for example $50 million in revenue in five years), work out the growth rate and attributes needed, then only focus on the next step (B) of roughly 3 to 6 months. When you reach B, set C, then D. Don't worry about later steps.
- Example B goal: go from $2 million to $3 million a year by spending more on ads to get subscribers and hosting 50 events. A different profit-based version works too, he said. Make profit the goal if that's what you want.
- Analogy: to bench 300 lb, you talk to trainers, eat 200g protein a day, lift 3 days a week with 5x5 near failure. It's not guaranteed, but far better than random gym time.
- Staying on track: build systems with stakes (hire a large team so you get dragged toward the goal even on days he complains; posting a shirtless photo monthly to lose weight). "Discipline is not nearly enough."
- Constraints: "constraints are key when you're starting a company."

## Hiring, management, working hours and company-stage lessons

- Sam says his strength is hiring good people and letting them do their own thing. At the Hustle he had about 30 employees at the sale; he says roughly half became "newsletter millionaires" or ran their own newsletter businesses. They were good at finding outcasts and empowering them.
- He says he's not a good manager: he ranks high on neuroticism, thinks nothing is good enough and wants everything better immediately, and wears people thin. He needs someone as a buffer. He says kids and success have made him more patient and less caring in a good way.
- Working hours: about 40 hours a week (10 to 5 or 6) but thinks about work constantly. He doesn't believe in 996. His view is that people claiming 60-70 hour weeks mostly work in sprints or waste time. He says having kids made him "way less ambitious" and he doesn't like that feeling.
- The messy middle: he says the hardest stage of business is between roughly $1 million and $10 million in revenue. It's where you're hiring a real team but still constrained.
- Hampton data point: he reviewed about 5,000 interview transcripts of applicants (those with $3 million+ revenue). Between $3 million and $10 million, founders talked mostly about money problems. After $10 million the talk is about hiring and scaling problems. There is a distinct drop-off at $10 million (possibly as low as $5 million), which he ties to a CEO moving from "what/why" to "who."
- Current Hampton weaknesses he names: branding and copywriting ("ironic given that's my thing"). What's going right: the pivot to in-person, strong hires, and a strong executive team.

## AI's effect on agencies, his AI use, and media predictions

- Sam rates himself an intermediate AI user; his team and business partner are far more advanced (his partner sends him AI-built dashboards). He uses Claude daily, has made a couple of Chrome plugins, and lets new tools go into his workflow.
- He thinks agencies are the biggest near-term winners: agencies have been easy to start but painful to run since growth meant adding more people and client services is hard. AI changes margins because fewer, more talented people can do high-quality work, so he expects agency valuation multiples (public and private) to rise.
- His personal setup: "Ari," a fictional AI persona (styled after a young Jackie Kennedy) who reads his texts, Slack and email plus news he cares about and prints a personalized newspaper each morning. He has made other personas with different political takes. The printer breaks about half the time.
- Business idea he offers: an all-AI New York Times, a physical paper or newsletter produced by AI personas that eventually do their own scoops. He acknowledges many will hate it.
- Luddite history he told: weavers in England (about 300,000 to 400,000 artisans) smashed steam-powered machines; destroying them became a capital offense. They signed letters as "General Ludd." He says refusing AI means you'll get left behind if your goal is to get rich, but you may choose a happier life. He adds, "I just don't think your emotions matter that much" if you set a goal to build a company, though "not using AI" could itself be a marketing angle.
- He is glad his own business is human-driven (events, groups), and says he picked it for durability, not AI reasons. He was always nervous about depending on Gmail changes at the Hustle ("a newsletter is like a pirate ship, every email is more wind in your sails").
- On the OpenAI-TBPN deal: he thinks it was great for John and Jordy but "stupid" for OpenAI, and predicts OpenAI will give the company back within two years, after which the founders "win twice."

## Corporate media inside a company, and how a startup founder should get attention

- Sam says starting a funny or opinionated media company inside an organization is nearly impossible, because employees don't want to get fired and the voice gets sanded down. Successful ones need a powerhouse opinionated person with free rein.
- HubSpot bought the Hustle largely because they couldn't build a media program themselves. Its CEO Brian Halligan told Sam that if anyone ever tried to censor him, he should text Halligan directly, and nobody has ever told Sam not to say something. Sam says the Hustle (now HubSpot Media) and My First Million drive large amounts of lead generation and revenue for HubSpot.
- Advice for a startup founder who wants attention: pick a super niche, narrow field (probably one you think is too small), and share about it every single day. Make it jargon-filled and esoteric; once you build that nucleus it's much easier to expand.
- Be specific about who you appeal to and who you don't. Have an enemy and be willing to "piss that person off." Define the audience narrowly across demographics and psychographics and only appeal to that person.
- Example: Derek of Die, Workwear, who posts hyper-niche menswear content for about 15 years, grew huge, and got a lift when Elon bought Twitter.
- Media people he admires: Lenny and Newcomer, practitioners who are niche and don't answer to anyone. He notes writing is harder than people think ("just cuz you've always thought about running doesn't mean you can run a marathon"), and says to just start writing a few posts rather than dreaming about it.
