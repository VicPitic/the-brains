## How Sam writes a complaint that actually gets results

Sam's core claim: most people complain by being "pissy and rude," which is "totally counterproductive." A hostile email gives the company nothing to act on except to let you leave.

## The $80 Uber ride example

- A friend visiting San Francisco tried Uber for the first time and got hit with 3x surge pricing. A 2 mile ride cost $80. This was before Uber showed many surge notifications.
- The friend's draft email: subject "Pissed customer," saying he would never use Uber again and calling them "Assholes."
- Sam's critique: the company's likely reaction is "looks like this guy will never use us again," so they apologize and move on. The email "wouldn't accomplish a THING": no refund, and Uber loses a customer.
- Sam rewrote it using principles from *How To Win Friends and Influence People*:
  - Subject: "Helpful feedback from an unhappy user" (not "Pissed customer").
  - Open with a positive: he had heard "a ton of amazing things" about Uber.
  - State the specific problem: charged $80 for a 2 mile ride, and while he saw there was 2x surge, he expected $20 or $30.
  - Say he wants to keep using the product, but is unhappy and considering Lyft.
  - Close with a reasonable question: "Doesn't $80 seem a little ridiculous from SOMA to Mission?"
- Result: the friend got $50 back plus a $20 credit on his next ride.

## Make competitors compete for you

- Sam says to use Twitter when you want to get "real frisky," because you can include the company's competitor in the tweet.
- His reasoning: "most companies care WAYYYYY more about beating their competitors than they care about making you happy. Use this."
- Example: Sam used Slacker Radio daily for 2 years but hated its new design, though he was still considering their premium product. He tweeted about it in a way that involved Spotify.
- Spotify responded by giving him 3 months of Spotify Premium. Sam says he planned to become a full-time paying customer once those months ended.

## Sam's rules for dealing with customer service

- Don't insult or yell at them.
- Explain that you want to remain a customer if they fix the issue.
- Tell them you will use a competitor's service otherwise.
- Tweet when possible and tag the competitor's Twitter handle.

## Framing

- Sam's premise: if you use a product, you are probably a fan, and a company would want your feedback. Complaining politely and specifically is feedback; complaining rudely is noise.
- He wrote this in 2014 in his early 20s on his old blog, The Anti-MBA, and labels it an "old post."
