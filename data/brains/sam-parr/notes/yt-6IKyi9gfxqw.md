## Sam's origin story: fast action, plotting, and focus

- Sam's college side hustle: he bought mypoisonivytreatment.com to learn SEO after getting poison ivy. He found Zanfel sold for roughly $50 an oz, and a commenter said it was basically the mechanic hand cleaner "Mean Green" (barrels for about $50).
- He repackaged Mean Green as "Itch Juice" with a Fiverr logo, selling for about $40 against roughly $1 of cost. It did $200-300 a day in week one and later $500-1,000 a day. He quit because he didn't want to build a poison ivy business.
- His rule: when he has an idea and momentum, he acts within 24 hours. He ties this to impulsiveness, OCD, and a habit of giving himself 3 seconds to approach a girl. Early results create dopamine and keep him going.
- He calls himself a "manifest cowboy" and uses self-hate as fuel ("you're soft, you can't pull this off").
- Plotting phase: before The Hustle, he spent about 12 months researching. He maps economics, who is winning, and where they are weak. He cold-calls bankers who sold companies in the space, founders, and ex-employees, and reads Glassdoor, Reddit and app reviews. He keeps it all in a Notion doc, then checks which idea fits his skills.
- He told Shaan (co-host) after Milk Road sold to take 6-12 months to plot with no timeline. Once you have an audience, getting customers is easy; building a product you want to run for 10 years is hard.
- After deciding, he goes all in and treats it like a 9-to-5 (longer in practice). He argues for focus, not juggling many things.
- With an audience he talks less about new ideas before they work: smart audiences copy you, early exposure risks reputation, and too many early customers is a problem. He avoids hyping tests because people read a launch as the next big company.

## How Hampton was launched and run

- Hampton (joinhampton.com) is a vetted peer community for founders of digital-first companies, like YPO for internet entrepreneurs. Founders need at least $1M in revenue (average is about $30M, per Sam).
- The idea came from a monthly "business group therapy" with Sean, Siavash and Ryan Hoover. They discussed layoffs, what to do with money, and how to sell without looking desperate, things you can't Google or tell employees.
- Process: members apply, are interviewed, are placed in a group of 8 with similar-sized companies and industries, and meet monthly with a facilitator. An online community runs alongside. Example: during the Silicon Valley Bank scare, members got a heads-up and pulled money out.
- Launch: a Google Doc describing it, ending in a Typeform. He sent it to about 50 friends via Twitter DM, interviewed everyone who responded by phone, and with partner Joe Spicer hired executive coaches and wrote a curriculum.
- He and Joe recorded the interviews and hand-matched members into groups. They used his free HubSpot account (from selling The Hustle) and emailed Stripe links.
- It was manual at first and reached multiple millions in run rate before there was a website. Work started in summer and the site came in December.
- "Hand-to-hand combat": some days his calendar is booked 9am to 9pm with customer calls, and he won't build a website until there are sales.
- Why he built it: people said he could only build things because he had an audience. He did it quietly, without announcing it, to prove otherwise. He also had Tiger 21's founder on the pod and interviewed him publicly so everyone had the same info he did.
- Before Hampton, he had run Trends (trends.co), which had Hampton-like qualities, but he felt he got parts of it wrong.

## Bootstrapping vs. raising money

- Sam and Joe chose to self-fund. Before starting, they wrote out what success meant to each of them in a Notion doc (daily life, values) and aligned on freedom: "whatever the hell we want, whenever the hell we want," working hard without outsiders telling them what to do.
- His case: you can build more wealth by owning most of a company you grow at your own pace. A community product doesn't need to scale like software, so he didn't want to be forced to grow fast.
- Raising money still fits when you have "a machine that turns one dollar into five." Then you "fill up the dump truck with money." He cited Uber and Chewy beating Joe's profitable-from-day-one PetFlow.
- Exits are rare: the stars have to align. When The Hustle sold, the CEO had a bad accident a week later. He knows someone who sold a billion-dollar company and made $4M because of four partners.
- He thinks the odds are better with a company you mostly own that sells for $30-50M than a venture-backed one that needs a huge exit.
- He tells people the safest path to wealth as an individual contributor is to join a Series C company with 250-1,000 employees, good benefits and equity with 10x upside (Sarah's List on the pod). His wife joined Airbnb at about $18B.
- Founder pay anecdote: a friend sold a company for hundreds of millions, and just before the wire hit he had about $2,500 in his account.

## Distribution and growing an email list

- Sam's first 100,000 Hustle subscribers came in year one from long blog posts he posted on Reddit, with about 3% of visitors signing up. Today he would start on Twitter plus the blog.
- Growth rule: aim to grow the list 3% per week (he cites Paul Graham). His team held a Monday "numbers meeting" for years, picking one metric, later two opposing ones (new customers and renewal rate), so none could be gamed.
- The Hustle reached about 3.5M readers (about 2M at sale). He estimates a list that size could make $40-50M a year from email ads alone.
- Paid marketing only after organic: he, the Skimm and Morning Brew began paid acquisition once they had hundreds of thousands of subscribers and understood CAC and LTV (about $2 per customer). Newer publishers do it too early, which makes a leaky bucket and false signals.
- Paid marketing should wait until about 20,000-40,000 subscribers (a rough ballpark) for a mass-market email.
- List value is roughly subscribers x engagement/influence x spending power. 10,000 CTOs at software companies with 1,000+ employees beat 500,000 stay-at-home moms.
- Real influence shows when readers buy what you recommend, read your book picks, or show up to your meetups. Subscriber growth is easy to buy ("you need a tow, man? I'll get you a tow"), but retention for 3-4 years is the hard part, so get the content first, then the growth.

## Copywriting as the core skill

- Sam calls copywriting the most important skill: "understanding what motivates people and then using the written word... to get them to do what you want."
- He says it applies to hiring, dating, fundraising for charity, and selling. He claims that given a plain text page versus a designer's fancy page, his words would win on conversion.
- Method: copywork. Print great writing (sales letters, in his case) and copy it by hand daily, the way you learn piano by playing other people's songs. Hunter S. Thompson did it with books, and Judd Apatow with SNL scripts. He says it imprints texture and flow.
- Agora case study: Bill Bonner's Baltimore publishing company has dozens of newsletters and does over $1B a year in revenue, per Sam (he is vague on profit). The funnel: free newsletter or webinar, then a $100 product, then a $2,000 product, and most money comes from the back end. Pages are about 4,000 words of long-form direct response with a small "buy now" link at the bottom. He says the company has been sued repeatedly for misleading claims, but the copywriting is worth studying.
- AI: he is unsure whether copywriters survive, but he says AI is a productivity tool and some humans will still edit. He fed the book *How to Get Rich* (Felix Dennis) to ChatGPT and asked it to rewrite his sales page as Felix Dennis, then iterated and stole the best lines.

## How Sam sizes people up and builds a team

- He hires for "freaks": original thinkers who take the risky right option instead of the safe embarrassing-proof one. At The Hustle he told hires "let your freak flag fly."
- Authenticity check: he distrusts people who turn on weirdness in the interview. He asks what they read every morning, who they follow on Instagram, and which subreddits they use.
- The "bottom fourth of the resume" trick: ask about clubs or the major, e.g. "what was the most interesting class and why?" If they can't talk with passion about what they spent four years and $100,000 on, he passes.
- His admiration is for quiet operators: CB Insights' Anand (reportedly around nine figures in revenue on about $10M raised, patient for 10 years, low profile) and Dharmesh Shah of HubSpot, who funded HubSpot with $500,000, told Brian he'd be CEO, and still builds side projects like WordPlay and ChatSpot.
- He also admires John D. Rockefeller (honest dealing, left in his early 40s and let CEOs run things) and Rob Dyrdek.
- He says he reads fiction and history now, not business books.
- Biggest insight from HustleCon green rooms: successful founders are not 20x smarter, just braver, and they share the same anxieties. Surrounding yourself with a crew that expects success builds confidence (Jamaica sprinters, NWA, Basquiat/Haring/Warhol). It made him believe he can do almost anything that doesn't require genius-level IQ.
- He says he is not as interested in dinners with the older and richer crowd as in hanging around young builders, such as the AI scene in San Francisco, for new ideas.

## Ideas he sees working, and AI experiments

- Brokerage businesses: asset-light marketplaces in industrial niches (e.g. bricks, aluminum, materials) where a national buyer wants one phone number and local suppliers fill orders. He says he has seen ones with $50M+ revenue and 20 or fewer employees. His dad brokers onions (to chains like Walmart and White Castle) with one other person. Look for national reach matched with local production.
- Small asset managers: a $100M fund at a 2% management fee is $2M a year before expenses, in his estimate.
- Jackson Greathouse Fall's Hustle GPT experiment (a $100 budget handed to GPT-4): he found it fascinating and sees AI as a co-pilot for business research and drafts, though he still edits.
