## Lloyd Blankfein: what Sam observed about power, money habits and upbringing

Sam recorded My First Million with Lloyd Blankfein, former CEO of Goldman Sachs, on June 3, 2026. Sam has met many billionaires through the podcast, but Blankfein was the first guest who was both powerful because he ran a powerful organization he didn't start, and a big name on a global scale beyond business.

- Blankfein's father worked for the post office and he grew up in the projects. He became "successful" in his 30s, which Sam calls young.
- Sam's takeaway: "growing up poor impacts you for life."
- Blankfein still uses the cheap, ad-supported Netflix subscription.
- He wore a "cheap(ish)" Suit Supply sports jacket.
- Sam assumes he is a multi-billionaire with a fancy life, and finds it funny that small habits from his upbringing remain.

## Blankfein's investing habits: day trading and a mostly individual-stock portfolio

- Blankfein has been retired for 10+ years. His passion and hobby is day trading.
- He joked that he had to place a bunch of orders right before recording, because he would normally spend that time trading. He said he can't stop checking his phone because he loves it.
- Sam relays rough numbers only: about 25% of his net worth is in an index, and about 75% is individual equities.
- He likes big tech, meaning the big players plus some of the smaller secondary ones.
- His advice for becoming a great investor: the number one thing to study is history. "History goes in cycles," so knowing what happened before helps.
- Sam shares the history-nerd streak. Blankfein is passionate about the founding fathers, the medieval era and Robert Caro's books.

## Climbing a power ladder versus owning a business

- Sam prefers the term "small business owner" for most entrepreneurs he meets. Even at $10b+, he says most still act like small business owners in some ways.
- Blankfein wasn't that. Goldman was already one of the best firms when he joined as an entry-level employee, and he rose to become CEO and steward of it.
- Sam says he prefers being an entrepreneur because it suits his personality.
- He still found something "intoxicating" in joining a storied, powerful institution and leading it. He doesn't typically think about prestige, but says he gets why it's appealing.

## Why Blankfein was so relatable, and why that explains his rise

- Blankfein works with and influences presidents, Putin, Warren Buffett and Elon Musk, according to Sam.
- Even so, talking to Sam (who calls himself "a peon"), he was "locked in, kind, charming, and very, very relatable."
- Sam concludes: "I can see why he became CEO."
- His overall read: sharp, charming, very likeable, and it is clear why he is so successful.

## Work ethic and thick skin

- Blankfein had no work-life balance and it didn't bother him. He was high energy and seemed to enjoy the grind. His career was "go go go."
- During the Occupy Wall Street and mortgage crisis era, Blankfein was "enemy #1" because he represented the big banks. Sam notes the oddity that you can't even get a mortgage from Goldman.
- Protestors gathered outside his apartment building. When Sam asked if that worried him, he joked: "No, that's what doormen help with."
- Sam says he repeatedly came across as quite tough-skinned.
- Sam's lesson: he "internalized" that achieving greatness means having a lot of critics, and that is simply part of it.

## Takeaway: powerful people stay normal in small ways

- After the recording, Sam asked what Blankfein was doing next. He said he would walk the 2 miles home because he had nothing else going on.
- Sam's framing: powerful people, in his words "ballers," are "just like us" in everyday habits.
- Across the notes, the pattern is frugality from a poor upbringing, a hobby he loves (trading), curiosity about history, charm and relatability, and tolerance for criticism.
- Sam hopes to record with Blankfein again.
