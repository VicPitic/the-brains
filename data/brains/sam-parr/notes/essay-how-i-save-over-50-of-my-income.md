## Saving 50%+ of income: the core rule and his goal

- Sam says he has saved "like a mad man" since age 19. He aims to save 50% of post-tax income and often exceeds that.
- Saving equals earning more plus spending less. He treats this essay as the spending-less half.
- Saving is not being cheap. It means having money for things that truly make you happy instead of over-consuming on stuff that "leaves you empty."
- He frames it as partly spiritual: knowing what makes you happy and what doesn't, and knowing you need very little to be happy but can splurge if you want.
- His stated payoff is coming from a "position of fuck you": you don't need much to be happy, and you have the money to do what you want.
- Benchmark he cites: the average American saves a single-digit percentage of post-tax income per year.
- When he earned very little, he still managed to spend only $1,000-$1,500 a month while living in San Francisco. "I lived like a spartan...and I was very happy."

## Why he targets 36 months of savings, not 6

- His target is 36 months of liquid savings. This is aimed at anyone with less than 36 months saved.
- "Savings" means assets easily liquidated: cash savings, stock, 401k.
- Common expert advice is a 6-month emergency fund. Sam thinks that is "way too short."
- Reason: he is an entrepreneur and very conservative with money. When business booms he is "rolling in it," but when business is bad or he is starting something new, his own income is the first thing to stop.
- 36 months is the number he feels comfortable at.

## Step-by-step system: track, measure burn and runway, set goals

1. **Aggregate accounts with Personal Capital.** Free, similar to Mint but easier to use in his view, about 10 minutes to set up. Check it monthly for income and expenses. He calls it his favorite tool.
2. **Track spending manually with the My Weekly Budget app ($0.99).** Write down every purchase. Whether or not you have a budget (he says you should), tracking forces you to notice spending and typically leads to improvement.
3. **Build a personal burn and runway spreadsheet.** Take trailing 3 months of spending from Personal Capital and average it; that is your burn. Divide liquid assets by burn to get runway. It doesn't need to be fancy, "just know your numbers."
4. **Set milestone goals:** 3, 6, 12, 24 and 36 months of runway. If you have under 3 months in cash, reach 3 first, then 6, and so on. Put these in the spreadsheet.

## Setting the budget and feeling the pain

- Review current expenses in Personal Capital, decide what makes you happy and what doesn't, and cut what doesn't.
- Your monthly burn without those cut items becomes your monthly budget. Convert it to a weekly budget in My Weekly Budget, log every spend, and stick to it.
- "If you're not hurting then you're not saving enough."
- If you are young, in debt, or starting from $0, he says this can take years, maybe decades. "Embrace it. It's worth it. It hurts so good."
- Caveat: if you're happy with your current situation, ignore all of it: "You're winning."

## Debt rules

- Pay off debt before building savings, and build debt payments into the budget.
- He names two approaches: pay off high-interest debt first, or pay off small loans first to build momentum.
- If you have no debt, do not take on debt again until you've hit your savings goal. "Debt can be crushing."

## Tools he uses to grow the savings

- **Robinhood:** he buys stock and rarely sells. He says he pretty much only buys Facebook and a general S&P 500 fund.
- **Ally Bank:** savings account at 2% interest at the time of writing. He gives the math: $10,000 earns $200 a year, $100,000 earns $2,000.
- **Robo-advisors:** he likes Wealthfront, and says Betterment and others work too. He keeps "a fair bit of money" in Wealthfront with risk tolerance set as high as it goes.
- Context: the essay is from 2019, written in his early 20s on his old blog, The Anti-MBA, and he keeps it up for nostalgic reasons. The rates and tool choices reflect that time.
