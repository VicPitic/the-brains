## Setting a money number, hitting it, and still wanting more

- At 24, Sam asked a rich person what he spent. The answer was about $80-100k a month, while Sam was spending roughly $3,000 a month in San Francisco. He asked himself what sum would let him spend that much without ever running out, and settled on about $20 million.
- He set a goal of having roughly $20M post-tax, liquid, in index funds and bonds by age 30. He sold his company at 31 for around $20M and doesn't regret selling.
- He still wants more, though not for lifestyle reasons. He grew up without much money and is "always afraid of running out." He calls that a personality defect.
- He compares goals to bench pressing: he did 295 and wants to see if he can hit 300 in two or three weeks. He gets a rush from seeing progress.
- Selling gave him "less of a chip on my shoulder." It proved he was decent at the trade of business, and he describes it as a nice chapter, not an arrival. He says the old rage at ex-girlfriends and doubters is "awesome fuel," though it is hard to turn off.
- He paid himself $20k in year one, $20k in year two, about $50k in year three and about half a million in year four. For the first two or three years in San Francisco he was on Obamacare. His conclusion: "people who say money doesn't make you happy, it definitely makes you happier."

## Arriving isn't real: insecurity among the very successful

- At his Hustle events, Sam would bring speakers in two hours early and sit quietly backstage listening. The founders and billionaires there complained about avoiding firing someone for a year, whether a $100M raise would pay off, and stage fright before 3,000 people.
- The lesson was that he could be insecure and still be good at his job. Insecurity is normal and doesn't go away. "Arriving is not the reality"; the destination keeps moving.
- Meeting many successful people showed him they are often in the same league on IQ and ability. A few are on a different level (he named Grammarly's Max as someone whose brain "has more power"). For most, the gap is choosing a path, timing, genre and luck.
- He believes that with 10 dedicated years in a craft or sport you can get surprisingly close to your heroes. That belief shifted his sense of what he could do, and now the question is which game he wants to play (for example, not the high-stress Wework-style raise-money game).
- Luck is real. "Work hard and you're lucky," and he dislikes bootstrap talk.
- Hampton, his CEO group, ran a wealth survey of members spending anywhere from $10k to $200k a month personally. Both groups reported similar happiness, and both said the spending didn't feel like much. Takeaways: most people don't know what they're doing, most aren't happy, and things aren't what they seem.

## Fear of being taken advantage of, and owning things instead of being owned

- Sam is paranoid about being taken advantage of. When Scott Galloway asked him on the podcast what he'd made from his sale, he choked up partly out of that worry, not necessarily shame.
- At his ranch he hides his Tesla when a vendor comes to fix the driveway.
- His stated aim: "I own my things, they don't own me." He remembers his dad flipping out over a dented car and doesn't want to be like that.
- He has soured on possessions. He has owned nice cars and found them a burden (a $2,500 set of tires, dead batteries, special insurance), plus the chain of an assistant to manage it, a bigger house to store it, and junk haulers to clear it.
- In 2024 he was getting rid of nearly everything, selling items on Facebook Marketplace (a workbench for $400 and an old Xbox for $100) and enjoying it. He was moving into a furnished rental with only his clothes.
- He plans to sell his house and rent until he finds a home he'd keep for 50 years, instead of rushing a purchase.
- On "reduce, reuse, recycle," he says to drop the recycle part. Waste-management owners he interviewed told him most of it goes in the garbage.

## Buy it for life, and building slower

- Sam is into the "buy it for life" movement (a big subreddit): rather than the $50 version you keep replacing, buy the thing that can last decades. His example was a convertible Amish-style crib that grows from infant to full-size bed.
- He applies the same slowness to work. He has been trying to build companies and projects more slowly: "what are you rushing towards?" He used to rush and is glad he did, but now asks why you'd hurry through something you enjoy.
- He reads for interest, not to learn. He read self-help heavily between ages 20 and 28, and now reads only what he finds interesting, with learning as a by-product. He thinks speed reading is a scam.
- His fitness is goal-driven. He picks a new goal each quarter, such as a bench or squat target, the splits, or an ultra marathon. His NFL combine attempt, with a 4.6 forty, 33-inch vertical, 107-inch broad jump and 16 reps at 225, was about 70% of his best. He says it was average for a wide receiver. He was a Division I track and field athlete and believes fast-twitch vs slow-twitch muscle is largely inborn.
- He theorizes an ultra is easier than a fast marathon because success is just not dying, but says he hasn't tested it.

## Newsletters as a business model

- Sam asks whether the email newsletter is one of the greatest business models ever, if set up right.
- He cites the Hustle: about 4 million subscribers, a team of three writing it, and revenue of around $1 million a month on the way to roughly $20M in the year it sold. It had 30-40 employees at sale, partly because monetizing required accounting and other staff.
- Growth cost was small relative to scale: he says they spent about $400,000 in the month before the sale on growing the list.
- The model he likes: an input of a few writers produces millions of impressions daily, and you can write better when you write to a subscriber base instead of an unknown audience.

## Marriage, a pointed direction, and dependents instead of psychedelics

- Sam spent ages 20 to about 23 or 24 intoxicated daily. He went to jail more than once, slept on the street and on buses, and expected to end up homeless or a criminal.
- He quit cold turkey at 23 or 24. A seizure and hospitalization came first. After a false start he locked himself in a room for about six weeks, with a blood pressure machine to monitor himself.
- Two things pushed him to quit: watching Oasis play to 100,000 people and realizing he could have that kind of impact but was a "loser" sitting intoxicated, and coming home after 24 hours in jail to a dog who had messed the house.
- He doesn't crave alcohol now and drinks non-alcoholic beer. He knows he is prone to addiction (nicotine pouches, formerly sugar) and calls himself a "better not" person.
- His view on relationships: "the best life hack on Earth is who you pick." He uses an image of a car with its back wheels jacked up and spinning. A good partner helps you point it in the right direction, then you pull the plug and go.
- He says if you can't commit, that may mean you have the wrong person. As an alternative to psychedelics for people lost at 40, he suggests having a kid, a dog, or employees, anything where others rely on you, so the question changes from "I am lost" to "I need to provide."
- On psychedelics, he has taken shrooms only once recreationally, is reluctant to mess with brain chemistry, and respects veterans who say it helped. He doesn't think they should be pitched as a cure-all.

## Heroes are flawed, and Twitter vs the blog era

- Sam looks for people who are "mostly awesome." He names Lars Hamilton, Rob Dyrdek and HubSpot's Dharmesh Shah, admired for family-first lives, loyalty to spouses and a positive outlook. He also names Rockefeller, whom he sees as mostly honest and a good husband and father.
- He notes that heroes like Teddy Roosevelt and LBJ had ugly flaws. His line: "all great men are bad men," because achieving anything on a large scale means making decisions that leave a lot of people unhappy.
- On Twitter, he defends it as a place to find smart people. He gives the example of a thread by Die Workwear on how postwar Japan blended American fashion with Japanese craftsmanship, which led him to read a whole book on the topic.
- He also says it addicts successful people. He tells of friends with money or fame (he named hockey player Chris Pronger, whom he helped get started) who got hooked on wanting more audience, and he warns them they don't need it.
- He prefers the 2005-2015 blogging era, with blogrolls and long, nerdy, in-depth posts, and still reads Hacker News daily. He says you can't hide bad ideas in writing, which is why Amazon requires six-page memos.
