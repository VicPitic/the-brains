## Art vs. business: why Sam says he is an artist, not a businessman

- Sam says he is an artist. His art is "pouncing on an opportunity and putting my texture" on it: launching, driving hype, and turning a non-thing into a movement people buy into.
- He enjoys the zero-to-one stage. Around $10M a year in revenue, the work shifts to spreadsheet optimization (moving a conversion rate from 1% to 2%, allocating capital, hiring to a plan). A manager can do that, and he got no joy from it.
- He did not know the difference between revenue and cash flow until about four years into the company. He finds tax strategy and financial analysis boring and says to "just pay someone to do it."
- He tried reading Warren Buffett and talking to hedge fund people, and found hunting for loopholes in numbers dull. He prefers building brands and convincing people something new is worth buying.
- His decisions often make no logical sense at first and only look sensible a year later.
- Brand work is the part to nail early. Operations, finance and tax mess "figures itself out," but "the brand [stuff] doesn't figure itself out." Brand is rare and can't be bought, and more people can fix operational mistakes than can build a brand.
- Early-stage advice:
  - Don't form an LLC first; use your own social security number to collect money.
  - Don't buy a domain; use a free URL like "my product.squarespace.com."
  - Don't collect domain names. Get a sale, a customer or someone who likes the idea before doing any admin.

## Origin: blogging, Airbnb, and building The Hustle

- Sam sold sports equipment on eBay in high school, worked for Mike Wolfe of American Pickers in Nashville ($10 an hour), and ran a hot dog stand called "Southern Sam's wiener as big as a baby's arm."
- He ran track at a Division I school but quit in his junior year.
- He saw Airbnb ("air bed and breakfast") in 2010 and wanted to work there. He blogged on Tumblr and emailed the CEO ideas to get noticed. He got an interview and a job offer.
- The day before he started, Airbnb rescinded the offer because he had lied about a criminal record (a DUI and a fight from his college addiction issues). He was left in San Francisco with almost no money.
- He kept blogging, sometimes five articles a day, and used the blog to start a TED-style conference. He got his customers to the conference through the blog.
- He then realized email was more powerful than the blog: the bigger his email list, the more conference tickets he sold. That led to The Hustle, a daily tech and business email.
- Early on, he got conference speakers (founders of WeWork, Casper, Away, Grammarly and others) into the green room and let them talk. They complained about the same insecurities he had.
- From this he concluded that:
  - top founders have the same self-doubt as everyone else and are scared but act anyway;
  - luck is real;
  - most are in his intelligence ballpark, and a few are smarter;
  - many are in roles they didn't want but are too nervous to hire someone to replace them.

## Selling The Hustle to HubSpot

- Sam's personal goal was $20M after taxes by age 30, which would let him spend roughly $600-700K a year for life. He was inspired by Felix Dennis's book *How to Get Rich*, whose lesson is to sell out by about 35 and avoid becoming a "punch drunk boxer" who never quits.
- He sold in part because it was 2020, with Covid and the George Floyd riots, and he thought the world might end. He had also been sick, paid himself little, and felt basically poor.
- In 2019 he hired a banker, who lined up about 10 potential buyers. None were interested.
- In October a HubSpot corp dev person emailed him cold. Sam replied that he didn't want to waste time and asked whether they wanted to buy or not.
- He then sent a three-page Google doc listing every risk (revenue, projections, what could hurt the business) and said to call him only if none were deal breakers. None were.
- Process and timeline:
  - Letter of intent in the first month.
  - Term sheet at the start of the next.
  - Then about 90 days of diligence.
  - Close in February.
- Mistakes and lessons:
  - He had no banker and said his price first, and HubSpot accepted it right away. His lesson: "huge mistake, don't say the first number."
  - Diligence is brutal for the seller, with "the most painful 90 days." A $30-40M deal gets the same scrutiny as a multi-billion one because it is big enough to matter.
  - HubSpot's team said backing out after taking the deal up to their board would embarrass them. He had assumed they would walk or copy him.
  - Secrecy mattered because HubSpot is public, so he couldn't tell anyone.
  - He had just hired a CEO who hadn't started yet and had to tell him he was taking the deal.
- After the sale: he got cash and stock. He recalls the stock going from about $350 to about $850 a share while vesting, and he has not sold any. He still hosts the My First Million podcast, which HubSpot pays him well to do, and he promotes his own things on it.
- On money and happiness: "it kind of does" make you happier. Having money removes the stress of not having any, but happiness plateaus quickly. People adapt, and it's "never enough" even for billionaires.

## Hiring a CEO, hiring practices, and culture

- Stepping aside was easy once done. He feared everyone would quit or think he was soft, and the opposite happened.
- He says a good CEO often loves the admin he hates, so hiring one is the right move. Not every founder has to run the company.
- On culture: he once thought it was a weak word and now says it is important. Know your values and don't let in people who aren't on board.
- At Hampton he screens out entitlement and complaining about non-issues early in interviews.
- He uses the line "get on the pirate ship": those aligned with the values come aboard, and everyone else gets sailed past.
- Turnover is fine. Someone leaving after a couple of years is acceptable.
- A mistake he made at The Hustle: he told the team he wanted to create 10,000 jobs, then realized he didn't want that and lacked the nerve to say so. He tried to appeal to everyone and lost the juice.
- A strong leader holds the line on what the company stands for. Dave Portnoy at Barstool does that, and the people who are into it become more committed.
- He hated hard conversations early on and avoided them partly because he was young and lacked the tools.
- Hampton strategy for people:
  - Hire few, overpay, and aim for 5-10 year tenures instead of the roughly 18-month Facebook average.
  - He believes the business can reach $100M in revenue with about 50 people.
  - As a "collector of people," he hires the best and puts them in the right seat, because even a great hire takes 3-6 months to ramp up.

## Raising money, risk, and being "offensive" vs. "defensive" with capital

- "Should I bootstrap or raise?" is a bad question. Raising is rocket fuel and fits only if you want to build a rocket.
- Venture-backed growth math he cites: "triple, triple, double, double," meaning triple annually twice, then double twice. Example: $500K in year one, $1.5M, $4.5M, then toward $50M in about 6-8 years.
- Institutional money means expectations: an exit in about seven years, and you are one of a hundred bets where the firm wants 10-50x. Small seed money from friends and family is different.
- Raising money feels good while you do it, but afterward you carry expectations. This is why he prefers not to raise.
- Many founders who raise hundreds of millions earn only salary. One friend raised $50M, sold in an acqui-hire after five years, and made only about $125K a year in salary.
- Bootstrapped companies can take a long time to throw off cash. He guesses around 6-10 years before an owner can take a meaningful dividend, and restaurants take 8-10 years. Digital products can be faster.
- Be "offensive," not defensive, with capital. When his money was all in the business, he wouldn't let the bank balance fall under $1M and pulled back ad spend even when Facebook ads were working.
- His friend Austin Reef (Morning Brew), who has a finance background, treated it as capital allocation: put money into a machine that turns $1 into $1.50 as long as it works. Morning Brew spent harder when its customer acquisition costs improved, and Sam says that is when they pulled ahead.
- Now that he has money, he is looser and makes better decisions. His analogy is dating: when you don't need it, you do better. His attitude is: "if this works great, if it doesn't, I'm fine."

## Hampton: pricing, positioning, and the audience-led launch

- Hampton is a peer group for founders. Members are put into groups of eight at similar business sizes who meet monthly, like group therapy.
- Membership requirements:
  - At least $2M in revenue, or $5M raised, or a prior business sold for at least $5M.
  - A founder or CEO.
  - A digital-first business.
  - An interview, and "you can't be an [jerk]."
  - Average member revenue is about $25M, and the ideal range is $10-20M.
- Inspiration: Vistage, around since the 1950s, which Sam says sold to a PE firm for over $1B after about $400M in revenue and $150M in EBITDA. He argues community businesses run themselves once empowered.
- He used ikigai (what the world wants and will pay for, what you're good at, and what you love) to pick the business. He deliberately chose a business people dismiss, as they did newsletters, and plans a billion-dollar company without selling early.
- Pricing lesson:
  - First-time founders price too cheap because they want everyone to afford it, then can't afford to acquire customers.
  - His $300-a-year Trends subscription at The Hustle would need about 100,000 customers to reach $30M, which is nearly impossible.
  - Social apps need huge user counts, and only about seven are sustainable. Meanwhile, thousands of enterprise companies you have never heard of make hundreds of millions selling products that cost tens of thousands of dollars to a sales force.
  - Higher price lets him fund a better service. He tells would-be consumer app builders to map out how they'll reach 10 million users.
  - Higher prices attract buyers who want quality. He would pay $45 for a good protein.
- Launch:
  - He built it privately for about six months with one other person, at hampton.squarespace.com, and reached millions of dollars in revenue without his audience, to prove he could.
  - When he announced it on Twitter and his podcast, about 5,000 people applied.
  - The podcast gets roughly 150,000-200,000 downloads per episode, and YouTube adds more. Most of his time now goes to content because the audience builds the brand.
  - Paid ads are limited to bidding on the word "Hampton" on Google.
- He favors launching real businesses to an audience instead of selling ads: "more people should build audiences and sell real products." He cites the Kardashians and Rihanna as examples.

## Audience vs. community

- Sam says the terms differ tactically and are often confused.
  - Audience: one to many, like Nick posting videos or Sam hosting a podcast.
  - Community: many to many, in a specific physical or digital location.
- Test: stop creating for a month and come back. A community should have more content and interaction when you return, growing exponentially. An audience-led brand fades with its creator, as Casey Neistat's relevance did.
- A community helps you scale and sell because it doesn't depend on your name. People ask what happens if you leave.
- You don't need a community. If you are an audience company, be great at that.
- A community is like a marketplace and needs liquidity: enough people posting and consuming. Typical contributor rates are about 0.5-1% of active users.
- A forum on your own website is hard because few people will visit. Meeting people where they already are lowers friction. His Facebook groups see 10-15% poster rates. Slack suits professional communities, and he says Discord suits Gen Z.
- Reddit is a great community company but failed at media. The New York Times is an audience company with weak community-building.
- Community managers are a different skill set than content creators.
- He praised Nick's decision to pause building a Reddit-style forum inside his app.

## Real estate, renting, and ownership

- Sam bought a 20-acre property in Fredericksburg, Texas, Marathon Ranch (marathonranch.com), as an Airbnb-style short-term rental. He intended a fitness hotel but settled on a simple gym with a squat rack, dumbbells and a ping pong table.
- Real estate bored him because results take about six months to show and it rewards patience and spreadsheets. He hired a manager for 20% of revenue, which he calls a lot, and visits about twice a month.
- He says buying a primary home that doesn't cash flow is not a good investment. It is a store of value that grows with inflation at best, and early mortgage payments are mostly interest. Buy if it makes you happy, but treat it as an emotional decision, "just like you do with a car."
- He rents, splitting time between Brooklyn and Austin, and prefers calling a landlord to fixing things. He owns one nice car and says things can start to own you.
