## Sam's origin story and the Hustle path

- Sam is from Missouri and lived in San Francisco for a long time. He bootstrapped a newsletter business called The Hustle and sold it to HubSpot for tens of millions of dollars at age 31. HubSpot also got the Trends product and the podcast.
- He says The Hustle's goal was to be popular, so people know him more for that than for his actual success.
- He credits The Hustle partly to what he learned from Andrew Warner's Mixergy. He watched about 10 interviews, contacted the interviewees, "triangulated everything" and found his business that way.
- He says the first sale was "so much luck" and calls planning on a repeat of that luck "a pretty crappy plan." The longer the timeline, the less luck matters.
- Newsletters were seen as a joke when he started. He had the same experience with Hampton: serious operators don't take it seriously yet.
- He admits he got newsletters wrong in one case. When Tyler (Beehiiv) was building newsletter software, Sam told him it was a stupid idea. His reasoning was that he was already an investor in Kit (ConvertKit) and thought the market was too small for two.
- Before the first win, Sam describes the first five years of starting businesses as being "a loser." Google and an accounting firm wouldn't hire him.
- He credits a few decisions in his 20s for making the rest of his life easier: marrying the right woman, giving up drugs and alcohol, and getting his health in order.

## How Sam started Hampton and got it to roughly $1M ARR fast

- Hampton is a peer network for founders and CEOs. The average member runs a business doing about $25M in revenue.
- He and cofounder Joe Spiser first tested the idea on VPs. Sam tweeted a form asking who wanted their VP in a community and got about 200-300 replies. He interviewed about 60 of them and "no one wanted it."
- Joe pushed for CEOs instead. Sam thought it was "a horrible idea," then tweeted a generic version (a dinner in New York for CEOs), got another ~300 replies and called them. This time they said "Take my money. I'm in."
- They reached about $1M in revenue in two or three months with no real website. The site was a Squarespace page with a logo saying Hampton and a link to a Typeform.
- Distribution in the first six months was purely word of mouth and Sam's network. They worked on it for ~10 months before announcing publicly.
- He wrote a Google doc explaining the product and sent it to about 100 friends, saying "I am not selling you." He says he never tries to convince a friend to do anything.
- He called all ~300 respondents and interviewed 100% of them. At the end of an interview, if it seemed the person wanted in, he flagged it to Joe. Joe set up Stripe and found facilitators on the back end.
- Sam told accepted members "give me six weeks" and he'd build their group. His calendar in that period was stacked 20-minute calls with 10-minute breaks, and he bought a wind-resistant headset to take calls while walking.
- Division of labor: Sam did the talking, they collaborated on thinking, and Joe did the making.
- Sam says he can "blunt force most anything to like 5 million in revenue" through his network and brand, and the real challenge after that is making the product good enough that people stay and refer.

## Hampton's onboarding, product and group design

- The original onboarding: a Stripe link, payment, then weeks later an announcement email introducing each group. Sam wrote about why those eight people were together and "played it up" so everyone sounded amazing.
- Members went into a Slack, where Sam posted every day for a long time to set the culture.
- Groups started online because members were still traveling after COVID. Later members wanted in-person meetings, which Sam calls a big pivot. Digital is easier to manage but "way less good of a product."
- Each member is placed in a core group of eight people with similar sizes and types of businesses and similar-ish personalities. The group meets monthly. Facilitators moderate and are hired by Hampton.
- Beyond the core group there are hundreds of events and a network-wide community.
- Sam and Joe interview applicants and approve or deny all of them. Joe watches 100% of the interviews and clicks thumbs up or down. Sam will deny someone who is financially qualified if there is an energy or culture mismatch or something "shady."
- Sam says he turns down a lot of money this way, and it makes paid ads harder because "money is not just money."
- Sam's stated goal: "get eight amazing people in a room every single month and let them have the best conversations." He calls the business simple but "incredibly hard."
- Competitors and comps he cites: Vistage (sold about 1-2 years ago for roughly $2B, maybe $200-300M a year in profit, as he recalls), World 50 (nine figures a year in profit) and YPO. Vistage was founded in the 1960s and YPO in the 50s/60s, so he thinks the model can last 50-70 years.
- The moat is large, but he calls it a pain in the ass, logistics-heavy and "a very hard business."
- Vistage-style models are franchise-like: a moderator recruits about 18 people and earns on the 15th-18th. Sam says he's open to that but that the goal of this type of business is not to control everything. You build a framework and let customers' energy shape each group.

## Culture: speed to vulnerability

- Sam and Joe are blunt and confrontational and wanted core groups to be "confrontational with love."
- Early exercises included net worth reveals, where members break down their whole net worth for the group. It makes people uncomfortable but he calls it effective.
- He compares the meetings to group therapy. The joke (which he says is not a joke): "how fast can I make a grown man cry?"
- The key idea is speed to vulnerability: the sooner someone breaks down, the sooner they can be built back up.
- He likes that it shows successful people have flaws. Seeing admired peers be vulnerable makes him feel normal and empowers him. He says this goes back to hosting HustleCon speakers backstage, where he learned that big names are "mostly normal like me, but they still have the courage to move forward."
- Hampton is testing a formal "point of view" for the company, with a team member (Jory) sitting in on meetings to take notes in a room used as a lab.

## Picking a co-founder: the alignment doc

- Sam compares choosing a co-founder to marriage. With his wife he mapped out finances, lifestyle, kids and work expectations before marrying, and he did the same with Joe.
- He wrote about eight questions in a document, and he and Joe each answered separately, then met for an hour to review each other's answers.
- Questions included:
  - What does a win look like in 5 or 10 years?
  - What am I willing to put in emotionally, in time and financially?
  - What am I unwilling to do?
  - What values do we care about?
- Where they aligned: they want to win but also have fun ("winning without fun is silly"). Keep teams small, hire amazing people and give them lots of responsibility. Go big, meaning hundreds of millions in revenue, even billions, over decades. No venture capital, no plan to sell, and family first (e.g., dinner with family).
- They refused to build soulless or slimy products that make money without delivering value to the customer.
- The real difference was motivation. Sam says his comes from feeling inadequate and wanting to prove himself ("like a high school girl who wouldn't go out with you"), and he still feels that need. Joe's is pure passion.
- Sam says "I've done a lot of dumb things in my life. That thing was amazing," and he shared the questions publicly (searchable as "Sam Parr co-founder discussion").
- He first met Joe by emailing him after reading a Business Insider article, and said he'd be in New York the next day. He wasn't. He flew out after Joe agreed, and Joe invested $25,000 in The Hustle. Sam notes that people who do this to him now get told not to fly, because it's too much pressure.

## Lead generation and growth channels for Hampton

- Early leads came almost 100% from Sam's Twitter forms and his network.
- Current sources: his podcasts (My First Million, plus the newer Moneywise), referrals and Twitter/content. He says My First Million has been "so lucky" and will eventually phase out, so the product has to be good enough to be referred.
- Paid marketing started recently at about $10k a month, sending traffic straight to the homepage, with a join page for tracking. He calls it "not that sophisticated."
- At The Hustle he says he bought roughly $10 million worth of ads and let a team handle the rest, and he describes Facebook's targeting as good enough to "do it for you."
- Sam says Hampton can't grow for growth's sake: throwing people in "will ruin it."
- His current big goal is 10,000 members meeting in real life every month. Hard part: if five people sign up in a city this month for a core group, he needs three more. Decentralization is key.
- Hampton grew faster than he wanted. He says "I would like to grow even slower" and doesn't disclose revenue because he doesn't want members to think it's about money. He wants the company to last forever and deliver value.
- He sees AI making in-person meetings more valuable over the next 10-20 years, and Hampton as a base for that bet, with a 5-10 year commitment.

## Hampton's event tiers

- Sam lays out a ladder of in-person events, from cheap to expensive:
  - **Coffee hangouts:** a member visiting another city messages that city's members through the portal and the chapter lead helps organize. Example: a 20-person coffee hang.
  - **Evening experiences:** curated events like a sauna and cold plunge session at Other Ship in NYC, with a founder talk and group screaming.
  - **Adventures:** golf at Pimrod (a self-organized group that now goes yearly), rally car racing, lobster diving and a possible zero-gravity jet flight.
  - **Retreats:** multi-day trips like a health and wellness retreat in the mountains for about 15 members, spouses welcome.
- Both member-led and Hampton-organized versions exist. A member who owns a hotel can offer an at-cost deal for 20 Hampton members.
- Hampton's volume lets it get bulk deals. He says they ran about 300 events like these in the last 12 months.

## Building community yourself: do life with people

- Sam's community origins: he ran a business book club called the "anti-MBA," posting on Craigslist and Reddit. His first attendee, Sieva Kazinski, is now one of his closest friends, is in his Hampton core group, and has invested in his companies.
- In San Francisco he organized meetups constantly. His philosophy at the time (while single) was "I'm going to do dope stuff and bring people along with me."
- He was part of Founders Dojo (an office run by a friend, Dave, with free seats given to a hand-picked group). Alumni included Moiz Ali (Native), Roman (Raycon) and many others. He remembers an office full of degenerates having fun, with marathon 24-hour livestreams on Meerkat.
- His advice to young people: get in the mix. "Do life with them." Bring people with you to whatever you do, and be the one who organizes the cool thing (his example: book a group-buy winery trip and tell three great people to come).
- Early in life, the goal should be quantity of relationships. Later, he says, you keep a tight crew. His own rule at home is "no new friends."
- He has moved his family to New York to live near a Hampton core group member (Austin Reef of Morning Brew), because he wants to raise families together.
- Andrew Warner took him under his wing and "changed my life." He met Sean Puri (his MFM cohost) and Ryan Hoover in the same era.

## What Hampton changed in how Sam works, and the CEO reversal

- A core group practice: box breathing, to calm down before reacting to bad news or an email.
- He learned from a core group member (Val, who runs nine-figure companies) to stay unrattled: take bad news in, then ask what you can and can't get over.
- From Austin Reef he learned to rely more on intuition than data: "does this feel good, am I proud of this." Examples: he may know he can raise prices or optimize the funnel and choose not to yet, because he wants to treat customers like adults and not beg them.
- He originally installed a CEO because he was a bit intimidated by the opportunity and wanted to hand it off. He now thinks that was a mistake: "abdicating instead of delegating."
- He also thinks it was too early, because digital core groups turned out not to be the answer and people wanted in-person. He argues the person who can change fast is the one who would be fine if the company died, because it wouldn't hurt them financially. You can't ask a hired CEO to have that attitude.
- Joe is now CEO and Sam is president, a right-hand role. Sam went back into the business in March.
- Hard parts of coming back: working a lot, editing the team (he says he can do it but hates the pain it causes, "like working out"), and relearning systems like HubSpot, which had changed since he set it up.
