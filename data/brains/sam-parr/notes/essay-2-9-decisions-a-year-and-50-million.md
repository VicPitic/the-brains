## Sam's "2.9 decisions a year" stock-picking lens: few decisions, written down

Sam met a Hampton member who sold a company for about $10m in 2016. For the last decade his full-time job has been buying individual public stocks.

- Return: 21.8% a year, versus 13.5% for the S&P 500 over the same period.
- He usually holds only 4-8 stocks.
- His roughly $10m is now worth roughly $50m.
- Sam and the member put his brokerage accounts into Claude and asked how many decisions (buys or sells) he makes a year. The answer was 2.9. In some years it was 0.
- Sam's takeaway: "we often correlate motion with good results," but for the average CEO or person with leverage, that isn't the case.
- Sam's caveat: most people should not trade individual stocks ("Index 4 life"). He studies the rare outperformers' decision making because the habits transfer to business.

## Four habits Sam sees in successful investors that apply to business

Sam has interviewed many billionaire stock pickers (he names Ray Dalio, David Rubenstein, Lloyd Blankfein and Howard Marks, calling Marks a bond picker, "but close enough"). He says the successful ones share four things:

1. **They make few decisions.** He quotes Buffett: "Lethargy bordering on sloth remains the cornerstone of our investment style."
2. **They don't agonize over small decisions.** They split decisions into ones they can't undo and ones they can easily reverse. Most decisions are two-way doors and don't deserve agonizing.
3. **They know their circle of competence.** They say "no" often, not because something is good or bad, but because it's outside their area of expertise.
4. **They write their big decisions down.** They write pre-mortems and their thought process before deciding, then review afterward to judge whether their reasoning was good or bad.

## Pre-mortems and decision journals

- Pre-mortem method: imagine it's a year later and the decision blew up, then write why.
- Write the reasoning before the decision, not after.
- Review the notes after the outcome. The goal is judging the reasoning, not just the result.

## Reversible vs irreversible decisions

- Sort every decision into "can't undo" or "easy to reverse."
- Most fall in the reversible bucket (two-way doors). Decide those fast and move on.
- Save the deliberation for the few one-way doors.

## How the 2.9-decisions-a-year investor actually decides

Sam texted the investor to ask how he decides. His reply, quoted: "I study a few things I feel I can understand, and I study them very deeply. I keep very detailed notes and then just wait for an opportunity."

- Example from the investor: he studied a company extensively about 6 months before Covid and liked it, but it was expensive. He kept his notes, kept an eye on it, and bought when an opportunity showed up.
- The pattern: go deep on a small set of things, keep detailed notes, then wait for price or timing to come to you.
- Sam's observation: this matches the commonalities above (few decisions, circle of competence, written notes).
