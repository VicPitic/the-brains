## Sam's core principle: copywriting is understanding people and getting them to act

- Sam's definition: "copywriting isn't just writing words. It's understanding what people want and how to get them to do what you want them to do." Good copy gets what's in his head into yours and influences behavior.
- He uses the same tools in dating and sales. For a first date with his wife, he opened with a joke to grab attention (ADA: attention, interest, desire, action).
- He tells salespeople that in sales and flirting you can say almost anything as long as you make the person laugh.
- He recommends "restaurant owner energy": talk to a stranger as if you already know them.
- He cites David Ogilvy: on average five times as many people read the headline as read the body, so the headline is 80 cents of your dollar.
- Sam says most of what he writes about copywriting is "really just stealing" from Ogilvy and Joe Sugarman. He recommends the Ogilvy book because the less popular a book is, the less likely anyone notices you borrowed from it.

## Headlines: mind the gap and create open loops

- The curiosity gap ("minding the gap") works like music needing resolution. People can't leave a cliffhanger alone, so a headline should grab attention and make them read on to resolve it.
- Examples he cites:
  - The Rolls-Royce ad by Ogilvy: "At 60 mph, the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the electric clock."
  - The classic piano ad: "They laughed when I sat down at the piano, but then I started to play." (a course on writing music)
  - Upworthy-style clickbait ("You're not going to believe number seven"). A competitor run by his partner Joe went from zero to 200 million monthly visitors in about 6 months using this style.
- Open loops apply to short-form video too: stack one on another. A creator on the show adds a curiosity gap roughly every 8 seconds.
- The "you're reading this not because you want to, but because I want you to" hook is one Sam used often. It breaks the fourth wall and pulls the reader down the page.
- Tease what's coming ("what I'm about to tell you next will blow your mind, but really quick, let me tell you this"). Tony Robbins does this throughout his books. Sam says the same trick comes from pickup artist books: put a time constraint on the conversation so people relax and let you finish.

## Long copy, ugly pages, and the slippery slope

- Sam disagrees that attention spans have changed: "long means bad" is wrong. Long still almost always converts better; the real problem is boring, not long. "Ugly will almost always convert better, but it just has to be interesting."
- Affiliate marketers selling GLP-1 products run 27-minute video sales letters, and he says they work.
- His site copythat.com is about 2,000 words of black text on a white background and converts at roughly 5% of visitors. He says it earns a full-time salary and pays for much of his living expenses.
- The slippery slope, from Joe Sugarman: the first sentence exists only to get the second read, the second to get the third, and so on.
- It leans on the rule of consistency. Example he gives: ask someone to put a small sticker in their window, then two weeks later ask for a big yard sign. Compliance is far higher, he says roughly 40% higher.
- The goal is not for 100% to buy: "It's to get the right person to read all of the ad and a large percentage of them are eventually going to buy."
- The first word or sentence should "punch you in the face".

## Targeting a niche in the headline

- Name the specific person and they self-select in. "Niches make riches."
- Examples he gives:
  - BuzzFeed-style "50 reasons you know you're from Denver" gets about 30% of Denver readers to click.
  - Homesick Candles sold state-scented candles (e.g. Washington smells of evergreen) to natives and to people buying gifts for family back home.
  - Rolex's "The men who shape destinies wear Rolex" is his favorite.
- Hampton ads he runs say "if you make over $3 million a year, I know exactly how you feel" and then speak to that person intimately.
- Twist a well-known phrase to make it yours, as in "You may get lost, but not in the crowd" (a hostel ad), or Churchill's repetition and the "only thing we have to fear is fear itself" pattern. Sam recommends a book of phrase patterns (recommended by HubSpot's Dharmesh Shah) that lists many reusable phrasing structures, including alliteration.
- His most successful ad for The Hustle: "My friends think I'm smart. I'm not. I just read The Hustle." He estimates about $10 million was spent on it and that it was "singularly responsible for the exit." It originally used Mark Cuban's face, drew a cease and desist, then switched to Ben Franklin on a dollar bill.

## Sentence-level writing rules

- Write at a seventh-grade reading level. Check it at hemingwayapp.com. USA Today is fourth grade; the New York Times is seventh.
- One point per sentence. Where you would use a comma, consider a period.
- Start sentences with "and" or "but" to create rhythm.
- Vary length: short, medium, long, short. Good writing is like music, with rhythm that keeps attention.
- Warren Buffett explains complicated businesses like insurance in simple sentences. Sam analyzed his annual letters: average sentence length has fallen from about 25 words early on to about 17.
- Hemingway wrote profound things in simple language.
- Edit hard: write the first draft, then cut a third, and keep cutting ("killing your darlings"). Write with the eraser. Quote he shares: "write drunk, edit sober." Get the crap on paper first.

## Stories, the hero's journey, and objections

- Use stories: "There's no such thing as too long, just too boring." A story has a beginning, a middle, an end and some tension.
- The Wall Street Journal "two young men graduated from the same college" ad ran for 28 years and reportedly drove over $2 billion in revenue. Sam copied it for a $300/year product at The Hustle called Trends, and says it made a lot of money.
- Hero's journey framing: the customer is the hero and the brand is the guide. The Wall Street Journal is the guide that shows the way.
- Handle objections inside the copy, like Eminem in 8 Mile naming every insult before his opponent can. Write lines like "I know what you're thinking. Who is this person? Why should I trust them?" Put them in the copy or in an FAQ.
- Get small "yes" nods early. Open on something the reader will agree with.
- Failed-solution framing (from another speaker in the episode, not Sam's own point): absolve the reader of guilt for earlier failed attempts.

## Worked ad rewrites

- Sam says generic product-benefit ads ("the nightly drink for restorative sleep") are weak. For a sleep supplement, he would use proof (a before/after sleep score or a customer quote), or an educational ADA story:
  - Attention: "There's a scientific reason why after the age of 35, 75% of men wake up between 2 and 3 a.m." (he stresses he made up these facts for the example)
  - Interest: the feeling of waking up unsure why, and assuming it's just age.
  - Desire: it isn't your fault, here's the cause, here's the easy fix.
  - Then explain why other versions don't work, add social proof ("trusted by 100,000 dads"), and only then pitch the product.
- For glass food storage, he would make the shock visual: "Imagine storing your food in the toilet bowl", then claim that one-year-old plastic containers hold more E. coli than a toilet (he flags it as an unverified example and says he would verify the stat with research).
- For kids' clothes: "Would you put your kids' milk in a plastic cup and microwave it until the cup started melting? Of course not. But that's exactly what you're doing when you buy them polyester pajamas and put them in the dryer."
- Make numbers physical. "150 grams of fat" means nothing; showing the actual block of fat does.
- Bottom-of-funnel ads can still sell desire first and mention the price second.
- Use humor if it is part of your brand. Sam says it is one of the easiest ways to sell. The Hustle used funny headlines and a famous welcome email, written by funny people he hired.

## How to get good: copy work

- Copy work is the top method he recommends. Hand-write great writing word for word, like learning an instrument by playing Jingle Bells before writing songs.
- Writing it out by hand matters because "you have to feel the texture," and physical practice improves retention.
- What to copy: top ads, classic American short stories, best standup jokes, famous SNL bits and TV scenes, or whatever is considered the best of the genre you want. He has copied Gatsby, SNL scripts and the Boron Letters.
- The same method works for any skill: he recreated Kanye beats, and for design he recreates websites he likes in Canva.
- His advice is about 3-6 months of one hour a day ("copy hour"), then combining what you notice from your favorites.
- He keeps a 5-year journal, writing one entry a day, and says writing things down changed his life.
- Reading outside business is also a source of ideas. He steals headline ideas from Thrasher magazine and other print magazines. Read fiction too.
- He says that he is "not that good of a copywriter", just better than most.
- Two-sentence summary of his story hook: a viral Instagram video (about 4 million views, roughly 10,000 follows) showing his father's one-room onion brokerage that sells about $10 million of onions a year became one of Hampton's best-performing ads. The video's 3-second hold was about 85%, and its average watch time was 35 seconds.
