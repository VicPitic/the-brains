## How Sam launched The Hustle with a stunt post and content-marketing "side door" traffic

- Sam started The Hustle as a conference business. With no money for ads, he blogged about the speakers to get attendees, and it became profitable.
- He then decided to become a media company and announced it with a post saying he had $500,000 and would build the business "watch us blow all the money." He used a smug photo with his eyes closed on purpose because "it's going to get clicks."
- His second post was about a guy making $60,000 a month selling Kindle books on picking up women. The method: find top sellers, have them rewritten in the Philippines to get past plagiarism checks, pay $5 for a sexy title, pay for reviews, and put "best-selling author" on LinkedIn.
- To launch with a bang, The Hustle copied the method as a joke. It picked romance novels because they have the largest liquidity on Amazon (loads of new titles, readers who eat them up). It made a werewolf-in-the-military title, used Fiverr for a new title and headline, paid people to upload it, and hit the top of its category.
- Romance bloggers got angry and shared it widely. It hit the top of many subreddits, including the self-publishing subreddit.
- Harlequin, a major romance publisher, sent a cease and desist. A call with its lawyer resolved it: add a comment to the post, remove the book from the Amazon page, and apologize to the author.
- Results: with zero backlinks and zero traffic on a freshly bought TheHustle.co, the first week brought about 500,000 visitors and 10,000 email subscribers. The first year brought roughly 150,000 to 200,000 subscribers with no ad spend. He says "that strategy still works."
- Lessons he took away:
  - Look less douchey, because the persona wasn't him and he suffered the consequences.
  - Write like you speak, even if it annoys some people. Big companies can't do this because of committees.
  - Be "a missionary, not a mercenary."
  - This style of content works, so keep doing it.
- The Hustle still publishes these stunt-style posts every Sunday, hundreds of them. Often, not always, they go viral. A strong post gets 5 to 10 million views.

## The distribution-first content cycle

- Sam starts with distribution, before any article idea. The cycle runs: who do I want to reach, where are they, what medium do they come from, what is the headline, and last, what is the story and what emotion will it make someone feel.
- Most people start at the story. He saves it for last, though he often hears a story first (like the Southern guy in a Ramones shirt who runs a $30 million commodities newsletter with his son) and then works out the headline and where it would get popular.
- Audience locations he names:
  - Tech people: Twitter, which is having a resurgence.
  - Nerdy people: Reddit and Hacker News.
  - Fitness: bodybuilding.com or fitness subreddits.
- He says "most people... just say, we need users, write a blog post. Unfortunately it's not that simple."
- This approach is not search-optimized. It suits someone with no backlinks and no name, who can't rank. He says it can piss some people off, which helps the group who loves you become obsessed.
- His principle: "niches make riches." He appeals to one group of people and goes very hard on them.

## Reddit research method for finding post ideas

- Sam wants to know what his audience is already talking about and then make his own different version of it.
- The steps:
  1. Go to the relevant subreddit (his example is r/fitness, which has 1 million members).
  2. Sort by top posts over the last month.
  3. Scroll about 10 pages (about 10 minutes) to see what the group cares about.
  4. Pick a post, open the comments, and sort by most upvoted.
  5. Spot a highly voted comment to build on. His example: someone shows a kettlebell made from PVC pipe, which becomes a post on building kettlebells with PVC pipe for $8 from Home Depot.
- For the My First Million podcast he does the same on Hacker News: check what's trending, read the comments for the vibe (like people disagreeing with a headline), then form his own opinion.
- For YouTube, which they were starting the next month, he planned to use search data to see what people look for and cover topics where he has strong opinions or intel.
- Not everything comes from this. The rest of the podcast is whatever he finds interesting.
- Another tactic is newsjacking. When Steve Harvey announced the wrong Miss Universe winner, Sam zoomed in on the card, saw it was badly designed, and posted "don't blame Steve Harvey" the next morning with a redesign. It ranked on Google News and got 500,000 views. He says the goal is to be different, not better.
- Early on he had to tap into conversations already happening. With 2 million subscribers, The Hustle can now call things out and start movements.

## Converting traffic into email subscribers

- Sam called blog posts a "side door." Visitors arrive from Reddit, Hacker News, Facebook or friends, and the goal is to get them to give an email or go to the homepage. TheHustle.co is just an email capture page.
- He tracked daily visitors versus emails captured in a spreadsheet. Early conversion was about 3%, so a million visitors a month means about 30,000 subscribers.
- Pop-ups followed AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action) with unusual copy:
  - "Oh shit, not another pop-up."
  - "Now that I got your attention," then the post title the visitor came to read.
  - "If you don't like the email after a week, call me" (with his phone number), "and I'll Venmo you a dollar."
- He tailored pop-ups to the source. If analytics showed traffic from a Kindle subreddit, he addressed Reddit Kindle readers.
- On WordPress he used the Ad Inserter plugin to put a native-looking note at the bottom of every post: "you got to the bottom... I worked my ass off to entertain you... if you liked it, enter your email here." It compounded because posts kept getting traffic.
- Referral program:
  - Tiers rewarded 5, 10 and 100 referrals.
  - He knew one email subscriber was worth roughly $10 and set rewards so it stayed profitable.
  - Stickers worked best. They designed tens of them, and "people would do everything for a sticker."
  - A candle called "Elon's Musk" crushed it, but they had to stop selling it for breaking rules.
  - Most people reach the first tier of four referrals. Very few reach 1,000, but those few bring as many people as everyone else, and top referrers brought 30,000 subscribers.

## How The Hustle got a ~45% open rate

- The Hustle sends to about 2 million people a day. Unique open rate is about 45% and total open rate about 65%, which he believes is the highest he's heard of.
- Counterintuitively, sending more raised opens. It started at 28% sending once a week, then went to twice, three times, and finally six days a week.
- Why it works:
  - It's a daily news product that creates a habit.
  - It includes useful roundup content (for a health niche, "the 10 bullet points of what happened in the health world today").
  - It adds personality, like cute updates ("writing from my couch with my cat Whiskers").
  - It adds reliable extras like weather.
  - It uses cliffhangers ("Tomorrow I'm going to be in New York to meet with one of our biggest customers, I'll let you know how it goes").
- Deliverability tactics:
  - Move to a dedicated IP address once you reach roughly 10,000 to 50,000 subscribers. Mailchimp uses shared IPs.
  - Be consistent and send at the same time, never missing.
  - They paid Return Path about $40,000 a year to vouch for them with email providers.
  - Use Litmus before sending to catch spam triggers, like missing image tags or words like "free porn" when covering a story.
  - "The first, most important thing? Fucking good content."
- He segments subscribers as gold, silver and bronze. They know a gold subscriber stays about three years and study what those people read.
- Email is the last channel you can reasonably own. He compares building on Facebook to renting an office whose landlord raises the rent every three months.

## Business model, the exit, and what he'd do differently

- The Hustle was a media company with four revenue lines:
  - Native ads in the daily email, which Sam says would have been about $20 million in sales this year, an eight-figure business.
  - Trends.co, a paid subscription community with researchers and analysts, which he calls an eight-figure subscription business.
  - Conferences with tens of thousands of attendees, which would have made millions, but there was none this year.
  - The My First Million podcast.
- HubSpot approached them in October and closed the deal in late January, at about the time Sam turned 31. The price was tens of millions of dollars. Sam owned most of the company and raised no VC.
- HubSpot wanted the users, who might eventually buy HubSpot, not the ad business. It shut down the ad department even though The Hustle went into the year with millions of dollars of ads booked.
- Sam's formula for media value: "the quality of people who you have consuming your stuff, multiplied by the number of people, multiplied by your influence over them." 20,000 doctors who run hospitals can be worth more than 30 million 12-year-olds.
- His main regret was being too frugal because he grew up poor and treated the business money as his own:
  - "If you have a machine that turns $1 into $2, you load up a dump truck... and back it into that machine."
  - He hired a $50,000 employee instead of a $120,000 one who would have been more productive.
  - He wasn't aggressive enough with ads once the machine worked.
  - He didn't launch products fast enough.
  - He bought 35 employees' computers on Facebook Marketplace for $500 each.
- His lesson: he should have "defaulted to optimism." He thinks paranoia is good, but you also need confidence. He believes a path to $100 million or more in revenue existed.
- Because he had no money, he was forced to build something people liked organically, so ad money later was "fuel on the fire," not the fire starter. He says both ad-first and organic-first strategies can work. Ads on a weak product just fill a leaky bucket.
- His distribution reasoning: a good product with bad distribution fails, and a bad product with good distribution can win. His example is Heinz ketchup, which beats a better organic ketchup that's in no stores.

## Building a voice: copy hour and selling

- Before The Hustle, Sam ran a chain of hot dog stands in Nashville called "Southern Sam's Wieners as Big as a Baby's Arm." Hot dogs all taste the same, so he needed a shtick. The promotion: put your baby's arm in a bun with mustard and get a free hot dog.
- He read Robert Cialdini's *Influence* early and learned copywriting through a course from Noah Madora. He had been blogging since he was 18.
- Copywriting is "figuring out what motivates a human being and conveying or creating a solution that makes it easy for them to want to purchase it."
- His practice, "copy hour": one to two hours every day, hand-copying the best examples word for word. He copied *Catcher in the Rye*, Saturday Night Live scripts, movie scripts, the best sales letters of all time, and great blog posts.
- He compares it to learning guitar by playing other people's songs first. After copying enough, he said he could see the patterns, such as what Stephen King does that makes you feel a certain way, and then wrote his own.
- He wasn't an authority on tech when he started. He was a business nerd who lived and breathed it. He says media is hard unless you're obsessed with the topic, because "internet people have the greatest bullshit detector." He would not start a bone broth blog, even though it's a fine business, because he doesn't care about it. He advises against hiring Upwork writers for keyword filler.

## Managing writers and hiring them

- Sam says content teams are hard to build and that they hire quickly and fire quickly. They give new hires four weeks to show it works, and say so upfront.
- He hired editor-in-chief Brad Wolverton from NerdWallet (previously the Washington Post) to manage writers.
- On managing writers:
  - "Everyone's a diva. The best ones are divas."
  - They are often late, submit at the last minute, and don't know on Wednesday what they'll publish Friday. He says that's normal and often a sign of good ones.
  - Be patient. Let creative people create as long as they follow most of the rules, hit deadlines and hit goals.
- Example: a writer put "fuck" in every sentence of a piece about the Instagram account "Fuck Jerry," and Goldman Sachs, who sponsored the newsletter, refused to pay for the ad. Sam told the writer he supported it.
- His hiring method: troll Twitter, LinkedIn and publications he loves. He emails or DMs a writer saying he loved a specific article, follows up three weeks later describing the opportunity ("not nearly as prestigious as where you are now, but pretty exciting for this reason"), and says "worst case scenario, you've got a new friend in the industry."

## My First Million podcast: research and growth

- The podcast gets about a million listens a month. Episodes get from 30,000 to over 100,000 downloads. It's about two years old, and he expects over 100,000 per episode by year three. They had spent nothing on growth and were about to start spending heavily.
- He says it's the hardest thing he has grown. His caveats on why it's hard to copy: The Hustle gave them a built-in audience to tell, and his co-host Sean is "world-class."
- Episodes get more downloads when he does more research. They now have researchers, and Sam spends a full day building a big document before recording.
- The format: he hears a story (like the $30 million subscription business with two people), gets the numbers, deconstructs how it works, and tells Sean. Sean relates it to a similar business and they jump from topic to topic.
- He is always filing away ideas from people he meets, so he can connect them during research.

## Investing criteria and deal flow

- Sam runs a small fund with a partner who ran the viral site Little Things (200 million monthly uniques). He has invested $10 to $12 million in 40 to 50 startups.
- Since selling to HubSpot he likes subscription revenue and enterprise software ("so boring, but so lucrative"). The business needs to grow about 3x a year for the first two or three years, which correlates with a meaningful exit. He owns a tiny piece, so it has to get big.
- He looks for grit. He asks for every project someone has worked on and passes on anyone who jumps from thing to thing, preferring one or two long stays.
- He always asks how the product will be distributed. Does the team have a sales leader who can build a team, or a content-marketing person? He invested in Hiten Shah's company because Hiten is a content marketing pro who gets small businesses via SEO.
- Returns example: of his first 20 investments, a $15,000 check became $600,000 and a $20,000 check became about $200,000, totaling about $800,000. Until then he had invested only $200,000 to $300,000 of his own money, so two of twenty paid for the whole set.

## Distribution for a new product: the community playbook

- When asked how a home office accessories business should get attention, Sam said to start with Reddit, with Facebook groups as the second-best option at the moment, and not with Men's Health. Editors there get their ideas from Reddit and Twitter.
- Playbook:
  1. Join the relevant subreddit.
  2. Contribute genuinely, such as photos of your desk setup, a review of a chair, an offer to answer questions.
  3. Make about 10 posts that don't promote anything.
  4. On the 11th, either say you've followed the group for a long time, run one of these companies, and are offering members a discount, or share a problem you noticed everyone is getting wrong.
- Example of the second angle: "your elbows are in a horrible place on your desk," with a post explaining why and how to fix it.
- Paste the whole article into the Reddit post, then say the link is below if people want it. He says it "always gets tons of clicks."
- After you have traction there, email the big publications.
- His rule: avoid bullshitting, provide value a ton, then do your thing.

## Mindset and emotional management

- Sam says that 90% of the time things suck and 10% are awesome, and the 10% keeps him going.
- Most problems were about people, such as whether to fire someone. When the pandemic hit, he thought for the first three months that the business would go to zero.
- He quit drinking because he had a drinking problem.
- His view: building a business to $100 million in revenue is "very doable in most industries" and intellectually you can write down the steps. You do them consistently eight hours a day for ten years, which also needs some luck. He says the game is 100% emotional.
- His routine: meditation every morning, exercise, vitamins, therapy, self-help reading. All of it exists to keep his mood calm and steady, and he spends most of his non-work energy on it.
- Earlier plan: after reading *How to Get Rich* by Felix Dennis and a Mark Cuban story, he wanted by 30 to have enough money that it was never an issue again. He sold at 31.
