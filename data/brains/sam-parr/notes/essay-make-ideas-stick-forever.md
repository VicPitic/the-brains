## Making ideas stick: Sam's six-principle checklist (from Made to Stick)

Sam wrote this in 2014 as a cheat sheet of the book *Made to Stick*, mixing the book's six principles with his own examples from marketing work at ApartmentList. The principles: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Story. He keeps the post up "for nostalgic reasons" from his early-20s blog, The Anti-MBA.

## Simple: find the core, then share it

- Sam says to trim everything that isn't 100% needed and find the single most important thing, "being careful not to bury the lead."
- He borrows the military's "Commander's Intent": instead of detailed plans, state the goal (e.g. "take the bridge"). He used this at a meetup: he built a huge itinerary expecting changes, but made clear to the host that one specific goal held no matter what happened.
- He recommends the journalism inverted pyramid: most important point first, then supporting points, then details. It forces prioritization.
- The shared core should be compact and imply priorities so others can make decisions with it. Examples he cites:
  - A newspaper editor's motto "names, names, and names" kept reporters focused on local stories.
  - JFK's "put a man on the moon and return him safely by the end of the decade," versus the corporate version ("international leader in the space industry through maximum team-centered innovation...").
  - Southwest's "We are THE low cost airline" settles decisions like skipping dinner on flights.
  - Disney's "cast member" label tells even sweepers they're "on stage."
- Sam applied "names, names, names" to ApartmentList's Typical Roommates Infographic and Holiday Gift Guide. They deliberately named popular companies, and the people mentioned shared it most (each named company retweeted it).

## Unexpected: break the audience's patterns

- Getting attention is step one; keeping it is step two. Humans think in patterns, so break them.
- Sam's process:
  1. Find the core message.
  2. Find what's counter-intuitive about it, and why it isn't already happening naturally.
  3. Deliver it so it breaks the audience's "guessing machines" on that counter-intuitive point, then help them rebuild a better one.
- His warning: "The key to be unexpected is to avoid gimmicky."
- Example: a minivan ad that starts as a normal feature tour, then a speeding car hits the van and the screen reads "Didn't see that coming? No one does." It turns a car ad into a safety ad. He also points to Old Spice's Soul Patch Flavor campaign going viral on Facebook.

## Concrete: make it sensory

- Sam calls concreteness the easiest of the six traits to accept and implement. Something is concrete when human senses can detect it, and people "love real ideas."
- Examples he uses:
  - Abstract: movie popcorn has 20g of fat. Concrete: it has more fat than a bacon-and-eggs breakfast, a Big Mac and fries lunch, and a full steak dinner combined.
  - Michael Phelps's 12,000 calories a day is forgettable; a photo of the food is not.
  - An oral rehydration therapy promoter pulls out a packet and tells Prime Ministers it costs less than a cup of tea and can save hundreds of thousands of children.
  - Airbnb modeled each office room after a popular listing so investors, politicians and employees would feel that the site is fun and safe, rather than being told, countering the "couch surfing is for pothead hippies" image.

## Credible: five ways to earn belief

When you lack a true authority, Sam lists these:

1. **Anti-authority:** a dying smoker makes the anti-smoking case. A nonprofit that trains homeless people had a former homeless person drive prospective donors and employers, revealed later.
2. **Concrete details:** messages must have "internal credibility." Vivid details boost believability (a lesson from urban legends).
3. **Statistics:** use them to illustrate a relationship, not as isolated numbers. People should remember the relationship more than the figure. Don't decide first and then hunt for supporting numbers; let statistics shape your view, then share the pivotal ones. He calls Priceonomics "AMAZING" at this, citing their San Francisco rent piece.
4. **The Sinatra Test:** "If I can make it there, I can make it anywhere." A founder who has sold one startup finds the next raise easier. An Indian entrepreneur building a FedEx-style delivery service couldn't win contracts despite guaranteeing on-time delivery, until he solved film piracy deliveries and delivered flawlessly. Sam's takeaway: "Do small favors for people to build credibility before asking for something BIG."
5. **Testable credentials:** invite the audience to verify the claim themselves. Wendy's "Where's the Beef" ads claimed rivals had more bun than burger, which any customer could check.

## Emotional: make people care

- Sam's framing: the aim is not pushing tearjerker buttons but making people care, because "feelings inspire people to act." Build empathy for specific individuals.
- Three tactics:
  - **Associations:** link something people care about to something they don't. Watch for old associations that have become diluted. His examples: young kids avoid Facebook because their parents are on it; an anti-smoking ad ties cigarettes to big corporations, so smoking = "The Man," which isn't cool.
  - **Self-interest:** always speak to the reader, using "you" not "people." "No one cares about YOU...they only care about themselves." Don't say "we sell the BEST plant seeds in the world"; say "this seed will give you the BEST lawn in the world."
  - **Identity:** people decide by asking who they are, what situation this is, and what people like them do. Self-interest includes intangibles like self-esteem and duty. Examples: an anti-smoking ad built on not wanting kids to smoke; an anti-littering ad that shows littering hurts America and a crying Native American man rather than "the environment."
- He includes a teacher's answer to "why learn algebra?": "Never. You will never need it. But then again why do you lift dumbbells?" Algebra exercises your mental muscles, a means to an end. Sam flags it as combining surprise, analogy and a higher-plane appeal (learning, self-actualization).

## Story: collect them, don't invent them

- Stories make the listener mentally simulate the idea, which burns it in. Sam says the hard part is creating one, so "always be on the look out" for them, write them down, and save them. Most good stories are "collected and discovered, rather than produced de novo."
- Subway's original campaign was "6 under 7" (six sandwiches under 7 grams of fat) until they found Jared, who lost 245 pounds eating Subway. Sam contrasts the resonance of his story against the tagline.
- Three classic plots he lists:
  - **Challenge:** underdog, rags to riches, willpower over adversity (Jared; *The Pursuit of Happyness*).
  - **Connection:** a relationship that bridges a gap of race, class, religion or similar (the Mean Joe Greene Coke ad).
  - **Creativity:** a mental breakthrough or an innovative attack on a long-standing problem (Mentos commercials).
