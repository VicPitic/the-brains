## Sam's stance on whether ideas matter

- Sam splits people into those who do stuff and those who talk about stuff. When people DM him a launch idea, he replies "Awesome, send me the link when it's live" and doesn't read the idea.
- For talkers, he says ideas are irrelevant. Turning what's in your head into something you can see and touch (he calls it "manifesting") is a skill you build with reps.
- For people who already ship, ideas matter. One to three degrees off course early can mean a big gap later, like a rocket.
- He learned this from Kevin Ryan, who was an early employee at DoubleClick and later its CEO. Ryan funded about five ideas a year with $300,000 and had roughly two good ideas a year. The companies that came out of this include MongoDB, Business Insider, Gilt Groupe and Zola.
- Ryan's logic for raising money on a good idea: if you picked the right idea, you've built a cash machine where $1 in returns more than $1, so you pour money into it.
- Sam adds that you need to get quick at spotting patterns, because testing a bad idea costs hours of your life.

## Finding the "spark plug": learn the mechanics of business models

- Sam compares business knowledge to the "How Stuff Works" books. If you understand how an engine works, you know it needs a spark plug, so an idea without one should be abandoned.
- Good songs share structure (a chorus, an interesting melody, a bridge). You don't reinvent the structure; you add your own texture within the rules. Business ideas are similar.
- Every day he makes "a really small sketch of how a thing works" for whatever he buys or visits. He checks SimilarWeb on any interesting site, even a Topo Chico or a mattress company, and runs this on about five companies a day.
- He sees this as curiosity plus obsession, and says it also normalizes success: the more you see how things work, the less complicated they look.

## Back-of-the-envelope validation instead of spreadsheets

- Sam calls detailed financial modeling early on procrastination driven by fear. He fights the urge himself and says he has to "bully myself" out of it.
- Kevin Ryan taught him that you only need back-of-the-envelope math.
- His example for The Hustle:
  - Several people told him a high-quality newsletter audience can earn about $25 per 1,000 sends (he says "per 1,000 cents").
  - Business Insider reportedly had 80M monthly visitors for tech news, so a million interested people seemed plausible.
  - 1M readers at $25 per 1,000 is $25K per send, times 30 days, which he put at "around 10 million a year in revenue".
  - If it can make $10M, he figured he could find a way to $50M or $100M.
- His first check is who else is in the market and how big they are. A company at $1B proves the idea is physically big enough.
- He doesn't need a first-mover: Salesforce already existed when HubSpot started, and the point is that a market is big enough to pursue and you figure out the rest along the way.
- When a partner proposed building a revenue-model spreadsheet for a side project, he said he didn't care. His view is that models become necessary at roughly $30-50M, when executives need targets, and before that they can hurt you.
- Modeling is useful to avoid downside in a big business. If you're trying to maximize upside, it's mostly a set of assumptions that won't capture the crazy thing that works.
- Even so, he says new ideas still need assumptions (is 3% conversion too high, is 1%?), which you get by researching how others do it, not by building a model.

## Sam's research toolkit

- **Companies House (UK):** private UK companies above a revenue threshold (he says about $20M, possibly $10M) must file income statements, free and public. He found a VPN review site that owned several VPN companies. The company, Compare Tech, had about $15M revenue and $13M profit with 3-4 people and almost no marketing cost, and was acquired for about $150M roughly six months after he researched it. Lesson he took: search traffic in a rising market can unlock a lot, and a VPN with 5-6 people is probably a commodity.
- **SimilarWeb (browser plugin):** treat traffic numbers as directional only (divide or multiply by 2-3). He looks for four signals:
  - monthly visits above about 3M
  - traffic source (direct and search are interesting; social is less so)
  - time on site
  - which sites refer traffic
- **annualreports.com:** read the S-1 or annual report of public companies. Example: Tech Target owns about 31 niche sites (CIO-type sites) that sell leads from webinars and white papers to software vendors, with about 3M monthly visitors. The filing explains exactly how the business works and what's possible at that traffic.
- **Web Archive:** go back to a company's first months and see how it positioned itself, then pair that with news from each year. His example is Pandora, which started as software so Best Buy could recommend CDs in store kiosks, then became one of the first iPhone apps. He builds a timeline in a sheet and looks for the platform jump that drove growth.
- Other sources he lists:
  - Facebook Ad Library, to see successful advertisers' positioning and targeting
  - Moat, for banner ads
  - SEMrush, for search ads
  - Jungle Scout, SEO-style data for Amazon
  - Glassdoor, to read what employees say about churn, management and the business
  - newspapers.com, for press on pre-internet companies
  - LinkedIn profile searches, e.g. "HubSpot LinkedIn sales quota" to find typical quotas, or "The Hustle writer articles" to see how many pieces writers produce
  - reviews: Google "[company] reviews", Reddit, Amazon, Etsy and iTunes app reviews, to learn a product's strengths and weaknesses

## Example: turning SimilarWeb research into a tested business (erotic fiction)

- Sam read that Crunchyroll sold for about $1B. He put it in SimilarWeb and saw a "similar" site, Literotica, an ugly, 1999-looking site for erotic stories.
- SimilarWeb showed about 60M monthly visits and an average 17-minute session. His reasoning: even with only banner ads, that's a big business.
- To test his research method, he built a one-story site:
  - hired a writer on Fiverr for an erotic story and another Fiverr freelancer for an audio version
  - offered part one to read or listen to
  - ran $500 of Facebook ads
  - the end of the page offered $5 per month for part two and new parts monthly
- In one night he collected $500 from the $500 in ads, then refunded the buyers because it was only a test.
- He handed the project to a trend subscriber, who sold it about six months later for $500,000, after about $150K in revenue and a team of around five writers.
- He still thinks "Audible for erotic stories geared towards women" could be fairly big.

## Building a network by persistent cold outreach

- Sam found Kevin Ryan's email by typing "Kevin at [his incubator's domain]" into Gmail. He sent about 30 emails before getting a reply.
- Early emails were short updates: what he was building (The Hustle, partly inspired by Ryan's Business Insider), progress so far, and an invitation to point out mistakes. After about seven he got one line: "you're on the right track."
- When he told Ryan he'd be in New York, Ryan gave him 30 minutes. He says he flew there just for that meeting.
- He still cold emails and DMs people all the time, often asking for nothing and only complimenting them. Over time he built a reputation as a doer, which attracts other doers (he cites Dharmesh Shah).
- He keeps outreach casual, sometimes just messaging "what's up?" without a reason.
- He says he sends a few well-chosen one-line questions to people with perspective he lacks and gets a lot of value from a short reply.

## Keep new things weird and low-stakes; don't operationalize early

- Sam's advice to a $300M/year company that wanted a newsletter for non-converting customers: don't hire a team and operationalize it.
  - Hire one competent writer and send a weekly email to about 10 internal people.
  - After 3-4 weeks expand to about 30 people, then 50, including the CEO.
  - Don't act on all the feedback, since everyone complains about something.
  - After a couple of months, launch to customers.
- His reason: a hired writer who is nervous will write how she thinks she is supposed to write, not what customers want, and can feel she'll be fired if it fails. You need to remove consequences so she can take risks.
- At The Hustle he celebrated attempts that failed and used the phrase "let your freak flag fly". His rule was that you won't be in trouble for swinging and missing, but you will be for not swinging.
- He says boring companies are often the result of operationalizing too early and scaring employees.

## Everyone is afraid, so the real blocker is fear, and learn from direct marketing

- Hosting HustleCon gave him a view into 150-200 founders, including WeWork's Miguel McKelvey, Classpass's Payal Kadakia, Casey Neistat, ThredUp, Zapier and The Athletic's founder. Backstage they confessed fears: avoiding firing a bad employee for two years, doubting profitability, and the grind of renewals.
- His takeaway: most people are only a few percent smarter than he is. The difference is they act while still unsure. Feeling afraid is normal and shouldn't stop you.
- He distrusts people who make their work sound complicated. If he can't understand what someone does, he reads it as their problem, not his.
- He learned marketing on the Warrior Forum and in affiliate and black-hat SEO circles rather than from an MBA. He says you can learn from even unethical operators.
- He cites ClickFunnels as a company whose product is inferior to HubSpot's but whose direct-response marketing is very effective. He recommends combining direct-marketing skills with startup-style product focus and hiring.
- Long-form copy often outperforms minimal copy. He points to Hey.com, Basecamp and Apple's Kindle page, which is roughly 3,000-4,000 words. His reasoning is that with a new product you need many words to explain it, and a 1% conversion rate could rise to 5% if a few more people read.
