## Why Sam values being around more successful people

- Early in his career he started a weekly book club in San Francisco called "the anti-MBA", ran it for two years, and used ads on Craigslist to recruit attendees. It put him next to people who ran real companies, such as the founder who now runs a PE firm.
- At HustleCon he told speakers to arrive hours early for a "mic check" (not a real thing at conferences). Speakers included the founders of ClassPass, WeWork, Poshmark, The Athletic, and the OkCupid/Match CEO. He stayed quiet, listened, and occasionally threw in a question.
- What he learned: "they are not 10 times smarter than me... but they're like 10 or 20 times more successful." The gap in success is huge, but the gap in ability is not.
- Seeing it up close demystified achievement. He compares it to putting on glasses after having bad eyesight.
- He calls this habit "collecting people": finding 20-22 year olds who are getting after it and giving them a shot, and spending time with people doing interesting things.
- His value in a room is as a connector. He brings powerful people together and lets them talk.

## How to get his attention (and how he filters inbound)

- His inbox, and once his public phone number, drew constant inbound. He defaults to ignoring everything, since "usually people just want something that helps them and not you."
- Pitches that describe what you will do go to junk. What works is showing finished results: "I've already done X, Y and Z, here's the website and here's the results."
- "I'll work for free" backfires. He then has to invent work for you. He'd rather be told which problem to solve, with an example of the solution already built.
- His rule is "No New Friends." He assumes by default you're lying or selling, so you have to prove you're trustworthy.
- On second chances: "people don't change" and "the way you do one thing is the way you do everything." Late to meetings means sloppy. Lie once and he assumes you will again. He is very loyal once someone has earned trust.
- Example of the approach working: Michael and Simran won a viral-clips contest on My First Million (a $5K prize for making clips with a tracking hashtag), then built a short-form agency from it. They already had editors lined up and Sam's number, so they were ready when the opportunity came.

## Early Hustle growth: Reddit, Hacker News and stunt stories

- With no social following, Sam posted on Reddit and Hacker News. The first month brought about 800,000 visitors to the site, and he says this still works.
- The first 100-150K subscribers came in year one. Method:
  - Write a piece likely to rank in a specific subreddit and post it in about five places.
  - Example: a subreddit with 50-100K people followed Soylent, so he had someone live on Soylent for 30 days. It drew 500K to a million visitors.
  - A strong pop-up converted about 3% of visitors (roughly 30,000 subscribers per hit). He could do this about once a month.
- Stunt articles in week one:
  - He wrote about a man who rewrote Kindle books on picking up women (via Philippines freelancers, clickbait covers, paid reviews) and made $60K a month. He promised readers the next week's follow-up, which was a signup incentive.
  - They did the same: picked the most liquid Kindle category (romance), plagiarized a book titled "Captivating Claire", and reached number one in a niche. Publisher Harlequin threatened to sue, so he apologised and removed the book.
  - A friend's account of microdosing psychedelics (2016) also went viral.
- Sam says the moment they knew they'd "made it" in media was a defamation threat from a billionaire.
- On clickbait ethics: tell the truth, but the best possible version. Compressing details that don't matter is fine. "Bait" means a fake duck; give them the goods. Audiences aged 20-30 have a high BS detector, so lying costs trust.

## Media businesses versus products, and what he'd do about it

- Sam says it is easier to start with a product and add media than the other way round. He cites Car Wow (a marketplace), Hagerty (insurance) and Bring a Trailer as the best car content, made by companies that have money and "don't give a [___]."
- On media companies building tech products: possible but rarely done. The skills and cultures differ, and incentives conflict. Example: he would not offend a big advertiser if it cost his top ad salesperson (Katie, about $2M booked in a year). Promoting your own product also competes with ad inventory.
- Exceptions he names:
  - Craig Fuller (FreightWaves): enterprise software around $30M plus roughly $30M in ads. He also bought a flight magazine and built a residential airstrip community, using the magazine to sell homes.
  - Blockworks (information product with some data), and others. These are "exceptions to the rule."
- He does not think media brands are dead. Hearst and Fox endure, and Meredith (magazines) does about $2B a year, reaching Middle America. He pushes back on "this is the future" absolutes.
- On exits: media companies get bought because legacy players want the new thing on the new platform. When The Hustle sold he preferred a non-media buyer (he first thought WeWork, then HubSpot). He passed on Vice because it was all stock and a culture mismatch, and Vice stock later went to zero.
- Hustle sold in year four at about $20M revenue. Morning Brew, run by his now-friends and former rivals, is close to $100M a year. Sam notes they could have done it too, and that the business really compounds on a decade-plus timeline.

## Skills and low-lift paths for a young founder

- His biggest regret is not learning design (or no-code tools like Webflow). With design he says he could mock up a landing page for a software idea, cold-call, and get pre-sales or at least commitments.
- He is not an engineer and hires most of what he builds, but he can write.
- Writing method, "copywork": copy great writers by hand. It was common practice until about 1920. Like learning music, you copy existing songs and then recombine what you notice. It works for comedy, novels and ads, and he does it regularly.
- Why media personal brands get outsized: the niche was small, so being a pioneer (newsletters, five years ahead) stood out, and eyeballs are required. Media is "sexy" and rich people want in (Bezos and The Washington Post). Most people also think they can write or make video, then find it hard.
- His belief: distribution matters as much as or more than product, and second-time founders learn that.

## Building Hampton for decades, not an exit

- Goal at The Hustle: about $20M after tax by 30, a number a wealthy person once told him was "you're good."
- After selling he spent roughly two years "scheming" before starting Hampton. He used the Ikigai framework: what you're good at, love, the world wants, and will pay for. Too little of any circle gives you a hobby or a bad job.
- Core approach: build something that can last decades, because value compounds. He compares it to YPO and Vistage (founded in the 60s, sold several times, Vistage for billions). He believes peer and membership businesses hold up better than software, which must keep reinventing for things like AI.
- Brand lasting power: green and gold British racing green, Rolex and old Porsche ads as references, the name Hampton from a street he lived on. He likes products that stay unchanged for 100 years (the tuxedo, Rolex, E-Type Jaguar).
- Sustaining it: ask what people will always want. Connection with like-minded, high-caliber people, accountability, and feeling less alone. Same logic as Amazon's cheap, fast, wide selection.
- How Hampton works:
  - Applicants have a revenue threshold; no revenue means funding, or a prior exit. Digital-first. Some cities prioritised for density.
  - Five to six thousand have applied. All who fit on paper are interviewed; his partner Joe watches every interview and approves.
  - Members are matched into groups of eight with similar-sized companies, meeting monthly with an executive facilitator ("business group therapy"). There is also a member-wide digital community and industry sub-groups.
  - Average member revenue is about $25M a year. Examples include Andrew Wilkinson (about $600M company) and the CB Insights founder. Larger founders want to hear what's new from smaller ones, and smaller ones want next steps.
- Lesson from Trends, his earlier $300/year product: he priced too low. Big companies would pay far more (his example: a $30,000 subscription). Packaging for a different customer changes everything.
- Observation on growth: what got a company to $20M usually just needs a few small changes and another 5-10 years to reach $100M. Often founders just need more time.
- He chose to focus on one thing rather than spread across many projects, and he sees most peers as better off focusing. Having money changed his view: with a baseline secured, "the less I care the more easily it comes," and he thinks long-term.

## How to find your next business

- Write down the life you want: money, revenue, how you work, who with.
- Build a Notion doc of 10-20 industries and business models you find interesting. Research them hard for up to a year, and call the founders, employees and customers for behind-the-scenes information.
- Pre-sell lightly: see whether customers would buy, without actually building.
- Treat it as "dating an industry": talk to many, then pick one and commit three to six months to a single plan, saying no to everything else.
- He calls this "plotting" or "worry time." You don't need the answer immediately.
- If you're just starting out, take whatever works for a first win and financial security.
- Timing: most opportunities don't need you to be first. If an idea still has room in 24 months, don't panic about AI-style hype.
