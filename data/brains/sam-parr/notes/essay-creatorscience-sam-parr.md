## Sam's path from event to newsletter to The Hustle

- Sam sold his previous company and was "looking for something to do." He started Hustle Con (a San Francisco conference) "as a way to kill time" and expected the first one to lose money.
- Event 1 made about $50-60K in six weeks. After a three-month cross-country motorcycle trip, event 2 made roughly $150-200K against about $17K of expenses.
- He did not want to run conferences ("crazy high stress... so much that can go wrong"). He decided to build a content site and use email to make it popular.
- The Hustle began as a blog plus emails announcing new posts. Then he realized the post should live only in the email so people don't have to click off.
- It became a daily email on April 19-20, 2016.
- He chose email because he had seen it work for internet marketers since he was a teen. He recalls the long-form text emails from David DeAngelo's "Double Your Dating" list, which led him to buy a roughly $14 book.
- His insight: he could already sell with his voice (door to door, a hot dog stand). Email was the same selling, "but I only have to write it one time" and can send it to an infinite number of people.
- Many people thought the idea was dumb. An investor friend told him it was "not that good of an idea." Sam trusted his internet-marketer friends who were succeeding on email.
- Hacker News and Reddit drove traffic to the hustle's site. About 3% of visitors entered their email.
- For the first three years, roughly the first $500K in revenue, he spent almost nothing on ads (at most $500-$1,000 total).
- He sent the 3% frequent, interesting emails about the speakers. Blog posts like "How Buffer bootstrapped to $20 million in revenue" grew the list to 10-15K in a couple of months.
- He was 22-24, knew no one, and got attention with a plain site and good copy. Example headline: "The brown Don Draper is coming to Hustle Con," about his friend Neville Medora, then an explanation of what copywriting is and what attendees would learn.

## Copywriting as his most important skill

- Sam names the ability to control his emotions and copywriting as his most important skills. He says "most everything we did was rooted in copywriting."
- His method is copy work: copying great writing by hand. Musicians learn by copying songs and cooks by copying recipes, so you learn "the texture of what makes a good song."
- Once you see patterns, you know the rules, when to follow them and when to break them, and you develop your own style by stealing from many sources.
- He built a $50 course called "Copy That" with annotated examples of copywriting he likes and why it works. He charges $50 because people are more likely to follow through if they pay.
- Before writing anything, he decides what vibe he wants. He then picks an author or sales page with that vibe and copies a bit by hand to get into the zone.
- When he gets marketing emails, he often replies to ask whether the email is working.
- He says copying without data can mislead, since pages change. Replying to ask about results is his workaround.

## Asking questions and getting information from people

- Sam has asked questions since childhood. He never took for granted that buildings or companies are "just there"; a person with an idea convinced others. If he can figure out how that was done, he can repeat the pattern.
- He asks uncomfortable questions (revenue, cost) by modeling. He shares his own number first: "I made this much last year. How about you?"
- Intent matters. If your energy is clearly curious and well-meaning, you can "pretty much get away with anything." Humor helps, as in dating: "if I can make a girl laugh, I can get away with... flirting pretty hard."
- He triangulates instead of asking directly. Walking into a $5M house, he asks "do you have to go to a special bank to get like a jumbo loan?" and follows the answer until he knows how much they put down and whether they're stretched.
- He deliberately guesses wrong to get corrected. He says "What is this, like an $8 million house?" or "like a $2 million house?" and the person corrects him with the real number.
- Reading the person: if they want to brag, he flatters them and lets them. If they're nervous, he acts like what they say is no big deal.
- He says billionaires are not nervous-making. At Hustle Con he sat with founders of WeWork, OA Travel and ClassPass plus Casey Neistat, and they complained about the same things he did (firing someone, fear it won't work, stage fright). "You're not a million times different than I am."
- He was nervous only to interview Andrew Huberman and Ariel Helwani, because he admired them and wanted to impress them.
- Wealth alone doesn't impress him. He says that given 25-30 years of dedication, risk-taking and some luck, he is confident he could build a $100M/year revenue business in most industries. He is impressed by bold, courageous visions, with MLK and Jeff Bezos as examples.

## Research habits: biographies, footers and newspaper archives

- Interview prep is light: he tweets that he's about to interview someone to collect question ideas. He sometimes reads the guest's Wikipedia page and every citation, scans comments on their work, and avoids questions they're asked often.
- He reads one or two biographies a week. For each, he uses newspapers.com (about $10/month, an archive of old newspapers) to see what was said at the time.
- Example: for Dan Gilbert (Rock Financial, now Rocket Mortgage), he found the first mention of Gilbert, then read articles from three or four years before the IPO. The aim is to understand how people perceived him and how confident he was before the outcome was known: "this person wasn't that great early on," which he finds inspiring.
- He follows rabbit holes from small triggers. A huge building on a road trip led him to the website footer (copyright owner: Tyson), then the acquisition price, the founder, the founder's security company, and so on.
- He reads Wikipedia pages and their cited sources, and checks website footers (including advertising links for traffic numbers).

## Podcasting growth and paid acquisition for My First Million

- Sam says newsletters moved quickly, and starting The Hustle would be harder today because competition and acquisition costs have risen. The Hustle got to 100,000 subscribers organically before they started buying ads.
- He calls podcast tooling "still pretty shitty." Unlike email, he can't tell who a listener is or what they cost. They use Chartable (with deep links), which he finds hard to understand, and Megaphone.
- He is candid that he doesn't know why My First Million works: "I don't know... We worked hard on making good content." They had about 2 million monthly uniques around December/January.
- They had just started buying ads. Plan: Jordan Harbinger showed him how. Harbinger spent $400K in year one, $800K in year two and $1.3M in year three, and earned more than that in podcast ad revenue, so it was profitable.
- Sam's ad-buying playbook:
  - Buy ads on other podcasts, from about $500 to $5,000-$20,000, track which ones work, and repeat the winners.
  - Buy on podcast players too (Castbox, Spotify, Stitcher).
  - Skip agencies when starting. Make a spreadsheet of 30-50 podcasts with a good audience and reach out one by one, including swaps ("we'll mention you, you mention us").
  - Track results per source.
- Video: Twitter videos looked good but "didn't drive much views" and gave no ROI. About 95% of views come from podcast players and 5% from YouTube, and he is struggling to grow YouTube.
- What worked was a fan contest: $5,000 to one to three people (judged on views and creativity) who made TikTok clips from their YouTube clips. It produced about 20 million views in about 10 days, and friends started saying "you're all over my feed."
- Result: dozens of fan accounts, and the main account reached about 37,000 followers and 600,000 likes. Mistake: he gave two different hashtags (MFM clip and MFM clips).
- He would repeat the contest, expects diminishing returns, and would consider putting a few contest winners on retainer.

## Avoid building a prison: content creation and delegation

- His warning to creators is "make sure you don't build a prison."
- A daily email is a huge amount of work. At first The Hustle was built around him, which makes traction easier because people feel they know you. He couldn't sustain it, so he hired people and "can make money in my sleep." He thinks the product got a little worse as a result.
- Creators must decide whether to delegate or be the only person. Being the only person is fine, but know it is "grind mode" for years. Casey Neistat stopped daily vlogs, and the Paul brothers struggled with it.
- To make it big in content you need to ship every day, or at minimum every week. For most people listening, "you're pretty much gonna have to make shit every day."
- Ways around it: hire people, don't cover current events, and don't tie yourself to a daily schedule for too long.

## When and how to spend on paid acquisition

- Sam's mentor Kevin Ryan (Business Insider) gave him an analogy. If a machine turned $1 into $1.20, you would "get a dump truck, fill it with money and back it up into that machine." Paid acquisition is that machine.
- Start spending once you understand LTV, your cash flow cycle and how long payback takes. It took about 10 months to a year to figure that out, then he "started spending hard."
- Mistake: he was too frugal and should have spent more, because Facebook ads cost far more now. TikTok ads are cheap now. "Those windows close."
- Channel concentration follows an 8020 rule: about 80% of users come from one or two channels. For The Hustle, 100% of the first 100,000 users came from blogging, then Facebook ads (he could spend $1.50 to make $10).
- He compares to NerdWallet, which gets 80-90% of revenue from Google searches like "best credit card."
- Product Hunt-style channels (a bunch of small publishers) can be cheaper but need people to manage each relationship. One person can manage hundreds of millions of dollars on Facebook.
- Calculating LTV for a creator: measure revenue per post. Example: post to a million people at $300 product price with click and conversion rates, and earn $100K, so $100K per million. Test it several times, then work out what you can spend to acquire a follower.
- For ad-based creators: a million followers at $30 CPM is $30,000 per post. Divide to get revenue per user per post, and spend below that. Be willing to go a year without profit on an acquired user, and keep checking weekly that conversion matches your assumptions. Put ad spend on a good rewards credit card.
- Even a small craft business shouldn't guess. Know what you make per person so you know what you can spend.

## Platform picks and attention arbitrage (2022)

- Sam looks for arbitrage between the supply and demand of attention. LinkedIn once had more viewers than creators, so posts were shown widely and he got "millions of impressions" until the gap dried up.
- In 2022, his top pick was TikTok. It has a lot of supply but still a gap, because it's good at matching content to viewers.
- He also likes long-form YouTube (20-minute videos). A creator's ad value is roughly audience quality, times audience size, times influence over them.
- Illustration: 1,000 billionaires shopping for a 747 jet are more valuable than 10,000,012-year-olds in Missouri.
- Influence correlates with time spent consuming you. He just watched a 40-minute review of a Ford Raptor and now feels he knows the reviewer, so he'd buy whatever he recommends.
- He prefers the longer-form treadmill for creative fulfilment and because long-form is more evergreen than a TikTok that disappears.
