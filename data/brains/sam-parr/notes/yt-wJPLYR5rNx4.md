## Antler interview: failing to raise money and bootstrapping instead

- Sam tried to raise money for his first company (The Hustle) and failed. Investors called it "a lifestyle business." He was bummed at first, then thought: "that sounds awesome."
- He frames the choice by goal: if the main goal is freedom and making money for yourself, bootstrapping is "100% the way to go." If the goal is impact and buying a lottery ticket to go really big, raise venture capital.
- He says he is "anti-VC for me," not anti-VC. He supports VC-backed companies he invests in, but it doesn't meet his needs. He admits he may be "selfish": he cares more about his own lifestyle.
- He knows a founder who sold a company for $900 million and made $3 million after raising a lot and having four or five partners. The founder said it was worth it. Sam's takeaway: "then you just care about different stuff."
- He cites HubSpot, which bought his company, as an example of early dilution. By his recollection (he says he could be off by about 20%), HubSpot raised its first round at a roughly $5 million valuation and sold about $2 million of stock, so almost half the company went away right away.
- He says some of his friends sold VC-backed companies for hundreds of millions, while his own sale was "a rounding error" by comparison. He thinks he may have made more money than many of them. They may have more pride and street cred from building something huge.

## Cap table, growth pressure and why Sam won't raise for Hampton

- Sam says there are maybe only one or two mistakes in business you can't undo, and your cap table is one of them. Taking venture money means "it's my duty now to get a return," which is not good for every type of business.
- For some businesses, growth can really mess you up. He wants Hampton to grow but says you have to be more careful about how.
- He believes VC "would destroy our company." He points to Chief, a similar women's network that raised a reported hundred million dollars. He says its idea, branding and CEO were strong, but taking that capital changed the constraints.
- Hampton will never raise money. Sam says people message to offer investment and the answer is no, so as not to waste their time.
- He is unwilling to pay the price venture capital requires. Raising money, he says, means "signing up for a commitment" and getting a partner, and people often partner without thinking hard. He compares quick partner picking to arranged marriage: it can work and also fails a lot.

## How Hampton got to $1M in revenue before it had a product

- Hampton chose a business model that did not need outside capital. The constraint was: "we don't want to raise money, so what can we do without raising money?"
- Before it had a website, Hampton had about $1 million in revenue and roughly $800k to $1 million in the bank. The "site" was hampton.squarespace.com, essentially a Typeform. Sam invested zero of his own dollars.
- He messaged people like crazy and ran what he called a "zebra calendar": 20-minute calls with 10-minute breaks, so his calendar looked like stripes.
- They charged members up front. That made members more committed, and the cash was used to hire people to make the experience better.
- Sam's view: it is "a very easy thing to sell" but "a very hard thing to deliver," so the team works hard on delivery.
- Hampton's target is a big business ("I do want to build a big business"), but built without outside funding.

## Protecting the product: strict membership bar and customer acquisition

- Hampton gets roughly 2,000 to 3,000 applications a month, and many are under $3 million in revenue. Sam says he doesn't remember anyone below that threshold getting in.
- His co-founder Joe watches 100% of interviews and turns down about half right away. Sam says it costs revenue, but the bet is that a tight bar improves retention and growth over the next 20 years.
- Sam's view on audience versus advertiser: the problem with ad-based businesses is that you start asking how to sell more ads, which pushes you to be safe and avoid saying anything wrong. It is tempting because it makes more money early.
- At The Hustle, LinkedIn and Goldman were advertisers (some seven-figure contracts) and he sometimes held back a joke because of that. In one case, the headline "SoFi more like so [ __ ]" ran after the SoFi founder did something sexual-adjacent, even though SoFi was an advertiser. It hurt, but they published anyway.
- He argues that prioritizing the audience ultimately makes more money. His example is Barstool, which was unapologetic toward one type of person, probably made little money for a long time, and then advertisers came once the audience was huge.
- Hampton's top acquisition channels, in order: (1) content he makes, mainly My First Million, (2) referrals, which he calls the biggest after media, and then (3) paid ads, which are "way down" the order. They had only just started buying ads.
- The Money Wise podcast, where high-net-worth people open their finances (income, expenses, portfolio), is another media asset for the company.

## Using spreadsheets of biographies and constraints to pick a business

- In his 20s Sam read multiple biographies of a person and built a spreadsheet mapping their timeline: how much money they made and the inflection points. It gave him confidence about what is possible, the way seeing someone run a sub-4 mile does. He still does this.
- For new industries he reads about 50 interviews, then interviews early employees to get the real numbers, to understand "the physics of this business and how wealth is created."
- He used this to choose the newsletter business. His constraints at the time:
  - He could get about $25 to $50 per 1,000 email sends (he says $25 at the time, in his words "I think it was $25").
  - To hit his money-by-age goal, he needed a daily email, six days a week, for a niche with a $25 CPM and enough readers.
  - He then asked about his skills: he likes news, business and writing. Result: a daily business news email for higher-earning or aspirational younger people.
  - He projected roughly $100k in year one, $500k in year two and $1M in year three, and potentially $10M in revenue later.
- He says it is Ikigai and that he stole it: what the world wants, what it will pay for, what you're good at and what you love. He spent months on the exercise before deciding "daily newsletter, that's it."
- He once built a similar list for jobs: companies at Series B or C that pay well, have good parental leave, allow a normal-ish life, and whose stock could 10x. They interviewed and editorialized and sold access to the list, but "no one wanted it" (he says they may have executed poorly). He thinks the idea should exist, though it is harder to do now because companies like Lovable grow so fast.

## Lessons from 900 episodes: no single rule, design a rich life

- After 900-plus episodes of My First Million, his main lesson is that there are an infinite number of ways to reach the end destination. Silicon Valley rules (focus, MVP, working hours) can work, and he can show dozens of people who broke all of them and still ended up in a great place.
- Biography examples: Andrew Carnegie said he worked 8 to 11 a.m. and that if you can't do a day's work in three hours "you're doing it wrong." Sam's point is not "don't work hard" but to read the full story: Carnegie put in years of work first. John D. Rockefeller grinded, then at 36 went into "semi-retirement" and still became the richest American. Sam Zell worked around 100 hours a week until he died.
- On strengths: he believes weaknesses mostly can't be fixed. It is easier to change your environment and build systems that show off your strengths than to fix a weakness.
- He believes in "a rich life," a concept from his friend Ramit Sethi: get very specific about your ideal day, finances and what a full life looks like.
  - He did this with his wife while dating (six months in). Each wrote, in specific detail, what their ideal setup, routine and location looked like in 5 and 10 years, what they would and wouldn't give up, and where they'd compromise.
  - Example: she wanted New York City (her parents live there), he did not. They compromised on Texas first, with summers in New York. They now live in New York, after years in San Francisco.
  - He did the same exercise with a co-founder before starting a company: define success at 5 and 10 years, what you will and won't do (for instance, no sacrificing family time, no venture capital), and agree on the rules up front.
- On his own first companies: he started at 24, and his opinions changed. At the hostel he wanted to hire lots of young, inexperienced people because it felt punk rock. Later he preferred efficiency and more experienced hires. He wishes he had set expectations earlier.

## Sam's non-negotiables and a view on exits

- Hampton non-negotiables, set early and deliberately: he exercises every morning and has breakfast with his kids; no meetings before 10:00 a.m.; essentially no travel (he doesn't like flying or traveling alone). If a business opportunity requires him to fly, "either that opportunity is no longer going to exist or you can figure out how to do it without me." No venture capital.
- He says the first company is when founders say yes to whoever offers help ("whoever says yes, I'm into"). By later companies you avoid past mistakes.
- On what attracts investors or partners: he used the idea "treat them mean, keep them keen." Show a clear direction ("this is where I'm going, you in or out?"). He says that energy tends to attract people.
- On exits:
  - Timing an exit is "really hard," but getting it right can be the best thing ever.
  - He says many software founders who didn't sell around four years ago "probably should have." As an example he mentions Miro (reportedly bought for about a billion) and says it's not a great sign when a company like Bending Spoons is the one buying you.
  - Have a target price at which you say "mission accomplished" and take it. Selling for cash up front is rare, deals often have lockups, and he says cash in the bank is worth "like 10 times" the equity that could be. For private businesses, "take the money if that's your goal."
