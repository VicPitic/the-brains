## He opens with a "Missouri 4 to New York 7" glow-up and the point of the whole letter is that visible enthusiasm beats acting cool

Sam frames the essay as a glow-up story: he used to be "a Missouri 4" and is now "a New York 7 (roughly a Missouri 9)". The setup is that, at 16, he looked and acted like Napoleon Dynamite and couldn't get a girlfriend. He tried to fix it by reading every pick-up-artist book he could find.

- The takeaway he drew from those books was to be "cool" and act like he doesn't care.
- He calls that approach "dumb", and says it got him no girls.
- This is the foundation of the talk he gave at Inbound, "Be Chalant: The Art of Giving a Damn".

## He started openly showing enthusiasm in his 20s, and it worked on every audience that matters in business

When Sam started his first company in his 20s, he dropped the act of indifference and began showing more enthusiasm openly.

- Result in his words: "People were more attracted to me. Investors. Employees. Customers. Everyone."
- The principle: caring out loud is a growth lever across fundraising, hiring and sales, not just in dating.
- He treats detached "coolness" as a costly default that founders should unlearn.

## The Boston Inbound trip: what a "driver" actually looks like at his stage

Sam spoke at Inbound (HubSpot's conference) in Boston and uses the trip to puncture the image people have of a wealthy founder's life.

- He hates flying and hates being away from his family.
- His family uses a driver named Shah, who first showed up as an Uber driver. Shah owns a minivan with car seats, so he now drives the family to the airport for $175.
- For Boston, Sam paid Shah $1,300 for a same-day round trip, driving him there and back instead of flying.
- Sam's own description: "People have an image in their head when I say I have a driver. But it's really just my boy Shah in a minivan who wears a track suit with Pumas."
- The practical trade-off he is making: paying roughly $1,300 to avoid flying and to be home the same day, because time with family is worth more to him than the money.

## He tests content with a small subscriber pool and asks for replies as the signal

The essay is also an example of how Sam is launching a newsletter, framed as an experiment rather than a finished product.

- He says he's "thinking of writing a weekly newsletter" and that this is the second issue.
- Readers who recently signed up for CopyThat were put into his "testing pool".
- The feedback mechanism is direct replies: he asks people to reply if they like it and want to keep getting it, and to tell him what to write about.
- Before the paywall-style gate, the page promises a Monday letter to "32,000+ people" and asks signups to reply to the welcome email, even just "hi", partly to signal to Google that he belongs in the inbox and not in spam.
- The signup flow also asks an optional question (job title, company, company size from "Just me" to "1,000+") so he can "make cool stuff for people like you", which is lightweight audience segmentation at the moment of capture.
