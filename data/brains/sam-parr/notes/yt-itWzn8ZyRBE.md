## The five levels of making money, and what changes at each one

- **$10K:** sell a service, ask for the money, and charge more than feels comfortable.
- **$100K:** put your head down and repeat the same thing.
- **$1M:** the gap is fear and patience, not knowledge.
- **$10M:** the gap is people and systems.
- **$100M:** the gap is time (or capital and a great market).

Sam also covers copywriting, cold email and peer groups as cross-level skills.

## Level 1 ($10K): sell a service and ask for the money

- The easiest first $10,000 is a service. Find two or three companies that need something, do it free for a testimonial and proof, then charge.
- Sam prefers products to labor. His own first $10K was a hot dog stand ("Southern Sam's Wieners as big as a baby's arm"), started at age 20-21 with $500 in supplies and a borrowed stand he paid rent on at month end.
- The most common blocker is asking. When the buyer goes quiet after you say "$10,000", sit in the silence.
- $10K is nothing to many businesses. Sam says you probably need to charge more than you think.
- For businesses, the pitch is simple: how does your skill make or save them money? Hustling to $10K this way is "very straightforward".
- The first dollar stays exciting at any wealth level. The fear before starting is the same for beginners and veterans; the veteran knows it's normal and moves anyway.
- He compares it to a cold plunge or a professional runner: it hurts the same, the pro is just used to it.
- To find customers, use your skill. Sam used forums and Reddit because he could write. A service provider could cold call Yelp listings: "If I called 100 people, I'm going to get two people that are interested."
- Sam's "ikigai" check for what to sell: what the world wants, what it pays for, what you're good at, what you care about.

## Copywriting, cold email and follow-up

- Sam calls copywriting "without a doubt" the most valuable skill at any money level. It's getting what's in your brain into someone else's and moving them to act. "Copywriting is just sales."
- It also works if you're scared of people, because you can sell behind a keyboard.
- Books he recommends: Ogilvy on Advertising, Joe Sugarman's *Advertising Secrets of the Written Word* (he calls it the ad-a-week guide to copywriting), and Cialdini's *Influence*.
- He likes pre-internet direct-response writers because mailing letters was expensive, so they had to be good enough to get a stranger to write a check.
- Reciprocity example: he arrived 10 minutes early to buy a motorcycle off Craigslist and brought the seller a Coke. The seller asked $2,000, Sam offered $1,500, and he got it because he'd been nice.
- Cold email structure is AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action.
  - Attention: something that cuts through, e.g. "yes this is a cold email and it's totally out of the blue, but hear me out."
  - Interest: something specific to them, like an obscure old blog post you read.
  - Desire: what's in it for them.
  - Action: one specific next step, with an easy out ("if you're not interested, just say no").
- "Follow up or die." He emailed Pandora founder Tim Westergren 52 times before getting a yes. He treats silence as "he hasn't seen it yet", not rejection.
- Sam says five of his six groomsmen came from cold emails.
- Everything is a numbers game. Website conversion is about 2% at best, so plan for hundreds of conversations. His Mormon-missionary example is that door-knocking works through volume.

## Level 2 ($100K): don't overthink, then learn distribution

- Up to roughly $2-3 million in a business, Sam says you put your head down and repeat the same thing. $10K to $100K is just doing it more times. "You can be a sledgehammer."
- He says every business can reach $10 million in revenue and almost every one can reach $100 million, though it may take long or need a model change.
- Getting to about $3-5 million is straightforward and he's done it seven or eight times. Past $5-7 million needs teams, systems and leadership.
- To reach about $100K a month you need distribution, meaning some reliable way to get customers: an audience, buying ads, cold calling, cold email, door knocking, conferences, networking.
- Selling at the high end of your market is about as hard as selling at the low end, so he says charge more than you're comfortable with.
- His first conference had $250-300 tickets plus a $2,000 VIP tier. Someone bought the VIP. He made roughly $50K profit in six weeks that summer and then took a six-week motorcycle trip.
- He says he's never been driven by money; he likes turning an idea into reality quickly. His own anecdote of an early sale: Wealthfront paid about $8-9K to advertise in his newsletter, which he got by cold email.

## Hiring, leverage and one-person businesses

- After that first $50K he defaulted to hiring, accepting lower profit for long-run leverage. He quotes Rockefeller: "I would rather have 1% of 100 people than 100% of my own effort."
- Hire the thing you suck at. Founders should keep doing sales and marketing until about $5 million in sales.
- Once a company hits $5-10 million, he sees the CEO's job as: don't run out of money, recruit great people, and watch sales and marketing.
- Sam himself hires organized, detail-oriented "nerd" operators.
- Remote and overseas staff can be as talented as anyone, but he finds in-person co-workers add something.
- One-person businesses are possible. His Copy That course makes six figures a year and he hasn't logged in for eight months, but he doesn't find it fulfilling compared with relationships and teams.
- He says a great company is "a dictatorship" in decisions, though you listen to ideas and treat people well.
- He prefers a co-founder with the same values and goals but opposite personality. A partner levels you out, and he finds going solo lonely.
- Spotting a plus-talent hire: if they quit, would you say "have a nice day" or "please don't go"? He's had hundreds of employees across companies and about 8-10 "total killers". A killer tells you about problems you didn't know existed after solving them in ways you didn't know were possible.

## Level 3 ($1M): fear and patience, not knowledge

- The one trait Sam sees across the many million-dollar founders he's met is overcoming fear and taking the first step.
- The common failure is impatience and no systems or hiring. He knows a talented friend who repeatedly builds businesses to $1-3M, then gets bored after about three years and restarts. Sam calls quitting a working business a consistent habit of losers.
- What you sell matters less than staying with it. His mother-in-law, a Haitian immigrant, started selling decorative throw pillows on Etsy at about 55. She did $200-300K in year one and passed $1M about four years later with around 4-5 employees, spending only about $100 a month on ads and working two or three days a week.
- A million in a year is possible. His own timeline at The Hustle: about $350K in year one, $2.2M in year two, $5M in year three, $7M in year four, $12M in year five, and a planned $18-20M the year it sold. He says he could now reach $5M in year one with that business.
- For getting to $1M the gap is fear, not knowledge. You can Google how to do it, and he says courses help (Neville Madhora's copywriting course, Noah Kagan's "Make Your First $1,000", from which he built a poison ivy product, Itch Juice, that made about $2,000 a month for a few months).
- Lack of focus is the real objection. "Think of A, then B. Don't worry about C and D, but dream about Z."
- Revenue is not profit. A friend at $18M in revenue had only $25K in the bank because growth means buying more inventory, so cash flow suffers in e-commerce.
- A million won't fix your emotional issues. He meets sad rich people.
- Pick a business you'd do for about 20 years, since business is rare in letting you choose your location, colleagues and culture.

## Level 4 ($10M): people, systems and goals

- At $10M it's people and systems: the right people in the right seats, plus checklists. A VC friend (Jason Lumpkins) told him that once you hit $10M with the right systems, $100M is nearly inevitable given time.
- He thinks the market is big enough for $10M in almost any business, e.g. a laundromat via 100 locations, franchising, or licensing a lead system. He says you can often fix a stalled offer by repackaging it.
- Thresholds like $1M, $10M and maybe $30-50M each feel insurmountable and aren't.
- Sam sold The Hustle for about $20M-plus after setting that target at 24-25. He cites Felix Dennis, who regretted not stopping at about $20-35M, and the 3% rule.
- He says the goals he hit (a ranch, a Rolex) turned out dumb, but he's glad he hit them. His advice is to put your head down and achieve them, then figure it out. Better to drive fast in a direction than to overthink, like the pottery class that made 100 pots.
- To feel like it was real early, he put a large balance into a manual account in a budgeting app a year before.
- Pick explicit goals across family, fitness, finance and fun (faith if religious), "the total man". Examples: a quarterly fitness challenge, such as NFL-combine metrics like bench, 40-yard dash, broad jump and vertical, or a 21-minute 5K and a 300 lb bench in one hour, or a 50-mile run.
- With his wife and with his business partner Joe, he wrote down separately the life they each want at 30, 40 and 50, what they would and wouldn't do to get there, and how they define success. They compare notes and repeat the exercise every 6 and 12 months.
- Set an exit or success definition before you start, when you aren't emotional.
- Luck matters more than people admit, maybe about 80% of his own outcome.

## Level 5 ($100M): time, a hot market or capital

- Sam hasn't built a company past about $50M, so he's reasoning from others. He thinks the difference is time, "it takes longer than you think". His podcast took about four years, not six months, to reach 100,000 downloads per episode.
- Some people compress it with a wicked market where "the ball's rolling down the mountain", or by raising large capital and hiring the best people, like Mark Lore (diapers.com, Jet.com).
- Staying power: he puts himself in situations where he can't bail, e.g. "burning the boats", and having people (hires, a producer) depending on him.
- Decision lesson from a founder friend: when something fails, ask "are you sure you learned the right lesson?" The real lesson may be not to start the new initiative at all, because new initiatives reduce productivity first. Double down on what already works.
- Most business decisions are reversible (Tylenol's recall/tamper-seal recovery); cap table mistakes are the exception.
- Sort criticism into constructive feedback (your mic is bad) and hate, and only act on the first.
- He says high integrity is the common trait of successful people he knows. His partner pays vendor invoices the day they arrive despite net-90 terms.

## Peers, environment and Hampton

- Surround yourself with peers at your level or above. He says you don't need a mentor, "you could read a book".
- He cites a 1970s study (people around obese or unhappy people tended to become so) and MrBeast and friends working through YouTube together.
- Hampton's three C's: confidentiality, commitment, candor.
- He recommends being explicit in friendships: opt in, state expectations and goals out loud.
- Normalize success. Treat $100K as achievable and don't put money on a pedestal ("respect it, treat it mean").
- Hampton: 1,000-plus members paying about $15,000 a year, groups of eight meeting monthly with a trained moderator; applicants need $3M revenue, $3M funding or a $10M exit, with a four-step vetting and about 3% acceptance. He built it on the YPO model.
- Sam says he grows Hampton through retention and keeps churn low.
- He sets boundaries: he doesn't travel overnight since his kids were born, works 40-50 hours a week, and accepts he'd likely be wealthier otherwise.
