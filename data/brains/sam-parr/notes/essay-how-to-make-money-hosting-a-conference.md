## How Sam planned and ran Bootstrap LIVE, his first conference

Sam and his friend Teri hosted Bootstrap LIVE, a 3-hour meetup on bootstrapping (growing a company without outside capital). It was their first event, and Sam notes it was inspired by Noah Kagan's post on making $100k from tech events. He wrote it up in 2014 as a "how we did it" account, not expert advice. The event sold out at 120 tickets two days before it ran.

- **Purpose:** "to learn about the early days of bootstrapped companies," specifically the first $150k in sales.
- **Plan:** they knew how many tickets to sell, where to advertise, and who they wanted in the room. They sat down for a day, wrote the plan out, then "executed like crazy."
- **Mindset:** "Be ignorant, confident and over work." Because they rarely attended meetups, they didn't know most events are bad. They over-prepared and sweated the details.
- **Credit and caveat:** the event was meant as a test, with profit secondary. Sam says he'd raise prices or add sponsors and a special dinner if profit were the goal. They skipped sponsors so no one would tell them how to run it.

## Landing a "whale" first to create credibility

Sam's first step was establishing credibility, since ticket sales, PR and other speakers all get easier with it. They chose Andrew Warner of Mixergy as host: a talented interviewer with "a TON of clout." Sam had hung out with him a few times but they weren't close.

- **Make yes easy:** before asking Andrew, they built the Eventbrite page with speakers, schedule and location. They had no other speakers or venue yet. They listed them anyway, "knowing once we got Andrew we could get the other stuff."
- **Stand out:** popular people get constant requests, so they sent a handwritten letter and a $50 bottle of scotch. The letter spelled out "very, very, very clear" how the event benefited him.
- **Deliver in person:** they found his work address and Teri hand-delivered it. Andrew said yes on the spot.

## Choosing and recruiting speakers

Sam says landing great speakers was key. He wanted accomplished founders who were real experts, had a good story and were nice and fun, not "the VP of marketing of some silly company, a self-proclaimed expert, or someone bragging about being a 'growth hacker.'"

- **Lineup:** Chris Herndon (Apartment List), Nikhil Arora (Back To The Roots), and Neville Medhora (House of Rave, AppSumo, NevBlog).
- **Cold outreach:** the email to Neville was "funny and straight to the point," and it was the first time they had ever spoken. Sam also called him to convince him to come.
- **Accommodation as a perk:** Sam offered to cover accommodation, which meant Neville slept in Sam's room while Sam took the couch. Neville joined the Anti-MBA book club that week.

## Keeping speakers happy

Sam's goal was to make it so easy that guests "simply had to show up, sit down, and have a conversation (but in front of 120 people)."

- Printed Apartment List's and Back To The Roots' job openings on the back of the programs, since both were growing fast.
- Wrote a detailed itinerary for Andrew.
- Emailed every speaker the questions in advance.

## Getting a free venue

Sam calls this "pretty freaking easy." Most offices love hosting meetups.

- They cold emailed a few co-working spaces and all said yes.
- The key was appealing to the venue's interests: "NO ONE CARES ABOUT YOU...THEY ONLY CARE ABOUT THEMSELVES."
- The pitch: 150 entrepreneurs in one room would help the space land a new client or two.
- They picked Geekdom SF in SOMA. The venue also supplied free beer and pizza.

## Selling 120 tickets with almost no ad spend

Ticket sales were the hardest part, but the event sold out two days early. Weeks before selling any tickets, they spent a few hours writing down every place to advertise. Apart from $50 in Facebook ads, all promotion was free.

- **Free listings:** Startup Digest and The Fetch, where anyone can post.
- **Free tickets in exchange for promotion:** Ryan Hoover and The Bootstrappist.
- **Friends and cold asks:** Jason Evanish retweeted it; Web Wallflower was cold emailed by a friend; Neville emailed his followers without being asked.
- **Word of mouth** sold the most tickets. "If your event is awesome people will find out."
- **Timing:** Sam says most events sell 90% of tickets two days out, but they sold the majority two weeks before.

## The numbers: $1,940 revenue, $1,090 profit

**Ticket revenue**
- Early bird: 60 x $19 = $1,140
- Regular: 20 x $25 = $500
- Late buyers: 2 x $50 = $100
- Discounted: 20 x $10 = $200
- Free: 20 x $0 = $0
- Total: $1,940

**Costs**
- Neville's flight: $250
- Andrew's scotch: $50
- Facebook ads: $50
- Professional videographer: $500
- Total: $850

**Profit: $1,090.** (Sam's headline in the post says roughly $2,000 in sales.)

- The videographer was a deliberate investment: they expected to host more events, and good video should help future ticket sales.
- Profits were reinvested, like a bootstrapped business, to fund flying in the next guest.
- A commenter noted the math ignores the value of their time; if it took weeks, they effectively paid themselves below minimum wage.
