## How Sam decides whether a big career bet is worth the risk

Sam has studied risk-taking for about seven years: which bets to make, when to make them, and how to protect the downside. He says he is "far from being GREAT at it," but his process has served him well. He wrote this in 2017, in his early 20s, after watching people who wanted to leave cushy, boring jobs (to join a small startup in SF, or start their own company) get risk analysis badly wrong. His claim: most people are "HORRIBLE at measuring risk," and let emotions stop them from doing what they want.

## The downside-first process

Sam's method is to write things down and map the outcomes. The steps:

1. Write out the exact risk being decided.
2. List the possible worst-case scenarios.
3. For each one, ask whether you are willing and able to be happy if it happens.
4. If not, write a list of solutions that make that scenario "nearly impossible."
5. Only once you are comfortable with every downside and confident you can execute the contingency plans (while living a life you are willing to live), look at the upside.
6. List the positive scenarios the same way. If you like the upside, even a little, and can live with the downsides, "the solution is easy: do it."

He admits the process "isn't unique" and is "just common sense," but argues the process itself is what matters.

## Worked example: leaving a big company for a 5-person startup

Sam's sample decision: leave a 5,000+ person company, where you have been an accountant with little impact for 5 years, to move to San Francisco and join a 5-person startup with a product you love and a role with real impact.

Worst cases he lists:
- The company tanks after 3 months and you are laid off.
- You hate SF and want to move home.
- You embarrass yourself in front of friends and family who said you'd fail.

## Turning each worst case into a plan

**Company tanks after 3 months**
- Savings of $30k, spending under $3k a month, gives 10 months of runway.
- Take a $1,200 apartment with roommates, accepting a worse place.
- During runway, Uber or restaurant work is the fallback, which he says would make you unhappy.
- The better fallback is an accountant job at a large company like IBM or Intuit, which he says pays about $100k and isn't hard work. Start networking with their recruiters now, just in case.
- Test: "I'm comfortable with the above plan and know I can execute on it."

**You hate San Francisco**
- You will probably know within 3 months, leaving about 7 months of runway plus job earnings, and enough to get home.
- Ask your parents ahead of time if you can move back in for a few months.
- Tell your current employer your real motivation, end on good terms, and confirm you could rejoin.
- List companies you like in your hometown and make contact now, so a job is realistic within the 7 months.

**Public embarrassment**
- Sam notes that failing in front of people can be crippling, so you should plan around that rather than ignore it.
- Don't post on Facebook, Instagram or other networks until you are happy in SF and it is working.
- Tell only your employer, your parents and a tight circle of friends.

## Filtering out unrealistic worst cases

Sam's second technique is to list the truly worst outcomes, even unlikely ones, and go through them:

- "I'll run out of money and be homeless and hungry": not likely, so skip it.
- "I won't have a job": the IBM fallback already covers this, so it isn't worth considering.

The point is to stop fears that are already handled, or improbable, from driving the decision.

## Why writing it down works

Sam says most people let emotions hold them back from what they really want. Writing things down and mapping the outcomes typically gives people comfort. It also shows that "the downsides typically aren't that bad and the upside is worth the risk." For the upside he lists things like being happier, becoming rich, having no regrets in 50 years, learning about yourself, and meeting new people.
