## Sam's origin in copywriting and why it is "sales on paper"

- Sam started with a hot dog stand in college ("Southern Sam's Wieners as big as a baby's arm"). The product was nothing special, so he began Googling how to be a salesperson around 2010 and found copywriting.
- His core insight: if you can sell one-to-one and put it on paper, you can sell to an infinite number of people. He defines copywriting as "just sales on paper".
- He got hooked by early internet marketers. One example: David DeAngelo wrote 5,000-word plain-text emails and made about $30 million a year selling ebooks on picking up women. Sam admits it was shady, but he wanted to master the technique and use it to sell things he loved.
- Founders who think "ads don't work on me" are wrong. Human nature has not changed, and the same words still convince people to buy.
- Founders must first be convinced it is worth studying. His starting recommendation is David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising*, then *Confessions of an Advertising Man*, and Joe Sugarman's *Adweek Copywriting Handbook* (the title he gives is "Adweek Guide to Copywriting"), which he calls his favorite.

## Why learn from pre-internet direct-mail copywriters

- Before the internet, from the 1930s through the 1990s, people like Ogilvy sold products (e.g. a vacuum to a housewife) by mailing typed letters to a million households.
- That was expensive, so the letters had to earn their cost back. This forced copywriters to learn exactly which words get people to take action.
- Sam says these are the best teachers: "Those are the best places to learn from."
- Ogilvy started as a copywriter writing sales letters, then built Ogilvy & Mather into a multi-tens-of-billions-of-dollars ad firm.
- Sam says "Everyone says the same thing": the principles from these writers still hold today.
- His example of the same principles at work: Amazon and Apple product pages are long, with many photos, reviews and words. An Amazon book page has tens of thousands of words once reviews are counted.

## Why long-form beats short-form: the AIDA slippery slope

- Sam's claim: "Long-form copy typically is almost always better than short-form copy." Anyone who sells a lot of anything knows this.
- Reason 1: people skim, so a long page gives many places to grab attention and pull them back down.
- Reason 2: copy works as a "slippery slope" that takes the reader from interested to ready to buy. The framework is AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action.
  - Attention: grab it.
  - Interest: keep it.
  - Desire: make them want what you want them to want.
  - Action: tell them very specifically what to do next.
- The goal is the fewest words that are most effective. Often that takes a lot of words.
- Sam's water example: "Have you ever seen those big muscular guys at the gym carrying around gallons of jugs of water?" (attention), then a claim that water carries amino acids needed to build protein (interest and desire), then "fill up this gallon of water and drink all of it before noon" (action). He notes he made up the facts for the demo.
- Instagram influencers teach the same thing as "hook, meat, CTA".
- "There's no such thing as too long. There's only such a thing as too uninteresting." Long tweets and threads get shared most because they make people emotionally invested.
- The aim is not for 100% of people to read it. It is for the right 5% to read it and share at a high rate.
- Good copy makes the reader feel the writer knows their thoughts better than they do. His weight-loss ad example: "I know why you stand on the edge of your friends when you take a photograph with your buddies at the beach. I know why you wear that cover-up." He says to use this power for good.

## Sam's rules for writing clearly

1. Start with the big idea, also called the hook or angle. It is the one-liner you'd use to describe a movie to a friend (e.g. "a talking bear who's really vulgar").
2. Write like you talk. Write simply, at a fourth or fifth grade reading level. If you have to look a word up in a thesaurus, don't use it. The free tool hemingwayapp.com checks this.
3. Use the active voice. "The boy kicked the ball" is good; "the ball was kicked by the boy" is bad. Someone should be doing something.
4. Remove adverbs. He cites Stephen King: "the road to hell is paved with adverbs."
5. For a big sales page or cold email, pretend you are writing to one person and literally write them a letter.

- Warren Buffett example: his annual letters run about 18 words per sentence at roughly a sixth-grade reading level, and he uses simple analogies (the tide going out and who isn't wearing a swimsuit). Sam calls Buffett "a brilliant marketer".
- Grammarly and Hemingway help bad writers become good and good writers become great, and Grammarly flags passive voice accurately. He says ideas matter most.

## Using AI for copy, and training your writing

- Sam uses AI to find the big idea, not to write. His view: "it can never do a good job of writing."
- Example: for Hampton and conference marketing, he asked AI for phrases describing what it means to be around other high performers and got "the Michelangelo effect" (greatness is already in you; every great person you meet chisels away the unnecessary stone).
- Another example: for the "you're the average of your five best friends" idea, he asked ChatGPT for evidence and got a 1976 study of 20,000 people on obesity, poverty and happiness. He did not know the study himself. He says this evidence-finding is where large language models "have changed everything".
- Copy work is his main training method. He credits the old way of teaching writing. Like learning piano by playing existing songs first, you take the best sales letter or even a novel (he did it with *Catcher in the Rye*) and copy it word for word by hand on a yellow notepad.
  - Do it 15 minutes to an hour at a time. 20 minutes a day for 20 days will make you better.
  - Before an important sales page, hand-copy a great one. He names hey.com as a very good sales page.
  - You "feel the texture of the words", and that changes how you write.
- Social media gives instant feedback on whether your writing resonates, so use it as a testing ground.
- Writing is clarity of thought, and a clear writer is always a clear thinker. He worries AI note-takers let people skip processing what they hear.

## What goes above the fold on a product page

- Sam's rule: above the fold should be a feeling, not a feature list. Using TaxGPT as the example, he would sell the emotion (calmer life during tax season, "taking the terror out of tax season") rather than "AI tax assistant for tax professionals".
- Sell the feeling by intimately describing one the customer has. For accountants, the scramble around tax day, or a client getting a surprise tax bill they wish they'd seen coming.
- Find the lines by testing them. Pitch 10 to 20 customers and watch which ones make them lean in, like a comedian testing jokes in small rooms before the big stage.
- Founders fall into a "feature death march" (his co-host Jason's term, which Sam builds on): leading with the last three features shipped instead of the big vision.
- Words usually beat images, and video beats everything. He says copywriting also covers video and audio.
- For a small, early company, a founder letter near the top works well: "Hey there, fellow accountant. You're probably wondering what this is. Well, let me explain."
- Put a picture of the founder and team up front. The About Us page is typically the second most visited page on a site.
- His bet: with just a Google Sheet or Form and plain text, he could sell more of most things than a site full of fancy photos. His advice is to "lean into the personal side."

## Founder story as the strongest copy (Hampton's About page)

- Sam's Hampton About Us page tells a story about his co-founder Joe: Joe sold a company for $114 million at age 24, then built the viral blog Little Things to about $70 million in revenue. A Facebook algorithm change cut revenue from $60 million to $10 million just before a planned $100 million sale, and the company closed three months later. A photo from an Inc. magazine story about it sits at the top.
- Sam ends it with how that led to the idea for Hampton.
- His view: anyone can build a product now, so the founder's intent and the "why" matter most. A strong mission is a story (Palantir and Anduril cited as examples).
- Technical founders do not need to be on camera: "you could do all of this via a keyboard."
- Takeaway in his words: it's "rooted in compelling storytelling."

## Hampton: how Sam markets and structures it

- Hampton is a members-only community for founders. It runs on three things: an eight-founder in-person core group meeting monthly with a trained moderator, local chapters (e.g. about 190 members in New York) with events, and a Slack group of 1,000+ members where questions get answers within about 20 minutes.
- Membership is selective: only about 4% are accepted. Last month it got about 2,300 applications. Every applicant gets a roughly 45-minute interview, then Sam or co-founder Joe watches it, then the community votes thumbs up or down.
- Entry requires at least $3 million in revenue, $3 million in funding, or a $10 million company sale, plus a digital or internet business. Price for new members from January is $15,000 a year; he said earlier pricing was lower.
- Sam pivoted from digital to in-person after launch. Hampton hosted 150 events in the year, uses 100+ paid and trained moderators, and has a four-person team to find and train them.
- Business model: no outside funding. He wants it to last 50 to 100 years, modeled on YPO (1950s) and Vistage (1962, about 40,000 members at roughly $20,000 a year on average). He says it could bootstrap to a multi-billion-dollar company over about 20 years.
- Brand name: he is from Hampton Avenue in St. Louis, and it also evokes the Hamptons. His principle: "Brand is when you associate two things."
- MoneyWise (his podcast) works as marketing for Hampton. He noticed the most popular Hampton threads were about money (estates, spending, portfolios), so he got ultra-high-net-worth people to reveal income, expenses and portfolio allocation, and asked how they were "messed up" too. First guest example: Anne, who sold Solidcore for $250 million and has about $94 million.

## How Sam picks role models and plans exits

- He studied about five media people before building The Hustle and says he "just copied" Jason: build a media company around tech people rather than pretend to be a tech builder, then add conferences.
- He admires Felix Dennis (*How to Get Rich*, which he calls possibly the best book he has read), who advised making as much money as possible by about 35 and then relaxing. Sam's own goal was serious money by 31, and he says Jason's quick $30 million exit at 18 months fit the plan.
- He also studied Ted Turner. Sam loves that Turner was scrappy, made unilateral decisions on instinct, didn't take himself too seriously, and saved the buffalo population. Sam calls this type a "manifest cowboy": someone who turns dreams into reality in a shoot-from-the-hip way.
- On selling early: Sam says he sold The Hustle too early, since competitor Morning Brew is now at about $90 million in revenue. He had predicted $100 million within 10 years. Having "FU money" lets you play long rather than scared.
- Podcasts he recommends: Founders, and *How to Take Over the World* by Ben Wilson, which he calls "Acquired, but for people".
