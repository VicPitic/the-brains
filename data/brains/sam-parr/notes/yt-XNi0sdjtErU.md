## Cold email: the AIDA formula and why follow-ups do the work

Sam's cold email framework is AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action.
- **Attention:** a headline that stands out. For a speaker pitch he would leave the subject line blank, because "no subject" grabs attention.
- **Interest:** a short story or personal hook. His example: "Hey Tim from Pandora, I'm hosting this event in Oakland, which is right by Pandora's office. I'm going to have 3,000 people there, and you're going to talk in front of all of them."
- **Desire:** name the other speakers (or the people he is talking to about coming), "those are all ballers."
- **Action:** one specific ask. "All you have to do is say yes and I'll take care of everything else and you just have to show up. Are you in?"

His view of fear and volume:
- "You're 50 cold emails away from everyone on Earth." That means one initial email and 49 follow-ups. "The follow-up is key."
- To get over the fear of annoying someone, he asks "do you want to win or not?" He compares it to approaching someone you want to marry: if you want it, you have to do it.
- "No one cares about you. They only care about themselves." If you look foolish, they will most likely forget it.
- He thinks we will look back in 50 years and say "I can't believe you could just do that."

## Cold email follow-ups and expected close rates (HustleCon speakers)

Sam used cold email to book speakers for his HustleCon conference.
- Follow-up emails were mostly event updates: tickets sold (for example "we've sold out of a thousand tickets already"), other speakers confirmed, and "I'm holding a spot for you." The ask stayed the same: say yes and reply.
- Expected results: if he asked five people, he would "for sure get one" speaker.
- A yes or no usually came "by email three."
- He once emailed the Pandora contact about 30 times. Some nos turned into yeses on the spot because the updates showed momentum ("all right, screw it, I'm in").
- He has not used physical cold mail but would. He thinks catalogs for e-commerce and clothing brands are very effective, and Hampton has considered it.

## Copywriting: copy work, rhythm and plain language

Sam learned copywriting while running a hot dog stand in college, after finding Neville Medhora's blog. The technique that mattered most was "copy work": copying great writing word for word, the way schools used to teach children to write. His analogy is learning piano, where you learn simple songs and then see patterns well enough to write your own music.

What he copied:
- Ads from the 60s and 70s that ran for decades, on the logic that long-running ads were successful (some were 10 pages long).
- Powerful blog posts, books such as Catcher in the Rye, and SNL sketches. He copied anything he felt was timeless.

What he learned:
- **Rhythm:** a short sentence, then a slightly longer one, then a long one that lands the point.
- Use a period instead of a comma where possible, and make sentences more direct.
- Avoid adverbs.
- Write simply. He cites Hemingway and Stephen King (*On Writing*) as simple writers, roughly at a fourth-grade reading level. The goal is developing a voice, not memorizing rules.
- In lightning-round answers, beginners should follow formal copywriting rules strictly. He writes headlines as a near-full sentence and leans toward curiosity.

## Long-form copy and storytelling

Sam argues long-form sales copy still wins and has not gone away.
- Examples he points to: hey.com (Jason Fried), Amazon product pages (thousands of words, hundreds of images and reviews), Apple product pages, and a roughly six-minute deodorant video ad.
- **Reason one (logistics):** a buyer has many objections, and the page must answer every one so they are confident the product fits their needs.
- **Reason two (emotion):** "storytelling is the easiest way to make people feel something."
- Claude Hopkins example (Pepsodent toothpaste): "Rub your tongue over your teeth. Do you feel that film?" A simple demonstration story taught people to want toothpaste.
- Popcorn example: saying movie popcorn has a large number of grams of fat means nothing; showing a baseball-sized clump of fat makes the point. Sam notes he made the numbers up for illustration.
- A story can be short, even just a name. His examples:
  - The Anti-MBA Book Club, a free monthly book club in San Francisco with an expert on each book, started because he was envious he did not go to Stanford.
  - Sam's List: "Yelp for accountants." He asked his 400,000 Twitter followers for accountants they love, got 400 suggestions, called all of them, then published the database. The story is "I can't find an accountant, so I did the hard work for you."
- Write the way you would explain it to a buddy at a bar, and put those words on the site or in the name.
- Sam's List is a small business, roughly $300K this year by his estimate. He funded it with a small amount of money and gave half the company to the operator, Kimmy.

## Writing first, building second

Sam uses copywriting as a product-selection tool. He often thinks in reverse: instead of building a product and asking how to sell it, he asks "what can I write that will sell?" and then builds that.
- A line with a high conversion rate tells you the idea is sellable.
- Writing forces clarity. He believes Amazon has people write a memo describing a product before building it for the same reason.
- **Trends pre-sale:** he pre-sold about $60,000 of Trends before the product existed. The first landing page was on Gumroad and brought in about $10,000. The copy told a plain story: what the product will do, the full price he would charge later, a lower early-adopter price, a first version sent to buyers, a no-questions refund ("just hit reply and say not for me"), a free subscription forever if they love it, and a note that he might call for feedback.

## Going viral: make some people angry

- His rule: "If you want to go viral with content, the goal should be to piss off a certain type of person." Some people love it and some are angry, which is "oftentimes" what virality needs.
- Peloton's husband-buys-wife-a-bike ad is his example of outrage that probably helped.
- **Hustle launch article:** he plagiarized a romance novel, uploaded it to Kindle and made sales, to prove a scammy acquaintance's claim of making $60K a month this way. Middle-aged romance novelists were angry at a young Californian guy. The article got about a million views and about 30,000 new Hustle subscribers.

## Building The Hustle: newsletter math and bootstrapping with events

- **Why newsletters:** he did it years before newsletters were popular and was laughed at. He tells people he said it would become a $100 million a year company and thinks he sold too early.
  - Business Insider told him about 80 million monthly visitors, roughly half for tech. So a TAM of at least a million subscribers seemed realistic.
  - A Thrillist founder shared newsletter ad prices. A million subscribers and six sends a week implied he could make about $30K a day.
  - Facebook was cutting organic reach and marketers like Noah Kagan relied on email. His view was that email is the main thing and everything else is the afterthought.
- **Growth:** roughly 0 to 100K subscribers in year one, 100K to 500K in year two, 500K to 1M in year three, then to about 1.5M. It felt steady, not fast, at the time. Growth came in spikes from viral articles.
- **Operations:** the company ran tightly, with known acquisition cost per customer and known customer retention.
- **Events funded it:** he started HustleCon between ventures, and it made roughly $400K in the first 12 to 18 months by his recollection. After about a year of the newsletter he dropped the event, using about $500K saved from conferences to bootstrap the newsletter. He found the events business itself horrible.
- **Fundraising:** he tried to raise venture capital, and it ended up with a crowdfunding round. He regrets it because he did not need the money, and says "thank God no one invested" before he sold. He recommends fewer investors over more, and VC over crowdfunding if someone does need money.

## Choosing projects, hiring and focus

**Project selection**
- He calls project selection the most important thing when starting a company. He says it is "an art" with no math behind it.
- He sets an outcome ("if in 10 years I get to this spot I'll be happy") and checks whether the TAM and math get him within one or two multiples. If yes, he continues; if not, he does not.
- For Hampton he knows it is possible because other companies in the space are multi-billion dollar companies.

**Hiring**
- He says hiring is his core skill, more than operating. He sets clear performance expectations early, then lets people collaborate on the plan and gets out of the way.
- He uses "the bottom fourth of the resume" (education, hobbies, interests). He wants the person to entertain him and show passion and clear thinking: "if they studied four years of philosophy and can't tell me their favorite course, then they're a loser."
- He finds people through their writing, such as a blog, and does lots of outbound even though each job listing now gets about 500 applicants.
- His outreach is simply "I love your work." His goal is to "buy stock in people before it's matured" and give them a platform. He tells hires to stay three to five years and promises support if they move on.

**Time and structure**
- He says he has one company, Hampton. Sam's List runs by Kimmy with a half stake. He hosted Moneywise (Hampton's podcast) for its first year and then hired a host.
- My First Million takes Monday and Wednesday, 12 to 2 with 2 hours of prep from 10, and the rest of his time is Hampton.

## Bunk SF, tooling and skills Sam wishes he had

**Bunk SF (roommate matching)**
- Mechanic: get a landlord's permission to host a party in a three-bedroom apartment, invite about 100 loosely vetted people (similar age, work, neighborhood and budget), and match them into groups that then find their own place.
- Acquisition: post a $1,000 one-bedroom Craigslist ad framed as filling an empty $3,000 three-bedroom. It brought thousands of applicants a day until Craigslist shut it down. He says Airbnb grew similarly. Sam calls it a hard, dumb business because the customers have no money.
- Craigslist's old office later became The Hustle's first office.

**What he would do differently**
- He would learn to code and design. Even with tools like Replit and Cursor, he says you still need to be technical.
- He wishes he had paid attention to Photoshop or front-end design in high school, because good design matters to copywriting.
- He says he has good taste and sets direction for Hampton's branding by drawing and making a deck of colors and feelings, but cannot finish the details himself.

**Lightning round**
- Headlines: curiosity.
- Communities: growing slowly can make them last forever if done right.
- Clicks vs. connection: he can get anyone to click, but prefers connection since clicks don't convert.
- Paid or value commitments: paid.
