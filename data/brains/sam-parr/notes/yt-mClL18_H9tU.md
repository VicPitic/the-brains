## Sam's experience selling The Hustle to HubSpot

- HubSpot cold emailed Sam in October. The deal was announced on the last day of January, about 90 days from first contact to close.
- Sam had received buyer approaches for a long time. Early on he took them seriously and prepared; later he sent a one-pager listing why a buyer should and should not buy The Hustle, plus the pricing expectation, then "if you want to talk, call me." He says most people don't do this and HubSpot liked it.
- Due diligence was painful. He had a spreadsheet with about eight tabs (real estate, IT, revenue and P&L, daily email, trends and others), each with roughly 150 questions. He estimates he gave them 500 to 700 items, including things like aging accounts receivable by year for five years.
- His support was thin: one finance person (Edie), an outside law firm, an outside tax firm, and himself. He had to do much of it in private because employees couldn't be told.
- He notes founders and CEOs push to close fast, while the people doing the work need to button everything up carefully.
- He declined to disclose the price. His reasoning: he thinks it's weird that anyone can Google the HubSpot CEO's salary and share sales, and the CEO agreed. He won't confirm or deny press reports of the figure.
- Why he sold: he had raised a little angel money and felt a duty to return it to investors, and the deal let him hit a personal goal of making enough by about 35 to live lavishly and leave a large sum to his children. He says he now never wants to sell anything again and wants to own things forever.
- A serious illness (Lyme disease with facial paralysis) made him want enough wealth that an emergency wouldn't ruin him.
- He structured the business early so that employee profit sharing was nearly impossible to reverse later, and wishes he had set it up differently.

## How The Hustle ran with Sam hands-off

- Just before the sale he had made an offer to a CEO hire, which he rescinded when the HubSpot offer came.
- His operating pattern: start something, then hand it off. He sold the first $150,000 of advertising himself, hired a salesperson, and never sold again.
- He sets revenue targets with the sales team and growth targets with the growth team, then leaves them alone. If a team struggles, they ask for help and he drops in with introductions, creative ideas, or his personal brand.
- For new products he locks himself in a room and does it nearly alone. For Trends he took the first $50,000 in pre-sales on Gumroad and personally called customers to ask why they bought. Once it showed signs of working, he handed it to more skilled operators.
- His view: most people are bad at starting things, so use the few who are good at it for new stuff and put others on running it. He says a company that gets ADD can be ruined, and that he'd be bad at day-to-day operations.
- On hiring: early on you can only afford a $40,000 person who is smart but unskilled, or driven but inexperienced. Once bigger, hire people who have done the exact job before. He recounts Andrew Wilkinson's advice: "just go find someone who's already done this before," like hiring a mechanic who is a good mechanic rather than a good electrician.
- He enforces a rule that no one writes in his name without his approval, e.g. a re-engagement email sent as "Sam".

## Growing the list to nearly 2M subscribers

- Scale at the sale: close to 2 million subscribers, tens of thousands of paying subscribers, and about 900,000 opens on a recent send.
- Email's advantage: with a known unsubscribe rate (e.g. 5% a month) you can compute churn and LTV and therefore what you can spend to acquire a subscriber. He says you can't do that on a website.
- A media executive he admired once told him the business would never exceed $2 million a year and that they should do video instead. He disagreed.
- Acquisition is as important as content: "people think if you have good content, you're going to make it. Acquiring users is as important as the content."
- Paid acquisition got harder: Facebook subscribers cost about $1 four years earlier and about $3 to $4 at the time of recording.
- Co-registration (co-reg) is a major channel, with about 100 websites. Example: after someone submits a job application, the site offers "do you want to sign up for The Hustle too? Just click here" using the email it already has. Product Hunt does the same, listing The Hustle or Trends with a one-click signup.
- Co-reg requires building tooling, unlike Facebook which is plug and play.
- At higher spend (tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars a month), he negotiates quality-based pricing: pay $1 per lead only if the subscriber is a power user, e.g. opens at a 75% rate over the first 10 days. This guards against bot signups.
- Daily-newsletter peers he cites: The Skimm and Morning Brew. He says there was no playbook and they figured it out themselves.
- He says copying them isn't the right move; try something that seems silly now and may be normal in a few years.
- For solo creators like James Clear, tactics aren't that different. The main difference is that the founder-operator life is miserable: he was writer, growth marketer, admin and accountant at the start, and a daily email is very hard. A sellable asset requires interchangeable people.

## Subscription pricing and paid newsletters

- Trends at the sale had over 15,000 paying subscribers, and was on track for close to eight figures. He originally planned a company with advertising bootstrapping a later product, inspired by Nathan's "billion dollar blog" idea.
- Trends came from his research on which products to build: friends loved the research and asked for more, then a Facebook group where members shared their own research showed community plus research was the product.
- Financials: on track for about $20M to $22M in 2021 net cash receipts, with advertising roughly $14M to $15M. Ad revenue was over $1M a month.
- A Black Friday campaign drove about $800,000 in a day, around 3,000 customers at a price he describes as about $200 to $300 (he says "200" then does the math with 300).
- Reason for subscriptions: advertising-only media limits what you can say, because advertisers can pull their ads.
- Renewal rate on Trends is very high; he says they undercharge. They tested tripling the price and conversion stayed the same.
- His advice for paid newsletter creators:
  - Charge much more. Most charge too little (e.g. $4 a month). The difference between $99 and $299 a year likely won't change conversion, and likely not $299 to $499 either. People value what they paid more for.
  - Prefer annual billing only. Churn on content is higher than software, and monthly at $9 to $15 is rough. If you offer monthly, make it very expensive (e.g. $50 a month vs $250 a year) as an anchor.
  - He never offered monthly on Trends. He spoke with The Information, The Athletic, The New York Times and Motley Fool, who mostly push people to annual.
  - Make your homepage a long-form sales page, not a freebie-heavy page. Only The New York Times can get away with that. No freemium; they use a $1 trial.
  - Require an email to reach the checkout page. Conversion is around 3%, but about 10% to 12% will give an email, and nearly all would-be buyers still proceed. That builds a lead list to nurture with free value.
- He says to study the scrappiest commercial operators, ethically: Agora (about $1B a year selling newsletters, though sued over a diabetes cure book), Motley Fool (he guesses around half a billion in sales a year) and James Altucher. Ask why they win, and apply the tactics to a high-integrity product.

## Email promotion tactics and where email is going

- "Accidental send" Black Friday email: an email styled like an internal Gmail thread where Sam tells the team the Black Friday sale is ready and he thinks the discount is too big, sent to the full list from his personal address. Tens of thousands of people hit the site, he got about 10,000 replies, and it was a record sales day. He says the idea was borrowed from Chubbies or Brooklinen. Even friends called to say it was sent to the whole list by mistake.
- An earlier version in 2014/2015: they accidentally resent the previous email, then followed up with a screenshot of a Slack exchange where he threatens the writer's job; it got their highest open rate.
- His metaphor: "Email is like your pirate ship, and every new subscriber is a little bit of wind in your sails."
- His predictions: an email client such as Hey or Superhuman could gain enough traction to change how we use email. He expects richer email, with purchases, video, swipe, and live video inline. He thinks a news outlet like The New York Times might not need a website, and breaking news (e.g. election results) could go out in real time rather than at 8am.
- He notes AMP for email exists but nobody uses it.

## Learning to write through copy work

- Method: copy great writing word for word, by hand. He compares it to learning an instrument by playing songs before writing your own.
- He copied Hunter S. Thompson, J.D. Salinger's Catcher in the Rye (simple language, interesting storytelling), and long-form advertisements to learn copywriting and selling.
- He says Judd Apatow wrote out Woody Allen scripts and Saturday Night Live sketches word for word.
- He prefers handwriting because it's slower than typing, which forces attention. After enough of it, you start noticing why a piece works: opening lines, sentence rhythm, simple words for complex topics.
- Breaking a pattern like Ryan Holiday's one-sentence introductions can be learned by writing a segment out 10 to 30 times.
- General principle: remove emotion and decision fatigue from learning a skill by copying what already worked. He applies it to training (hires a coach to write a plan, sprinter training, 5x5 lifting) and eating (a personal chef, 1,800 calories a day).

## Launching a local newsletter and growing it

- For Nathan's Boise newsletter, Sam says a $25,000 budget is fine and "badass."
- Growth tactic: publish a listicle every 3 days such as "You know you're from Boise if..." and "The eight stereotypes of Boise," ideally as an infographic.
- Proven earlier on a roommate-matching iPhone app launched in Boston, Manhattan, Chicago and others: infographics of the stereotypical roommate in each neighborhood, naming as many local vendors as possible (they share) and gently making fun of the neighborhood (its residents share). He had never been to Boston.
- He thinks this would grow faster than asking other creators to promote it to their Boise subscribers, which he says is a lot of work for them.
