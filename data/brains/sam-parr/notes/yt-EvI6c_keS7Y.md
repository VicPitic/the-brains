## Choosing a newsletter niche with the CPM math

Sam says to do the math before writing a single issue, because most people pick a topic they love and discover it can't pay. The formula for the size of the opportunity is subscribers x sends per month x (CPM / 1,000).

His worked comparison:
- **UFC picks newsletter:** 50,000 subscribers, one send a week (4 a month), $10 CPM = about $2,000/month. "That's [ __ ]."
- **Doctor/heart-surgeon news:** 50,000 subscribers, 6 sends a week (24 a month), $1,000 CPM = about $1.2 million/month. He notes $1,000 is a high CPM; the point is the contrast.
- Two levers drive the gap: how often the topic produces news (news can go out 6 days a week, UFC cannot) and what an advertiser can earn from each reader.

How he values an audience:
- Value = number of people x how engaged/influenced they are x the price of what the advertiser sells.
- Example: 500 people who decide which supplies a top airline buys are worth more than 1,000,000 middle-America mothers earning $40,000. The airline contract is worth millions; 25-year-old guys who like comedy might return $500 on an ad.
- Brand advertisers just want exposure (his Toyota example). Direct-response advertisers want clicks and sales, so if they spend $10,000 with you they need to make at least $10,000 back.

Picking the niche:
- He uses an Ikigai-style overlap: what you're good at, what you love, what people want, and what people will pay for. "You want to find something right in the middle."
- Passion alone fails: "People don't pay for that [ __ ]" when the CPM is low (sports).
- For The Hustle he did the same math at the start: roughly 1 million to 3 million subscribers, about 26 sends a month, $50 per 10,000 sends, which pencilled out to tens of millions a year. He validated the audience size by looking at Wall Street Journal and Business Insider traffic (tens of millions a month) and trusting his gut that a million people wanted a daily business and tech email.
- His own preference today is B2B only, never consumer.

## Why B2B newsletters beat consumer ones

Sam says that if he started a newsletter now, he would only do B2B.

- B2B readers spend more and advertisers pay more per reader.
- The content bar is low. "A lot of B2Boring... it's not that hard to be cool." Tech news wasn't a cool industry, so an occasional silly joke made The Hustle stand out.
- Acquisition is workable: B2B readers can be found and bought on LinkedIn. His guess is a subscriber for about $5 that earns about $5 a month in ad revenue (he hasn't run it recently and says so).
- Example he gives of the upside: a friend sold a boring HR-focused newsletter with about 1 million subscribers for roughly $500 million. The Hustle had 2 to 3 million subscribers and sold for tens of millions. His reason: the head of HR makes costly software-buying decisions.
- He calls LinkedIn "wide open" and a much easier channel than TikTok, YouTube or Instagram for this.

## Learning to write well: copywork and the ADA formula

Sam's first requirement is "you have to be good... people forget this step." His method:

**Copywork**
- Hand-copy great writing word for word for about an hour a day, for roughly 10 months. He kept stacks of yellow notebooks.
- Sources: the "world's greatest sales letter" (a famous Wall Street Journal ad he found by Googling), classic books like Catcher in the Rye, famous newspaper articles, SNL skits (Judd Apatow did this).
- He annotated what he noticed, e.g. a period instead of a comma, then starting the next sentence with "and."
- His written steps: read and absorb; copy by hand 30 minutes a day with no missed days ("skip one day and you'll likely skip the rest"); keep a swipe file of writing you like; spend 3 to 5 minutes copying a favorite writer before any important writing.
- He still does the short warm-up before writing something important, choosing "today I want to write like this person."
- He credits it as the old way writing was taught in America, and says it was addictive because he could send an email and see ticket sales come in.
- Style comes from stealing from about five influences. His: Felix Dennis (How to Get Rich), Anthony Bourdain, David Ogilvy.

**ADA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action**
- He prefers it to the school-taught thesis-and-three-paragraphs structure, which he calls unengaging.
- Example (drink more water): Attention is "Have you ever seen bodybuilders carrying a gallon of water at the gym?" Interest is a claim that water grows muscles. Desire is the before/after, "do this for a year and you'll look like this." Action is specific: buy a gallon jug, fill it at 8am, finish by 9pm.
- He uses it for everything: sales calls, copy, headlines, popups. The same structure applies to short-form video (hook, build, desire, call to action).

## Writing like a friend to build influence

Sam says the thing that sold product was making readers feel they were friends with the writer.

- "The cool thing about a newsletter is you write it as if it's just me writing to you, not me to millions of people."
- Tactics: ask for recommendations, share silly bits of family life. His test of influence: email "I need a good book to read, reply with one." If only 3 of 10,000 reply, readers don't care about you.
- The Hustle's positioning: "your smart, no-nonsense friend who's going to send you free news every day."
- Shaan Puri's Milk Road copied this line for line for crypto ("your friend that understands crypto") and grew to 100,000 readers organically, then used paid ads. Sam's own growth ad was a made-up quote: "My boss thinks I'm smart. I'm not. I really just read The Hustle." He spent about $10 million or more running it on Facebook, and says it spread to many other newsletters. He credits some luck.

## Getting the first 150,000 subscribers without ads

Sam's growth recipe for The Hustle's first year (roughly 100,000 to 150,000 subscribers in 12 months, 250,000 within a year per another point in the talk):

1. **Name the reader precisely.** His: a 26-year-old in New York, San Francisco or LA, working at one of a thousand tech companies, earning about $100,000.
2. **Go where that person already is.** For him that was Reddit, Hacker News and Facebook groups.
3. **Write long-form articles built around a headline (the ADA hook) for those communities.** Example: he paid a guy $2,000 to live on Soylent for 30 days and write it up, posted "I lived on Soylent for 30 days. Here's what happened" to the Soylent subreddit, and got about 50,000 visitors that week.
4. **Publish at volume.** Only about 20 to 30 percent of articles hit, but he churned out so many that it added up. He also used fake bylines (including a female author) and wrote all of it himself at first.
5. **Convert with a custom popup.** It read "Oh [ __ ] not another popup," then explained the blog existed to promote a daily business email, with "if you don't like it, I'll Venmo you a dollar." It brought in about 30,000 signups a month.
6. **Customize the "forgotten text":** the welcome email and unsubscribe page. His welcome email became famous enough that people blogged about it.

Where he'd go today depends on where the audience lives: Twitter for tech and business, YouTube for cars, LinkedIn for B2B. The principle is to build in the audience's existing home and funnel into the newsletter.

## Monetizing: selling ads, then hiring the seller

Sam's ad-sales path for The Hustle:

- **First advertisers:** around 50,000 subscribers he found marketing managers on LinkedIn at companies whose founders had spoken at his events (35 of them). The pitch: "I have 50,000 subscribers, this is worth $1,500 to $2,000, do it for a grand and if it works keep going." Wealthfront bought and kept going.
- **Ceiling solo:** about $30,000 a month in ad revenue, doing the cold emailing himself.
- **After hiring a dedicated full-time ad salesperson:** about $70,000 a month within two months and $100,000 within a few months. "I'm not that good at selling advertising"; an organized specialist did better.
- **Make advertisers whole:** if a campaign underdelivers, run them again in a later edition. Production cost is near zero. His reason: repeat customers are the key, and an advertiser who doesn't profit churns. He later hired account managers to send results (clicks, signups) and call advertisers like HubSpot and Salesforce to iterate, treating it like enterprise sales.
- Same philosophy on his podcast: he doesn't want a sponsor advertising unless they will make a profit.

## What a solo newsletter can earn, and which model

- Sam says that if he started again, by himself plus an assistant, he could do "at least three million a year." Plan: write 3pm to 6pm, spend the rest of the day on growth and business development, reach about 250,000 subscribers (he got there in one year), then land five or six advertisers paying around $50,000 a month each. Today ad networks make finding advertisers easier than when he cold-emailed.
- Friends do $2 to 3 million a year with themselves plus two others.
- Paid subscriptions: harder to start, easier to run. A friend does about $20 million a year in paid subscriptions, mostly one writer, over 10-plus years.
- The Hustle's own revenue: year 1 about $300,000, year 2 $2.2 million, year 3 $5.5 million, year 4 about $12 million, heading into year 5 on track for about $18 million. It had both an ad newsletter and a paid subscription newsletter. At the sale it reached about 2 million readers a day.

## Origin of The Hustle, selling it, and mindset

- **From events to newsletter:** HustleCon's first event (July 1, 2014 era events) netted about $40,000 on $50 to 60,000 revenue; the second over $100,000. He worried a single rained-out day could kill a conference business. A newsletter of speaker stories had pulled in about 10,000 subscribers, and after reading Ted Turner's biography he did the daily-email CPM math. The Hustle launched April 19, 2016.
- **Speakers said yes through enthusiasm:** he told each of 10 prospects the others were interested, promised a car and to promote their company. "Sales is the transference of enthusiasm," and "it feels really nice to be led by a confident person."
- **De-risk, then go big:** he kept $1,000 in the bank as a flight home so every other outcome was upside. He hired people before he could fully afford them to force the business to work. He paid himself about $20,000 a year for the first stretch while keeping $2 million-plus in the business account, which he now thinks was low confidence and says owners often should pay themselves earlier.
- **Early near-disaster:** week two, a stunt where he plagiarized a bestselling romance novel to show a Kindle exploit drew a call from the largest romance publisher; they dropped it after he apologized in a blog post.
- **The sale to HubSpot:** its corporate development contact emailed about a "partnership." Sam replied asking directly whether they wanted to buy, then sent a Google doc of reasons not to buy him to surface problems early. Term sheet October 1, 90-day close, funds wired about early February. Due diligence was brutal for a small company (even receipts and NDAs for a $10 freelance logo). He says rumored prices were lower than the real price (mid tens of millions in his words). Strategic buyers beat private equity: HubSpot's logic was that converting 0.1% of a million-plus readers into $20,000-a-year customers justified buying the audience, which also earns higher multiples than a pure cash-flow buyer.
- Sam's rule of thumb: strategic buyers pay better multiples than private equity buyers who just want EBITDA.
