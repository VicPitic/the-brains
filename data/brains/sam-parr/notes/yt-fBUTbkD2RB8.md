## How Sam defines a community versus an audience

- Sam says "audience" and "community" get used interchangeably, and he thinks that's wrong. A blog or a Twitter following is an audience.
- His test: with a Creator or audience-builder at the center, you have an audience. A community is "a bunch of different people interacting without an audience Creator."
- The quit-posting test: if the creator stops creating content for two or three weeks, do more conversations happen while they're gone than when they were there? If not, it's an audience, not a community.
- He says the Hustle was often called a community but "that's not a community."
- He thinks "community" is an overused, ambiguous word (corporate teams say "we need Community"), so people dismiss it as silly and don't treat it as a serious business category.
- That dismissal is the opportunity. He likes finding ideas people write off that could be worth hundreds of millions or billions. He expects people to take community seriously in about five years, once numbers are released.
- A real community has its own culture and can organize against its owner. Example: the NYT Cooking Facebook group (he puts it at roughly 100,000 members) revolted after a policy change. He calls that "the sign of a good Community" because it's "its own thing" that's "incredibly hard to own and to like dictate."

## Spotting ideas early: do the math on paper

- Sam says he picks unfashionable categories by running simple arithmetic rather than following sentiment.
- Newsletter example (the Hustle, launched 2016): a CEO told him it would never pass $2 million a year in revenue. Sam's math was roughly 4 million subscribers leading to about $60 million in revenue, using known ad rates, with extra newsletters on top.
- Writer-leverage math: about 3.5 million subscribers and roughly 2 million unique daily opens, which he approximates as about 60 million reads a month (assuming about 30 sends; he says they send about 27). That ran on three writers. BuzzFeed had about 60 million monthly uniques with about 150 writers.
- Why a daily newsletter beats a blog: a blog must acquire new readers every day, while a daily send creates a habit and readers return.
- Proof point: he says competitor and friend Morning Brew is at about $80 million in revenue, so the prediction "kind of came true" even though he sold early and wasn't the one who pulled it off.
- His summary: "the math is the math and that doesn't lie." It sounds stupid because nobody is taking it seriously.
- He says the Hustle, theSkimm and Morning Brew each reached a similar conclusion without knowing each other, then success pulled in imitators. He predicts the same cycle for paid communities.

## Hampton: the business, the numbers and the $100M math

- Hampton (joinhampton.com) is a community for CEOs of companies above a revenue threshold. Sam calls it "a business Therapy Group."
- Origin: running the Hustle was lonely. He couldn't show fear to employees (not knowing how to make payroll, dreading firing someone). He had peers for this in San Francisco, but most founders don't.
- Format:
  - Small groups of 7-8 CEOs with similar types and sizes of business, matched from a waitlist.
  - Monthly three-hour sessions with an executive facilitator.
  - A digital community for all members.
  - About 150 dinners this year, plus retreats.
  - He says non-CEO roles (COO, a Chief of marketing-type role) might come later.
- Group size: he says he "just made it up." Seven still works if someone is out, and it feels like a round table of eight.
- Timeline: work started in June (about a year before the talk). It reached about 300 members through DMs and word of mouth, with no mention on the My First Million podcast despite its big audience. It was announced publicly about six months before the talk.
- Why he didn't build in public: he was embarrassed and didn't want to fail. He also wanted it big enough to be hard to copy before announcing.
- Why he waited to announce: he wanted to perfect the product first. "You can't just like throw a ton of people in there at the same time."
- $100M math: about 10,000 paying members gets to $100M. He says they are already in the thousands of members (the transcript is ambiguous on exact current counts). His reasoning is that 10,000 CEOs obviously exist who would sign up, so it's "a very easy math thing."
- The name: he grew up on Hampton Avenue in St. Louis. He names LLCs and projects after streets, and he likes the old-Rolex-ad look of British racing green, his favorite Jaguar color.
- Members run it too: he asks well-known members (including founders of public companies) to serve as local "social chairs." The pitch: "Do you want to be more popular and thought of as a thought leader?" Hampton gives them a budget and a spoon-fed discussion playbook to host quarterly dinners in their city, and these chairs greet and onboard new members.
- He says members aren't there to see him: the product and community are good enough that he's "one of the least successful people in the community," and Hampton champions members, not him.

## How to launch and seed a community

- Start where people already are, at the lowest friction. His examples:
  - Facebook Groups were his starting point.
  - Slack for white-collar or tech people. He says he never thought this would be true.
  - Discord for young guys or Gen Z.
  - WhatsApp for international audiences.
- He says his team's CTO Dharmesh at HubSpot disagrees and prefers owning the platform, though Dharmesh acknowledges that's very hard. Sam's view: getting people to visit a dedicated site or app multiple times a day to comment is "the hardest thing ever," and he doesn't think he or most people could pull it off.
- You need a leader who acts as the "cult leader," embodying the rituals and culture and setting tone the way a CEO sets company culture. This is hard in big companies because nobody wants to risk looking stupid or saying something inappropriate.
- The leader must be in the group constantly. Sam checks roughly three times an hour, and now has a trained community manager but still keeps his "finger in the pulse."
- Seeding example (Trends, the Hustle's paid community, launched around 2018):
  - Trends was about $300 a year, with a weekly newsletter deconstructing businesses.
  - He created the Facebook group two days before launch, as an afterthought.
  - He posted in it every day for about a year.
  - He asked smart friends to post content he wrote for them, daily, to create initial traction.
  - After about six months people started commenting on their own and it developed its own culture.
  - He says it reached roughly 20-25,000 members at one point and now has thousands more; he isn't sure of the current number.
- Start the group as a "dictatorship" and let it evolve into more of a democracy. Hampton has core rules, for example members can't talk about what's discussed in their core group, and anyone who does is removed.
- Needs a "shtick": something that differentiates the community, even a small twist. Examples he gives: developers who are "a little punk rock," or "the most helpful developer community" that replies thoughtfully to everything.

## Scaling a community without killing it

- Don't pour a lot of people in at once. Community is more like an event than software: with software you can add users and fix on the fly, but a community that grows too big too fast "sucks" and has "no soul."
- He cites the HubSpot event itself, which he says reached 28,000 attendees one year and was scaled back because the experience got worse.
- Be meticulous about who gets in, and "indoctrinate" them with the norms: how to behave, how to give, how to take.
- Hampton's structure for scale: chapters, where each person mostly interacts with a cohort of roughly 300, plus ways to interact with the whole network.
- Large communities can still work. Reddit's sub-communities each have their own culture. He says delaying growth is the hardest part, since you want to grow fast to make more money.
- Fear of damage: he worries daily that someone will say something bad, because he thinks a spoiled community is almost impossible to recover. "If you have a dead community, in my opinion just kill it and start over."
- FOMO is, in his words, the second or third most important thing about community. Hampton creates it with "Adventures" such as rally car racing, mountain biking and Lobster diving in Miami two weeks before the talk, with spouses invited so the whole family is bought in.
- Adventures are partly about raising energy: he cites a book saying exciting dates create dopamine and attraction, and applies the same idea to bonding members.

## Monetizing communities: charge more, not ads

- If community is the business itself, charge money. He says paid is "a significantly better route than advertising." He also says to charge more than you think, because the revenue lets you provide a better service.
- If you already own a product (like HubSpot) a free community is fine.
- His Trends mistake: pricing at $300 a year was "really stupid." It meant he needed tens of thousands of members and a small staff to earn what Hampton already earns, and he couldn't afford certain services.
- Pricing rule of thumb: for something job-related, where one conversation could earn someone more money or advance their career, charge much more. That ROI justification also makes people value the community more. For a casual giveaway-style group (the stay-at-home-mom giveaway communities he mentions), something like $50 a month could still be fine.
- Example he cites of a micro-community: a guy who reviews high-end CCTV cameras and sells memberships to large casinos, about 1,200 members at roughly $1,000 a year, around $1.2 million a year, with essentially no competition. His take on the appeal: unlike the newsletter he had to write six days a week, a community doesn't require making content constantly.
- Newsletters and communities pair well: a strong audience builder already knows what people want and how to evoke emotion.

## Where community is going: paid niche professional communities

- Prediction: newsletters had a boom, and communities will follow. He expects many paid communities for niche professionals, possibly going public.
- Example: Doximity, a paid community for doctors, which he says is valued at about $4-5 billion and doing hundreds of millions in revenue (he says he butchers the name and gives these figures from memory).
- Other niches he lists: orthodontists, dentists, lawyers, any profession.
- Why people want them: a safe space to ask things you can't ask your spouse or look silly asking in front of coworkers. He says social media got so big that people no longer feel safe sharing, so micro-communities will boom over the next five to ten years.
- Corporate takeaway: if your boss says "do community," focus on meticulously letting people in and teaching them the culture, not on a big launch or the software.
