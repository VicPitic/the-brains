## Sam's approach to copywriting: write colloquial copy, test phrasing live, and steal from the best

- Sam separates two kinds of copy. Direct-marketing copy (Motley Fool style, 3,000 words that talk you down a slippery slope into a purchase) is one thing. "Colloquial copy" is what he's good at: it's "almost like designing a website on Photoshop and making it look good." He treats it as texture: landing pages "need to feel and read a certain way."
- He says copy matters more than design. Bad design plus good copy can still hit your outcome, good design with weak copy can't, and having both is ideal.
- He wants a brand that's "professional yet informal": cheeky and fun, but elite. His reference points are old Wall Street Journal and New York Times full-page ads, old Porsche ads, and Rolex campaigns, plus Felix Dennis (author of *How to Get Rich*), whose voice he describes as "Richard Branson and Mick Jagger had a baby."
- He builds a brand by mashing together things he's stolen: British racing green, 1970s-80s print-ad sophistication, and Dennis-style voice.
- To find the right phrase, he says phrases out loud on sales or Zoom calls and watches faces "like a comedian." If the eyebrows move, the phrase stays (e.g. "business group therapy"). If it doesn't land, he swaps it and tries again.
- He keeps the vision/pitch language consistent across hundreds of conversations, tweaking a word at a time. He says great leaders and CEOs tell the same story repeatedly, and that this is a form of propaganda: "you have to repeat constantly."

## Making phrases memorable (and why he now keeps "soul" in writing)

- Sam is "obsessed with phrasing." He argues a rule or message only gets followed if it's memorable. Example from Hampton: instead of a long confidentiality sentence, the rule became "nothing, no one, nobody" (don't talk about anything to anyone).
- He notes that catchy phrases can override logic ("I might as well" keep waiting in line even when you don't want the thing). Good-sounding phrasing changes behavior.
- He studied *The Elements of Eloquence* (recommended by HubSpot CTO Dharmesh Shah) for rhetorical devices:
  - Using one word twice with two meanings ("forgive us our trespasses as we forgive them that trespass against us")
  - Antithesis ("not because it's easy but because it's hard"; "those who can, do; those who can't, teach")
  - Merism, pairing words for effect ("in sickness and in health," "hook, line and sinker," "ladies and gentlemen")
- He says he used to believe in cutting everything unnecessary, then realized "your writing needs to have some soul," and phrases like these supply it.
- He admires writers who use simple language for depth. Hemingway and Salinger write at about a fifth-grade reading level, and "he was sad" can carry a lot. He often checks writing with the Hemingway app.
- Dharmesh's experiment (Sam's account): he built a Zoom plugin to count laughs while he delivered a talk to groups of about 10 HubSpot employees, aiming for a laugh roughly every 90 seconds, and edited the talk to hit it. This year his goal was more memorable phrases.

## Headlines, opening lines, and "slippery slope" structure

- A 1% click-through rate can become 10% with better headlines, so he says to spend real time on the headline. Early Hustle writers wrote great articles with weak headlines, and he compared it to a tree falling in a forest with no one to hear it.
- He thinks in headlines even while developing an idea: "what would the YouTube title be?" It helps him frame the hook.
- The first line matters just as much. On Facebook, people's eyes go headline, then description, then picture.
- He rejects "it's too long" as a diagnosis: the real problem is "your slope wasn't slippery enough." Make the opening slippery so people fall down the whole piece.
- Length rule he and Perell endorse: write under 500 words or over 2,000, since the middle is "no man's land." At The Hustle, Sunday stories were either long (2,000+) or short, and people wanted one or the other.
- He doesn't buy that attention spans are shrinking, only that people want payoffs faster (a laugh or insight every 60-90 seconds). He mostly doesn't believe in changing trends: "human nature is almost always the same."
- On a homepage: use a clear promise with a number plus a sub-headline that explains it, and make it falsifiable, concrete and visual. His advice on the Write of Passage page was to turn a strong testimonial into a bold claim and lead with it.

## Learning to write: copy work, the bottom fourth of a résumé, and editing

- Sam learned to write through copy work: copying great sales letters by hand, with pen and paper rather than typing, for an hour a day for months. He compares it to learning an instrument by copying songs, then combining what you steal into your own voice.
- He still does it before writing. He'll copy 10 minutes of a book he likes (e.g. Anthony Bourdain, for boldness) to "get in the mode."
- He says the real writing comes in editing: "nine out of 10 times you write something and the first five paragraphs, just delete it." The first part is a warm-up and the workout is the edit.
- His motto is "Bold, Fast, Fun" (tattooed on his arm). Fast means speed and momentum, and putting stakes on a project by publishing or committing publicly (a shirtless "before" photo, money on the line, or a private blog that exists "on the internet" even if unshared, which he says gets around 100 search visitors a day).
- He captures ideas the moment they occur, using voice transcription on his phone while out walking, and never lets the epiphany go cold.
- He reads about a book a week, with 10:30pm to midnight for entertainment (history, Gilded Age 1880-1920, assassinations, thrillers) and a daytime half-hour to hour for "enrichment" (currently Berkshire Hathaway acquisitions). He uses Goodreads, where reviews are harsher so a 4.2 is world class, and he highlights on his Kindle and writes short notes afterward.
- On depth of research, he praises Nick Bilton's *American Kingpin*, where the author researched the wind, weather and Facebook photos to recreate a Glen Park scene, and he likes that his own neighborhood was described accurately. He also notes narrative nonfiction often smooths the facts into a cohesive story.

## The Hustle's growth playbook: email-first, then viral posts in the right subreddits

- Sam started The Hustle in 2016 (he was experimenting with email from 2014). His thesis was that retention on a website is hard, since BuzzFeed-style sites must win every visitor every day, while email puts you in front of the audience for free. His back-of-envelope math: 1 million subscribers emailed daily at a 50% open rate is about 15 million opens a day... which he extrapolated to roughly $2 million a month. He says that arithmetic "changed my life."
- Early skeptics, including a digital media CEO, said it wouldn't make money. His answer: it doesn't matter whether someone is in Chrome or an email app, if you have their attention the ad works.
- The core metric was opens, not pageviews. Subscriber path: roughly 150,000 in year one, 500,000 in year two, 1 million in year three, 1.7 million at sale in year four, and nearly 4 million now under new ownership.
- Growth engine for the first 150,000: blog posts built to go viral in the specific subreddits where the target audience already lived. His strategy: "go to where the people who you want already are and siphon them off."
- Example 1: he exposed a self-publisher making about $60K a month by remixing sex-advice books with sexy titles and fake reviews. Then he published a plagiarized werewolf-Navy-SEAL romance novel (picked because romance had the most liquidity: most new books and most buyers) with fake reviews and reached #1 in its Kindle category, to show Amazon was negligent. A publisher threatened to sue, he explained the point, and they told him to take it down. Posted to the self-publishing subreddit, it went viral across other communities.
- Example 2: someone lived on Soylent ("Slim Fast for nerds") for 30 days, and it was posted in the Soylent subreddit.
- He did stunts like these roughly monthly, and the loop was blog post to Reddit to a pop-up to a newsletter signup.

## Conversion copy: AIDA, the pop-up and the welcome email

- Framework: AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action). Example he gave: open with the guys at the gym carrying gallon jugs of water, claim water builds muscle faster, explain what that means for you, then tell them to buy the water.
- Hustle pop-up copy was written the way people talk in a bar: roughly "While you're here, The Hustle is a daily newsletter. We write these crazy blog posts every now and then. If you don't like it, we'll Venmo you a dollar." Tone matched the audience of a young male skew (around 60-70% young men) at the time.
- He calls the thank-you page and welcome email "the forgotten text," which 9 out of 10 companies leave generic ("Thanks for subscribing, click to confirm"). His welcome email instead said, in effect: when you entered your email a bell went off in our office, our head of operations ran outside and hugged a stranger, an employee started doing 15 push-ups to burn off energy... Then it thanked the reader and said the first email arrives tomorrow. This worked because the brand was a bit bro-y and could be obnoxious.

## Research tactic: use the Wayback Machine to see how winners started

- Don't copy a company's copy as it is today. Go to the Internet Archive and look at what they said in the early days, because "it's not exactly fair to compare yourself to the final version."
- His method: find a news article announcing a funding round or milestone (WSJ, TechCrunch), then look at the site about three months before it. That shows the positioning that was working before they had resources and breadth.
- It also shows culture and tone the company had when it had to stand out, rather than the polished version (his Shopify example).
- He cites a mistake at The Hustle and Morning Brew: the original homepage was just a plain email box and it worked. Later, committees (people who hadn't looked at the early numbers) added complexity by copying open-menu media homepages like GQ.com.
- Counter-position against incumbents: the Ben Thompson / Barstool pattern of looking at how the establishment (ESPN, New York media) talks and doing the opposite.

## Hiring writers and building the team

- Sam says he hired roughly 50 to 100 writers over the years, and of the 30 to 40 staff at the sale, about 30 became prominent in the newsletter space or run their own agency or newsletter. He says his approach was "buy her stock early before the market finds out."
- Many weren't writers when hired: Trung worked at an analytics company, Steph was a product manager (he found her through a personal blog post with the headline "To be great, just be good consistently"), and Zach Crockett was a blogger.
- Screening: look for people writing for fun, and read what he calls "the bottom fourth of a résumé" (what they studied, sports, hobbies). He asks for a story about it. His line: if you spent $200,000 on college and can't entertain him about a favorite class, he doesn't want to be around you.
- Onboarding: have new writers rewrite The Hustle's daily email by hand for a few weeks to absorb the voice, then let them go.
- Zach's pattern: every Sunday a long piece, "no deep cuts, hits only," with many hitting 2 to 5 million views.

## Brand positioning: know what you stand against (Hampton)

- Sam says his mistake at earlier companies was being good at writing what the company stood for and bad at writing what it was against.
- At Hampton, the brand is deliberately masculine in the sense of attracting people aggressive about business growth and emotional growth, with no preference for men over women. He accepts that some will call it too masculine or exclusive and says "it's not for everyone."
- At The Hustle, he hired people who called themselves artists and found they were too slow, a lesson in defining who and what you're against.
- He prefers narrative written memos to presentations because writing forces clear thinking ("you don't have to think clearly to be a good speaker but you have to in order to be a great writer"), and he cites Amazon's six-pager as the model.
