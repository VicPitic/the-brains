## Why Sam started the hot dog stand and what it taught him about selling

- Sam grew up in St. Louis, Missouri. His parents owned a fruit stand, which is where he learned entrepreneurship, though "back then they didn't call it entrepreneurship. It was just like making a living."
- In college he ran a hot dog stand called "Southern Sam's, wieners as big as a baby's arm". The work was hard and hot, and it pushed him toward online selling (eBay, then a couple of e-commerce stores) because he could make money from an air-conditioned room "in my sleep".
- Hot dogs are undifferentiated, so he read books on selling. His stunts included a free hot dog if you put your baby's arm in a bun with mustard and posted a photo on social media (matching the slogan). He also riffed with customers to get them laughing, which he compares to flirting.
- The insight that led him to copywriting: written copy can be written once and sold to an infinite audience, not just the people walking past his block.
- He left school and tried to join the early-stage Airbnb (then "airbed and breakfast"), which began his internet career.

## Differentiation beats being better

- Sam's view: "It's a lot better and easier to be different than it is better."
- He corrected the idea of "sell what everybody is selling but say it better" to "say it different".
- His reasoning: business is mostly perception. He asks whether one car is really better than another, yet people perceive a Mercedes as higher quality.
- He says he learned the effects of differentiation from selling hot dogs.

## Hosting events and book clubs as a way to become the node of a network

- Sam started events to meet friends. He read a book arguing that to attract friends and partners you should live an interesting life and bring others along with you.
- He ran a business book club, advertising it on Craigslist and in the San Francisco newspaper. About 2,000 people signed up to hear about it over a year, and he met his now best friends (including those at his wedding) and his wife's introduction through it.
- Running it took little effort: read one chapter ahead and lead the conversation. In return he got status and friends.
- He launched HustleCon, "basically like a TED talk for entrepreneurs", after selling a failed-ish roommate-matching app (a "Tinder for roommates", which he says was a much smaller market than Tinder itself). He ran the event to kill time until his next idea, knowing events make you "the node of a network".
- He says he is actually introverted and likes being alone now; the events were a means to get friends.

## Why he started The Hustle as a newsletter and how it grew

- Three things converged: he liked writing, he saw newsletter founders on Andrew Warner's Mixergy (including Ben Lear of Thrillist), and media companies built on Facebook were losing reach as the algorithm changed.
- His thesis: "newsletters are like a pirate ship and every newsletter subscriber you get, it's like a little bit of a wind in your sails." Two to five writers sending ~1,500 words a day could reach millions of people.
- His math: with five or six million subscribers he thought a newsletter could make $100 million a year. Media execs he cold emailed called the idea stupid; he asked why it matters whether people read in the Gmail app or a browser, since "your attention's there or it's not".
- Growth: 100,000 subscribers in year one, then roughly 500,000, 1 million, and about 1.7 million by the sale.
- Revenue went roughly $300,000, $2.2M, $5M, $12M, then about $18M in the year he sold.
- Luck: there were only three companies doing it (The Hustle, Morning Brew under Austin Rief, and theSkimm), and advertisers started deliberately budgeting for newsletters, which got easier every year. All three founders reached the same insight at about the same time.
- He sold The Hustle to HubSpot at 31. His goal was $20M by 30 (based on a friend spending about $60,000 a month, which he calculated needed roughly $20-25M), and he missed by a year. He paid himself about $2,000 a month for the first years.

## Newsletter growth tactics: free first 100K, then paid ads

- The Hustle reached 100,000 subscribers without spending "one cent in marketing", via blog posts that went viral on Reddit and Hacker News.
- Method: decide where your audience already lives, create content that gets popular there, and siphon readers into the newsletter.
- Example: because the audience was nerdy engineers, he paid a friend $2,000 to live on Soylent only for 30 days and wrote it up, aimed at the Soylent subreddit.
- Paid acquisition, later: first get to tens of thousands of subscribers organically to prove the content keeps people. Then calculate what a subscriber is worth, decide the payback window, and buy ads anywhere you can acquire a subscriber at that value. He says he and Austin Rief at Morning Brew took this seriously (Facebook ads) when almost nobody else did.
- He says newsletters are far more formulaic to grow than podcasts. For a new newsletter from scratch he estimates six months and about a quarter of a million dollars to reach 100,000 subscribers.

## Newsletter monetization and ad pricing

- Roughly the first $15M of revenue came from ads alone. He thought ads could reach $40-50M but later said that was wrong and the number is higher, possibly $100M from advertising alone.
- The year he sold, he expected about $18M: roughly $12-13M from advertising and about $6M from subscriptions.
- He launched paid products at hundreds of thousands to a million subscribers, including Trends, which he priced at $300 a year and says was a pricing mistake ("I should have charged way more money").
- First ad: sold in June 2016, about two months after launch, for $30,000 that month. He offered the first advertiser (Chris Martinez at Wealthfront) "$20 per 1,000" with a money-back guarantee, and the $20 CPM stuck. Native ads (about 150 words) went for roughly $25-30 CPM, with cheaper units beyond that.
- At around a million subscribers he estimates each send was worth tens of thousands of dollars (he recalled about $45,000 and said he'd have to redo the math).
- Podcast lesson: when HubSpot bought My First Million it was about 300-370K downloads a month and earning about $10,000 a month in sales. Sam says his team specialized in newsletter ads and "did not know how to" sell podcast ads. The show now makes many millions a year.

## The order of media influence: podcast, YouTube, newsletter, blog

- Sam's ranking of channels by influence: podcast first (you're in someone's ear for hours a week), YouTube second, newsletter third, blog fourth.
- His formula for media value: number of people x how influential you are with them x their buying power. Low influence over 10 million Midwest moms can equal a newsletter reaching 1,000 purchasing managers at Boeing.
- Podcasts are the hardest thing to grow ("like the hardest thing on earth"), but once you hold someone's ear 50 to 100 minutes a week you have real influence over them.
- He says he's a great writer, not a great podcaster; his co-host Sean is built for podcasting. Starting a new podcast from scratch under a different name in a new industry would be very hard; he calls podcasting "a little bit magic in a bottle".

## Playbook: a niche B2B newsletter to $100M (construction/HVAC example)

- His claim: almost any business can reach $100M in revenue given enough time and the right model. B2B newsletters are more profitable than anything else he has done.
- Model to copy: Industry Dive, a B2B newsletter business sold to Informa for roughly $800M by his recollection.
- Steps for construction:
  - Daily newsletter, six days a week, aimed at decision makers who buy tens of millions of dollars of products.
  - Editorial philosophy: make a big industry feel small, like a high school yearbook. Name as many names and companies as possible, with quotes from people (e.g. a 500-person company owner in New Jersey), so readers see themselves and their peers and tell friends.
  - Target about $5M in revenue, then launch adjacent newsletters (plumbing, HVAC).
  - Layer on a paid community and a conference.
- Why ad pricing can be far higher than broad newsletters: vendors need to reach a hard-to-reach group. If an HVAC company buys about $30M a year of units, a vendor will pay a lot to reach that buyer. He gives the example of a friend's $100M HVAC business that he guesses buys $30M a year of air conditioning units. Pick industries where many vendors want to reach the buyers and Facebook ads don't work.
- Choosing a niche: "Which industries can I enter where there's enough people buying ads but it's really hard" to reach them any other way.

## Quick takes on other business ideas

- Car wash franchise: decide who it's for and sweat every detail of the experience. His example is a family-friendly wash painted in bright colors, with toys and coloring books so kids can play while the car is cleaned. Expand through franchising and fund new cities by selling founding or annual memberships.
- Blue-collar roll-ups: he says it's a cool idea but "so much work". He'd rather build a newsletter to $100M, and jokes that he's romanticizing blue-collar work the way someone romanticizes hunting but wouldn't sleep outside in the cold.
- AI personal trainer app: the hardest of the group. Distribution is the hardest part, so he'd need a co-founder who is a fitness influencer with a large Instagram or YouTube audience (offering half the company), while he builds the product.

## Why Hampton is built to last, and the community gap it fills

- The Hustle was built to sell. Hampton is meant to last "50 to 100 years" because "if you do it right, [growth] should compound", people at the top do the same thing for decades, he wants his two-year-old daughter to be able to work there, and he enjoys it.
- He thinks the wealthiest and most impactful people stay on one thing for many decades, and that huge growth compounds if you keep at it.
- The gap: YPO (about 30,000 members, more old-school and inherited family or real estate business) and Vistage (an estimated $800M a year in revenue, mostly blue-collar owners like plumbing or HVAC) exist, but he found internet-space communities borderline scammy or "broy". Nothing served young-thinking internet entrepreneurs who are past survival and thinking about thriving.
- The problem Hampton solves: after years with your head down you look up and have outrun your peers, so you make $5M, $10M, $20M, $50M decisions alone. "That's when mediocrity tends to creep in" because staff won't be honest, and a spouse knows your personal baggage but not your business.
- Standards: Hampton turns away more applicants than it admits. Rule from early on: "we will never trade off cool". People should join for utility (making more money, building a better business, feeling better), not because it's cool.
- Vetting: he and his co-founder have watched 100% of the recorded interviews of tens of thousands of applicants, and the team debates each one weekly, researching businesses to check they're legit. He thinks Hampton should tell that story better.
- Brand lesson: he started by trying to build Hampton without relying on his reputation, then realized that was "the stupidest thing ever". Leaning into his personality as a kind of "cult leader" increased engagement.

## Habits and advice: master few books and set goals in four categories

- Read less, master more. Sam says he used to read one book over and over to master the techniques, which is a better way to consume information. He suggests sticking to three or four classics (such as Robert Cialdini's Influence) and reading nothing else.
- On breadth: "there's an overemphasis on breadth of knowledge and not depth", and it's far better to be an expert at a narrow thing than a generalist at many. Test: you could teach it back to someone.
- Goals: set them each year in four categories: family, fitness, finance and fun (add faith if religious). He reviews them with his wife once a month to check they're on track, with specific plans such as scheduling a tour of Gilded Age architecture or a trip he's always wanted.
- Good goals are time-bound, achievable and super specific. His analogy: a trainer can't help you with "I want to be fit"; they need to know if you want muscle, front splits or a fast 5K.
- Common failure: too many goals and not enough reminders. "Distractions are loud," goals let you "put blinders on", and focus and patience are underrated traits.
- Picking a spouse: he calls choosing a good life partner the best financial decision he made, because his wife kept him on track and helped him up "when I wanted to quit, which was often".

## Sobriety and facing fear in his 20s

- Around 20-21 Sam developed an addictive personality problem with drugs and alcohol and used alcohol to cover insecurity, especially around women. He was arrested two or three times in about three weeks and spent time in jail. Airbnb rescinded the job the day before he started, over how he answered a question about convictions.
- It took about 12 months sober before he felt confident in his own skin.
- His view: alcohol is like beer goggles that blur reality, and sobriety forces you to face fears and consequences. Small acts like talking to a stranger sober build the muscle of confidence, and after a couple of years it "became like a high".
- Advice for young men: don't avoid fearful things, acknowledge the fear and do them anyway. Drugs and alcohol give fake courage and create a bad cycle.
- He notes that young men lack a clear rite of passage, so at 21-22 he felt like a boy who was supposed to act like a man.
